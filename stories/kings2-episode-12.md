2 Kings Episode 12: Hezekiah and the Assyrian
2 Kings 18-19, Berean Standard Bible

After Ahaz comes the book's highest verdict on any king of Judah: Hezekiah “did what was right in the eyes of the LORD, just as his father David had done.” Not the sliding scale of his ancestors, the David grade itself. He removed the high places his righteous predecessors had all left standing, shattered the sacred pillars, cut down the Asherah poles, and demolished even the bronze snake Moses had made, because Israel had begun burning incense to it: “He also demolished the bronze snake called Nehushtan that Moses had made, for up to that time the Israelites had burned incense to it.” The book's summary is unmatched on either throne: “Hezekiah trusted in the LORD, the God of Israel. No king of Judah was like him, either before him or after him.” “And the LORD was with Hezekiah, and he prospered wherever he went. He rebelled against the king of Assyria and refused to serve him.” The rebellion is where the episode's trouble starts. Samaria fell in his sixth year, on schedule, and in his fourteenth year Sennacherib of Assyria took every fortified city of Judah. Hezekiah first tried his father's tool: “I have done wrong; withdraw from me, and I will pay whatever you demand from me.” The price was three hundred talents of silver and thirty of gold, and to pay it Hezekiah emptied the temple treasury and stripped the gold from the temple doors he himself had plated. The tribute changed nothing. Assyria sent its three great officers with a great army anyway, and they took their stand by the aqueduct of the upper pool, where the city's officials came out to negotiate and the whole population could listen from the wall.

The speech the Rabshakeh delivered there is the finest piece of enemy rhetoric in the Bible, and the book gives it in full. He opened by stripping Hezekiah's strategy to a slogan: “You claim to have a strategy and strength for war, but these are empty words. In whom are you now trusting, that you have rebelled against me?” Egypt was a splintered reed “that will pierce the hand of anyone who leans on it”. And he had a theological argument built for the wall's defenders: “is He not the One whose high places and altars Hezekiah has removed”, this God they claimed to trust? He mocked the army with a wager, two thousand horses if Judah could find riders for them, and then played his strongest card, that Assyria's invasion was itself commissioned: “The LORD Himself said to me”, and the inner word he claimed was, “Go up against this land and destroy it.” The officials begged him to switch to Aramaic, the language of diplomacy, out of the people's hearing. He refused and answered in Hebrew, louder, addressing the men on the wall over the officials' heads, and describing what siege does to a city in terms too coarse to soften. Then came the offer, pitched like a travel brochure over a starving wall: “Do not let Hezekiah deceive you; he cannot deliver you from my hand.” “Make peace with me and come out to me. Then every one of you will eat from his own vine and his own fig tree and drink water from his own cistern”, until deportation to “a land of grain and new wine, a land of bread and vineyards, a land of olive trees and honey”. And the close was the catalogue of the fallen: “Has the god of any nation ever delivered his land from the hand of the king of Assyria?” “Who among all the gods of these lands has delivered his land from my hand? How then can the LORD deliver Jerusalem from my hand?” Against all of it, the city's answer was discipline: “the people remained silent and did not answer a word”, because the king had commanded, “Do not answer him.”

Hezekiah tore his clothes, put on sackcloth, and went into the house of the LORD, and sent his officials, also in sackcloth, to Isaiah son of Amoz. Isaiah appears in this series here, in place, as the book places him; his own book comes at its slot in the prophets' run, and his account of these same days will stand beside this one then, whole and unstitched. Hezekiah's message to him was a birth metaphor for a nation out of strength: “Today is a day of distress, rebuke, and disgrace; for children have come to the point of birth, but there is no strength to deliver them.” “Therefore lift up a prayer for the remnant that still survives.” Isaiah's first answer was short and total: “Do not be afraid of the words you have heard, with which the servants of the king of Assyria have blasphemed Me.” “I will put a spirit in him so that he will hear a rumor and return to his own land, where I will cause him to fall by the sword.” The rumor arrived, Tirhakah of Cush marching out, and Sennacherib withdrew to meet it, but first sent a letter to finish what the Rabshakeh had started: “Do not let your God, in whom you trust, deceive you by saying that Jerusalem will not be delivered into the hand of the king of Assyria.” Hezekiah took the letter up to the house of the LORD and spread it out before Him, an exhibit laid on the courtroom floor, and prayed the prayer the episode is remembered by: “O LORD, God of Israel, enthroned between the cherubim, You alone are God over all the kingdoms of the earth. You made the heavens and the earth.” “Incline Your ear, O LORD, and hear; open Your eyes, O LORD, and see. Listen to the words that Sennacherib has sent to defy the living God.” He conceded the Assyrian record in full, the nations wasted, their gods burned, “for they were not gods, but only wood and stone”, and drew the line at exactly that point: “And now, O LORD our God, please save us from his hand, so that all the kingdoms of the earth may know that You alone, O LORD, are God.”

The answer came through Isaiah with a verdict attached: “I have heard your prayer concerning Sennacherib king of Assyria.” The oracle that followed addressed the Assyrian's boasting as a charge sheet: “Whom have you taunted and blasphemed? Against whom have you raised your voice and lifted your eyes in pride? Against the Holy One of Israel!” The sentence was a herdsman's image for the king of the world: “Because your rage and arrogance against Me have reached My ears, I will put My hook in your nose and My bit in your mouth; I will send you back the way you came.” And the military promise was absolute: “He will not enter this city or shoot an arrow into it. He will not come before it with a shield or build up a siege ramp against it.” “I will defend this city and save it for My own sake and for the sake of My servant David.” That night the promise kept itself: “that very night the angel of the LORD went out and struck down 185,000 men in the camp of the Assyrians.” Sennacherib broke camp, went home to Nineveh, and stayed there, and the book ends his story in the temple of his god Nisroch, where his own sons Adrammelech and Sharezer put him to the sword as he worshiped. The god whose name had headed the Rabshakeh's catalogue of the unconquered could not keep his own king alive in his own house. Jerusalem, which had not fired an arrow, was still standing.
