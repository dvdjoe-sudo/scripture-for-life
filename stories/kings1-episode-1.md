1 Kings Episode 1: The Old King and the Contested Throne
1 Kings 1-2, Berean Standard Bible

The book opens on a king who cannot get warm. David was old, and though they covered him with blankets, he could not keep warm, so his servants searched Israel for a beautiful young woman to attend him and lie beside him and give him heat. They found Abishag the Shunammite, unsurpassed in beauty, and she cared for the king and served him, and the text says plainly that he had no relations with her. It is a strange, quiet picture to set at the head of a book of kings: the giant-killer, the psalmist, the man after God's own heart, shivering in bed while the court whispers around him. And into that quiet, before David had named any successor in public, his oldest living son made his move.

Adonijah was David's son by Haggith, born next after Absalom, and he began to exalt himself with a sentence that explains the whole chapter: “I will be king!” He acquired chariots and horsemen and fifty men to run ahead of him, the exact stagecraft his brother Absalom had used before his revolt, and the text adds a parenthesis that lands like a verdict on David's whole career as a father: he had never once reprimanded Adonijah by saying, “Why do you act this way?” The court split along lines anyone could read. Joab the army commander and Abiathar the priest backed Adonijah. Zadok the priest, Benaiah son of Jehoiada, Nathan the prophet, and David's mighty men would not join him. Adonijah held his coronation feast in advance, sacrificing sheep and oxen and fattened calves by the stone of Zoheleth near En-rogel, and he invited every royal brother and every man of Judah in the king's service. The guest list was the message. He did not invite Nathan, Benaiah, the mighty men, or Solomon.

Nathan moved first, and he moved through Bathsheba. He told her the news she had not heard, that Adonijah had become king and David did not know it, and he gave her the approach word for word: remind the king of his oath that Solomon would reign after him, and ask why Adonijah was king instead. Then, while she was still speaking, Nathan would come in behind her and confirm her words. Bathsheba went into the bedroom, where Abishag was serving the old king, and bowed in homage. She laid out the oath, the feast, the invitations, and the danger, in one sentence that turned a family claim into a death sentence if it failed: “Otherwise, when my lord the king rests with his fathers, I and my son Solomon will be counted as criminals.” Nathan entered on cue and put the same facts as a question about the king's own honor: had David let this happen without telling his servants who should sit on the throne? David answered by calling Bathsheba back in, and the dying man swore the oath again, out loud, before witnesses: “As surely as the LORD lives, who has redeemed my life from all distress, I will carry out this very day exactly what I swore to you by the LORD, the God of Israel: Surely your son Solomon will reign after me, and he will sit on my throne in my place.”

What followed was a coronation staged in an afternoon, and every detail was a counter-claim to Adonijah's feast. Solomon was set on David's own mule, not his own. He was taken down to Gihon, a spring inside David's own city, not out at En-rogel. Zadok took the horn of oil from the tabernacle and anointed him, the ram's horn blew, and all the people proclaimed, “Long live King Solomon!” Then they followed him up into the city playing flutes and rejoicing, the text says, with such great joy that the earth was split by the sound. Down at En-rogel the feast heard it before it understood it. Joab asked why the city was in such an uproar, and Jonathan son of Abiathar came in with the news item by item: David had made Solomon king, Solomon sat on the royal throne, and the old king on his bed had bowed in worship and blessed God for letting his eyes see a son on his throne. Adonijah's guests rose in terror and scattered, every man to his own road. Adonijah himself ran the other way, to the altar, and took hold of its horns. Solomon's answer to the report was a sentence with a door and a drop in it: “If he is a man of character, not a single hair of his will fall to the ground. But if evil is found in him, he will die.” Adonijah came down from the altar, bowed before the brother he had tried to displace, and was sent home.

Then the old king died, but not before he charged his son, and the charge has two halves that sit uneasily together, as so much in David's life sat together. The first half is the covenant: “I am about to go the way of all the earth. So be strong and prove yourself a man.” Walk in God's ways, keep the Law of Moses, and the promise would hold, a son of David always on the throne of Israel. Show kindness to the sons of Barzillai, who stood by David in his flight from Absalom, and let them eat at the royal table. The second half is unfinished business, and David named names. Joab had murdered two commanders, Abner and Amasa, in peacetime, and David, who had publicly cursed that deed and publicly done nothing about it for decades, told Solomon to act according to his wisdom and not let Joab's gray head go down to Sheol in peace. Shimei had cursed David on the road to Mahanaim, and David had later sworn not to kill him; the oath was David's, he pointed out, and Solomon was a wise man who would know what to do to bring that gray head down in blood. David rested with his fathers and was buried in the City of David, forty years a king, seven in Hebron and thirty-three in Jerusalem, and the text says of the son who replaced him: “So Solomon sat on the throne of his father David, and his kingdom was firmly established.”

The establishment had a price, and it was paid in the same order David had listed it. Adonijah came first, and he came politely, through Bathsheba, with one small request. He conceded the throne in words that cost him nothing: “All Israel expected that I should reign, but the kingship has turned to my brother, for it has come to him from the LORD.” Then the request: give me Abishag the Shunammite as my wife. Bathsheba carried it in good faith, and Solomon rose to greet her and had a throne set for her at his right hand, an honor no queen mother in Israel had been shown before. Then she asked, and the king heard the request for what it was. In that world a dead king's attendant belonged to the trappings of the crown itself, and asking for Abishag was asking for a claim. “you might as well request the kingdom for him”, Solomon told his mother, and he swore the oath and sent Benaiah, and Adonijah died that day. Abiathar came next. The priest who had carried the ark before David and shared all David's sufferings was sent home to his fields at Anathoth with his life and without his office: “Even though you deserve to die, I will not put you to death at this time”. The text marks the moment with a long memory: so Solomon banished Abiathar from the priesthood, fulfilling the word spoken generations earlier at Shiloh against the house of Eli. Joab heard the news and ran for the same horns that had not saved Adonijah for long, and this time the altar gave no shelter at all. Benaiah brought the order to come out, and Joab refused it in five words: “No, I will die here.” So he died there, at the altar he had fled to, for Abner and Amasa, and Benaiah took his place over the army, and Zadok took Abiathar's place at the altar. Shimei lasted three years under house arrest in Jerusalem, bounded by the Kidron Valley and a sworn sentence of death if he crossed it. Then two of his slaves ran away to Gath, and Shimei saddled his donkey and went after them and came back, and the king who had promised nothing to his father's curser kept the oath Shimei had sworn instead. Benaiah went out a third time. The chapter ends where it began, with the same sentence now heavier: “Thus the kingdom was firmly established in the hand of Solomon.”
