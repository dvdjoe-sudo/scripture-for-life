2 Kings Episode 13: Hezekiah's Last Years, and Manasseh
2 Kings 20-21, Berean Standard Bible

The deliverance of Jerusalem was followed by a death sentence delivered to one man. Hezekiah fell mortally ill, and Isaiah came with a word as blunt as the Rabshakeh's: “Put your house in order, for you are about to die; you will not recover.” The king turned his face to the wall and prayed, and the prayer is a righteous man's whole defense, offered in tears: “Please, O LORD, remember how I have walked before You faithfully and with wholehearted devotion; I have done what is good in Your sight.” Before Isaiah had crossed the middle courtyard on his way out, the word came back reversed: “I have heard your prayer; I have seen your tears. I will surely heal you.” “I will add fifteen years to your life”, and the city would be defended with it. A poultice of figs was laid on the boil, and Hezekiah recovered, and for a sign he was offered the shadow on the stairway of Ahaz moving either way. He chose the harder direction, ruling that lengthening was easy: “It is easy for the shadow to lengthen ten steps”, “but not for it to go back ten steps.” The shadow went back. The man who had spread a letter before the LORD and watched an empire's army die in the night now had fifteen added years, a sign read on a staircase, and a future he had not asked about.

The future arrived as a diplomatic courtesy. Babylon, then a junior power courting allies against Assyria, sent letters and a gift to the recovering king, and Hezekiah received the envoys and showed them everything: the silver, the gold, the spices, the precious oil, the armory, all the storehouses. The book measures the thoroughness with a comprehensive negative: “There was nothing in his palace or in all his dominion that Hezekiah did not show them.” Isaiah came afterward with an audit in three questions. Where did the men come from? “They came from a distant land”, “from Babylon.” What have they seen? “They have seen everything in my palace,” answered Hezekiah. “There is nothing among my treasures that I did not show them.” Then the prophet delivered the word that reaches past Hezekiah's fifteen years to the end of the book itself: “The time will surely come when everything in your palace and all that your fathers have stored up until this day will be carried off to Babylon. Nothing will be left, says the LORD.” “And some of your descendants, your own flesh and blood, will be taken away to be eunuchs in the palace of the king of Babylon.” Hezekiah's response is the most quietly damning line ever given to a good king: “The word of the LORD that you have spoken is good.” The text opens his reasoning to the reader: “Will there not at least be peace and security in my lifetime?” The man who had prayed for the remnant and spread the letter for the city accepted, as good news, a catastrophe scheduled safely beyond his own death. He died honored, remembered for his might and for the pool and tunnel he built to bring water into the city, works of the siege years. His son Manasseh reigned in his place. Manasseh was twelve years old, and some of what Babylon's envoys had admired was still in the storehouses.

Manasseh reigned fifty-five years, the longest reign in the book, and the book treats its length as an indictment rather than a blessing, because he spent it systematically reversing his father. “he rebuilt the high places that his father Hezekiah had destroyed, and he raised up altars for Baal.” “He made an Asherah pole, as King Ahab of Israel had done, and he worshiped and served all the host of heaven.” He built altars to the host of heaven in both courtyards of the house of the LORD itself, the house of which God had said, “In Jerusalem I will put My Name”, and set the carved Asherah pole up inside the temple. “He sacrificed his own son in the fire, practiced sorcery and divination, and consulted mediums and spiritists. He did great evil in the sight of the LORD, provoking Him to anger.” Hezekiah had wept and been heard; his son filled the fifty-five years with everything his father's reform had torn down, replanted in the temple courts. And the people followed where he led: “the people did not listen and Manasseh led them astray, so that they did greater evil than the nations that the LORD had destroyed before the Israelites.” The comparison is the verdict's floor: worse than the nations dispossessed for exactly these practices.

So the prophets brought the sentence, and it reaches back to the last episode's verdict and applies it to the south: “Since Manasseh king of Judah has committed all these abominations, acting more wickedly than the Amorites who preceded him”, “Behold, I am bringing such calamity upon Jerusalem and Judah that the news will reverberate in the ears of all who hear it.” The instruments named for the measuring are borrowed from the north's autopsy: “I will stretch out over Jerusalem the measuring line used against Samaria and the plumb line used against the house of Ahab”, “and I will wipe out Jerusalem as one wipes out a bowl”, “wiping it and turning it upside down.” “So I will forsake the remnant of My inheritance and deliver them into the hands of their enemies.” The book adds its final count against him in a single sentence of scale: “Manasseh shed so much innocent blood that he filled Jerusalem from end to end”. Kings records no repentance for him, not in fifty-five years; Chronicles, when this series reaches it, tells something more of Manasseh, and it will be told there, in its place, whole and unstitched. Manasseh died and was buried in his palace garden. His son Amon copied him for two years, “He abandoned the LORD, the God of his fathers, and did not walk in the way of the LORD”, and was murdered by his own servants. The people of the land executed the conspirators and made Amon's son king in his place. The boy's name was Josiah, and he was eight years old, and everything now depends on what a child raised in Manasseh's palace will do with a throne.
