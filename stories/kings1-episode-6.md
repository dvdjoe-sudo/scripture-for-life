1 Kings Episode 6: The Kingdom Tears
1 Kings 12-14, Berean Standard Bible

Rehoboam went to Shechem to be crowned, because all Israel had gone there to make him king, and Shechem was not a neutral place to ask for loyalty. It sat in the hill country of Ephraim, in the north, in Jeroboam's country, and the north had come with its spokesman already chosen and its demand already worded. Jeroboam was summoned home from Egypt, and he and the whole assembly put the case in one conditional sentence: “But now you must lighten the burden of your father's service and the heavy yoke he put on us, and we will serve you.” It was an offer, not a revolt. Serve us lighter, and we will serve you. Rehoboam sent them away for three days and took counsel twice. The elders who had stood before Solomon all his life gave him the wisdom of the old court: “If you will be a servant to these people and serve them this day, and if you will respond by speaking kind words to them, they will be your servants forever.” Serve one day, be served forever. Rehoboam rejected it and asked the young men who had grown up with him, the friends of the new reign, and their counsel was theater: tell them “My little finger is thicker than my father's waist!”, tell them the yoke would grow and the whips would become scorpions. On the third day the king answered the people harshly, in the young men's own words: “Whereas my father made your yoke heavy, I will add to your yoke. Whereas my father scourged you with whips, I will scourge you with scorpions.” And the narrator, who has been quiet through the whole negotiation, steps in with the sentence that governs the rest of the book: “So the king did not listen to the people, and indeed this turn of events was from the LORD”. Rehoboam meant every word of his threat. God meant to use it to keep a promise made to a runaway labor boss in an open field. Israel's answer was the cry of secession: “What portion do we have in David, and what inheritance in the son of Jesse? To your tents, O Israel! Look now to your own house, O David!” The assembly went home, and the united kingdom, eighty years old counting from David's anointing at Hebron, ended in an afternoon.

What happened next shows how fast the abstraction became a corpse. Rehoboam sent out Adoram, who was in charge of the forced labor, the office that Solomon's lists had filled under the name Adoniram, the human face of the yoke, sent to the people who had just renounced the yoke. All Israel stoned him to death, and Rehoboam mounted his chariot in haste and escaped to Jerusalem. “So to this day Israel has been in rebellion against the house of David.” Rehoboam still gathered an army, a hundred and eighty thousand chosen warriors from Judah and Benjamin, to take the kingdom back by force, and that war, at least, God stopped before it started. Shemaiah the man of God brought the word to the assembled army: “You are not to go up and fight against your brothers, the Israelites. Each of you must return home, for this is My doing.” And the soldiers, to their credit and the king's frustration, listened and went home. The split was permanent, and it was God's doing, and Rehoboam was left with Judah and Benjamin and the city where God had put His Name.

Jeroboam, crowned by the assembly at Shechem, fortified his new capital and built Penuel, and then he reasoned his way into the act that would define his house forever. The text lets us hear the reasoning from inside: “Now the kingdom might revert to the house of David.” If his people kept going up to Jerusalem to sacrifice, their hearts would follow their feet, and they would kill him and return to Rehoboam. It was sound politics. After seeking advice, he made two golden calves and presented them to the nation with words that reached back three centuries to the foot of Sinai: “Going up to Jerusalem is too much for you. Here, O Israel, are your gods, who brought you up out of the land of Egypt.” Aaron had said nearly the same words over one calf in the wilderness, and the book expects the echo to be heard. One calf went up at Bethel, at the southern border where his people could worship without crossing into Judah, and one at Dan, in the far north. “And this thing became a sin”, the text says, in its flattest voice. He built shrines on the high places and appointed priests from every class of people, not Levites, anyone who wanted the office. He invented a feast on the fifteenth day of the eighth month, a copy of Judah's feast moved one month later, “a month of his own choosing”, and offered sacrifices on his own altar to his own calves. Ahijah had promised him a lasting dynasty if he walked in God's ways as David did. Jeroboam had been king a matter of months, and he had already built the machine that would grind his dynasty to nothing, and staffed it, and given it a calendar.

The judgment on the machine arrived while its maker was still standing at its altar. A man of God came out of Judah to Bethel by the word of the LORD, unnamed, and cried out against the altar itself, addressing the stones instead of the king: a son named Josiah would one day be born to the house of David, and on this altar he would sacrifice the priests of the high places and burn human bones. The book's readers will not meet Josiah for nearly three hundred years of story. The sign for the present day was immediate: “Surely the altar will be split apart, and the ashes upon it will be poured out.” Jeroboam stretched out his hand from the altar and ordered the man seized, and the hand withered where it pointed, and he could not pull it back. The altar split. The ashes poured out. The king who had built the altar to keep his throne now had to beg the prophet he had tried to arrest: “Intercede with the LORD your God and pray for me that my hand may be restored.” The man of God prayed, and the hand was restored, and Jeroboam, grateful, offered him dinner and a reward, and the prophet refused both with the terms of his commission: “If you were to give me half your possessions, I still would not go with you, nor would I eat bread or drink water in this place.” He had been commanded: “You must not eat bread or drink water or return by the way you came.” He left by another road.

In Bethel lived an old prophet, and the old prophet's sons told him everything, and the old man saddled his donkey and rode after the man of God and found him sitting under an oak. He invited him home to eat. The man of God gave him the same refusal he had given the king, word for word. And the old prophet, a man of the same guild, answered with a counterfeit word: an angel had spoken to him by the word of the LORD, he said, and told him to bring the man back. The narrator does not let the lie wear its costume for one sentence: “The old prophet was lying to him”. The man of God went back, and ate bread, and drank water. At the table, mid-meal, the word of the LORD came to the liar and made him the unwilling spokesman of the sentence on his own guest: “Because you have defied the word of the LORD and have not kept the commandment that the LORD your God gave you”, the guest would die away from home: “your body shall never reach the tomb of your fathers”. The old prophet saddled the donkey for him with his own hands. On the road a lion met the man of God and killed him, and left the body in the road, and stood beside it, and the donkey stood beside it too, and travelers carried the impossible report into Bethel: the lion had done its errand and nothing more. “The lion had not eaten the body or mauled the donkey.” The old prophet fetched the body himself, and buried it in his own tomb, mourning “Oh, my brother!”, and charged his sons to lay his bones beside the prophet's bones when he died, because the word cried against Bethel's altar would surely come to pass. A liar who believed the prophecy he had interrupted with a lie, asking to sleep beside the man his lie had killed, on the strength of that man's word against his own town. And Jeroboam, whose hand had withered and been healed at that altar, whose kingdom stood under that sentence: “Even after these events, Jeroboam did not repent of his evil ways”. The chapter ends with the epitaph of his house written in advance: “And this was the sin of the house of Jeroboam that led to its extermination and destruction from the face of the earth.”

The sentence began to fall inside his own family. Abijah, Jeroboam's son, fell ill, and the king sent his wife to Ahijah at Shiloh, disguised, with peasant gifts of bread and cakes and honey, to ask the prophet who had once promised him ten tribes what would become of the boy. Ahijah was old and blind, his eyes dim with age, but God had told him who was coming and what she was pretending, and the blind man called out before she could perform her part: “Come in, wife of Jeroboam! Why are you disguised? For I have been sent to you with bad news.” The message was the dynasty's death warrant. God had raised Jeroboam up and torn the kingdom from David's house for him, “I tore the kingdom away from the house of David and gave it to you”, and Jeroboam had outrun every king before him in evil and flung God behind his back. So the house of Jeroboam would be burned up like dung, its dead eaten by dogs in the city and birds in the field. For the woman, one instruction: “As for you, get up and go home. When your feet enter the city, the child will die.” And in the middle of the warrant, one mercy, given its reason: “For this is the only one belonging to Jeroboam who will receive a proper burial, because only in him has the LORD, the God of Israel, found any good in the house of Jeroboam.” The boy was the best of the house, and the best of the house died young and was buried and mourned, the only one of them who would be. She came home to Tirzah, and as her foot crossed the threshold, the child died. Jeroboam reigned twenty-two years and rested with his fathers, and his son Nadab took the throne of a house already under sentence.

Judah's account runs in parallel and it is not a comfort. Rehoboam was forty-one when he began, he reigned seventeen years in Jerusalem, and his mother was Naamah the Ammonite, a detail the text repeats at his death, as if to keep the foreign marriages of Solomon's house in view. Judah did evil and built its own high places and pillars and Asherah poles on every high hill and under every green tree, outdoing the fathers who had done the same before the kingdom was one. In Rehoboam's fifth year the bill for Solomon's century arrived from the south: Shishak of Egypt, the same throne that had sheltered Jeroboam, attacked Jerusalem and stripped it. “He seized the treasures of the house of the LORD and of the royal palace. He took everything, including all the gold shields that Solomon had made.” The two hundred gold shields of chapter 10, the hammered splendor of the Forest of Lebanon, went out of the city on Egyptian carts five years into the new reign. Rehoboam replaced them with bronze shields, and the guards carried the bronze ones in procession whenever the king went up to the house of the LORD, and carried them back to the guardroom after, a ritual of pretending preserved in metal that told the truth. “There was war between Rehoboam and Jeroboam throughout their days.” The united kingdom had lasted two reigns. Its two halves would now spend generations bleeding each other along the border their grandfathers had crossed as one people, and the north, the larger half, stood under a sentence already pronounced, with a calf at each end of it.
