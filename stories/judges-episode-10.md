Judges Episode 10: Gibeah, and the War That Cost a Tribe
Judges 19 to 21, Berean Standard Bible

The book's last story opens with the refrain, worn smooth by now: "Now in those days, when there was no king in Israel, a Levite who lived in the remote hill country of Ephraim took for himself a concubine from Bethlehem in Judah." She was unfaithful to him and left him and went home to her father's house in Bethlehem, and after four months her husband went after her, the text says, "to speak kindly to her and bring her back," taking his servant and a pair of donkeys. Whatever passed between them, her father welcomed him gladly, and then the father began the friendliest delay in the book. Three days of eating and drinking became a fourth day, with bread to refresh his heart before the road. The fourth day became a fifth, "Please refresh your heart," until late afternoon. On the fifth day the father tried once more, the light already going: "Look, the day is drawing to a close. Please spend the night. See, the day is almost over. Spend the night here, that your heart may be merry. Then you can get up early tomorrow for your journey home." This time the Levite refused, and got up and left with the day nearly spent, a man, a servant, two donkeys, and a woman riding into the dusk because hospitality had eaten his margin of daylight.

Opposite Jebus, which is Jerusalem, the servant suggested stopping in the Jebusite city for the night. The Levite's answer is the sentence the whole story turns on, because every assumption inside it is about to be destroyed: "We will not turn aside to the city of foreigners, where there are no Israelites. We will go on to Gibeah." Foreigners were the danger; Israelites were the safety. They pushed on in the dark and came to Gibeah in Benjamin, and sat down in the city square, and discovered the first fact about Gibeah: "no one would take them into his home for the night." A traveler could sit in an Israelite town square at nightfall, with donkeys and luggage announcing him, and be left there. Only an old man, himself a sojourner from the hill country of Ephraim who worked fields outside the Benjamite town, came in at evening and took them in, with the one condition that names how dangerous the square itself was: "Peace to you," said the old man. "Let me supply everything you need. Only do not spend the night in the square." He fed the donkeys, they washed their feet and ate and drank, and the story slowed down for one paragraph of ordinary shelter, because what came next needed a house with a door.

While they were enjoying themselves, suddenly the wicked men of the city surrounded the house, pounding on the door, and demanded of the old man who owned the house: "Bring out the man who came to your house, so we can have relations with him!" The scene is Sodom, beat for beat, in a town of Benjamin: the traveler taken in, the mob at the door after dark, the demand for the guest. Only the address has changed, from the city God destroyed outside Israel to a city inside it. The old man went out and pleaded with his neighbors in the language of the guest-code: "No, my brothers, do not do this wicked thing! After all, this man is a guest in my house. Do not commit this outrage." And then, exactly as Lot had, he made the offer that exposes what the age had done to the men as well as the mob: "Look, let me bring out my virgin daughter and the man's concubine, and you can use them and do with them as you wish. But do not do such a vile thing to this man." The guest, a man, was to be protected at the price of the women. The mob would not listen. "But the men would not listen to him. So the Levite took his concubine and sent her outside to them, and they raped her and abused her throughout the night, and at dawn they let her go." The text does not decorate it. Her husband, the man who had gone to Bethlehem to speak kindly to her, put her out the door himself, and the narration gives the crime one sentence and gives her the last movement of the chapter: she crawled back to the house where her master was staying and collapsed at the doorway, and lay there until it was light, and when he opened the door in the morning to go out on his journey, "there was his concubine, collapsed in the doorway of the house, with her hands on the threshold." He told her to get up, that it was time to go. "But there was no response." He put her on his donkey and set out for home.

What he did at home is the act that summoned a nation. He took a knife and cut her body into twelve pieces, limb by limb, and sent the pieces throughout the territory of Israel, one for every tribe, a summons no herald could have matched. And the response the text records from everyone who saw it sets the scale: "Nothing like this has been seen or done from the day the Israelites came out of the land of Egypt until this day. Think it over, take counsel, and speak up!"

Israel spoke up in one body. All the Israelites "from Dan to Beersheba" and from Gilead assembled "as one man before the LORD at Mizpah," four hundred thousand swordsmen, the largest gathering of the era, and there was no judge over them and no king; there was a congregation doing what the cycle had never yet produced, unified national outrage. They asked how the wicked thing had happened, and the Levite testified. His account stands in the text beside chapter 19's own narration, and the two do not cover the same ground. He told the assembly: "I and my concubine came to Gibeah in Benjamin to spend the night. And during the night, the men of Gibeah rose up against me and surrounded the house. They intended to kill me, but they abused my concubine, and she died." Then: "Then I took my concubine, cut her into pieces, and sent her throughout the land of Israel's inheritance, because they had committed a lewd and disgraceful act in Israel. Behold, all you Israelites, give your advice and verdict here and now." What his testimony does not contain is the doorway, his own hands sending her out to the mob to save himself. The assembly heard the edited version, and the reader of the book has both. The verdict was immediate and unanimous: "Not one of us will return to his tent or to his house." They would go against Gibeah, and first they sent messengers through Benjamin with the demand any court would recognize: "Hand over the wicked men of Gibeah so we can put them to death and purge Israel of this evil." Justice, targeted at the criminals, with the rest of the tribe untouched. "But the Benjamites refused to heed the voice of their fellow Israelites." Benjamin chose its worst men over its brothers, mustered twenty-six thousand swordsmen from its cities plus seven hundred select men of Gibeah itself, and among them the seven hundred left-handers the book had promised, "each of whom could sling a stone at a hair without missing."

The war that followed did not go the way numbers or righteousness suggested it should. Israel went up to Bethel and inquired of God, and heard the oldest answer in the book: "Who of us shall go up first to fight against the Benjamites?" "Judah will be first," the LORD replied, the same appointment Judah received the day after Joshua died. Day one: Benjamin came out of Gibeah "and cut down 22,000 Israelites on the battlefield that day." Israel took courage, formed up in the same place, went up and wept before the LORD until evening, and asked whether to go again against their brothers, and the answer was: "Go up against them." Day two: Benjamin cut down eighteen thousand more. Forty thousand Israelites were dead in two days for demanding justice against rapists and murderers, at God's own direction to keep going, and the text offers no explanation on the spot. It shows Israel doing the only thing left: "Then the Israelites, all the people, went up to Bethel, where they sat weeping before the LORD. That day they fasted until evening and presented burnt offerings and peace offerings to the LORD." Then they inquired a third time, and the narrator supplies the credentials of the moment, which are also this story's birth certificate: in those days the ark of the covenant was at Bethel, "and Phinehas son of Eleazar, the son of Aaron, served before it." Phinehas, who had speared an Israelite man and a Midianite woman at Peor in the wilderness and turned away a plague, Aaron's grandson, still ministering. This war is an old man's war, a first-generation war; the whole appendix is. The question was whether to go out again "against our brothers the Benjamites, or should we stop," and this time the answer carried the promise the first two had withheld: "Fight, for tomorrow I will deliver them into your hand."

On the third day Israel fought Benjamin the way God had taught it to fight Ai, with an ambush, the only stratagem that had ever taken a walled Israelite-era city in the conquest. They arrayed against Gibeah as before, let Benjamin come out and draw them into a feigned retreat along the roads, killing about thirty as the trap was set, and Benjamin read the battle wrong out loud: "We are defeating them as before," said the Benjamites. The ambushers rushed Gibeah and put the whole city to the sword, and sent up the arranged signal, a great column of smoke, and the main body turned, "and the men of Benjamin were terrified when they realized that disaster had come upon them." They fled toward the wilderness and the battle overtook them on the roads and in the fields east of their own city: eighteen thousand fell that day in the pursuit, five thousand more cut down on the roads, two thousand at Gidom, twenty-five thousand and one hundred by the day's own count, "all men of valor." Six hundred Benjamites reached the rock of Rimmon and held out there in the wilderness for four months. And Israel completed the work past the battlefield, turning back through Benjamin's territory and putting to the sword every city, the people, the animals, everything, and burning the cities in its path. It had demanded Benjamin hand over a city's criminals, and Benjamin's refusal had widened the sentence to a tribe: twenty-five thousand dead, every town ash, six hundred men alive on a rock.

Then the victors sat down at Bethel and wept over what they had won, and discovered the trap their own oaths had built. At Mizpah, in the heat of the summons, Israel had sworn that none of them would give a daughter in marriage to a Benjamite. Now: "Why, O LORD God of Israel," they cried out, "has this happened in Israel? Today in Israel one tribe is missing!" The six hundred at Rimmon would die unmarried, and Benjamin, Jacob's youngest, Rachel's son, would end in a generation, erased not by any enemy but by the arithmetic of Israelite oaths. Grief produced a search for loopholes, and the first loophole was a second oath: anyone who had not come up to the Mizpah assembly was under sentence of death. The count was taken, and one town had never come: "no one from Jabesh-gilead had come to the camp for the assembly." So the congregation sent twelve thousand of its most valiant men against an Israelite town that had committed no crime except absence, with orders in the language of the old conquest: "Go and put to the sword those living in Jabesh-gilead, including women and children. This is what you are to do: Devote to destruction every male, as well as every female who has had relations with a man." They found four hundred young women who had never known a man, and spared them, and carried them to Shiloh as wives for Benjamin. Four hundred women purchased with their whole town. The congregation sent a message of peace to Rimmon, and the six hundred came down and received the women of Jabesh-gilead, "but there were not enough women for all of them."

The second loophole was worse, and the elders designed it themselves. There was a yearly feast of the LORD at Shiloh, with the daughters of Shiloh dancing in the vineyards. The elders commanded the remaining Benjamites: "Go, hide in the vineyards and watch. When you see the daughters of Shiloh come out to perform their dances, each of you is to come out of the vineyards, catch for himself a wife from the daughters of Shiloh, and go to the land of Benjamin." And for the fathers and brothers who would come to complain, the elders had the legal theory ready: "Do us a favor by helping them, since we did not get wives for each of them in the war. Since you did not actually give them your daughters, you have no guilt." You did not give them; they were taken; the oath is intact. Everyone could recite why it was permitted, and no one in the text calls it anything but what it is, an arranged abduction at a feast of the LORD, engineered by the elders of Israel to outwit an oath they regretted. The Benjamites did it, carried away the number of women they needed, went home, rebuilt their burned cities, and settled in them.

"And at that time, each of the Israelites returned from there to his own tribe and clan, each to his own inheritance." The assembly that had gathered as one man dissolved into its inheritances, and the book of Judges closes with the line it has been building since the angel wept at Bochim, the refrain in full, as the final word on the era: "In those days there was no king in Israel; everyone did what was right in his own eyes."

That is where the book leaves Israel: not under a foreign oppressor, not crying out, not waiting for a deliverer, but quiet, and maimed by its own hands, having started the era unable to drive out the nations and ended it unable to stop destroying itself. And yet the series does not leave the era on this page. The next story in the order Joe set for this telling is a small one that happened somewhere inside these same years, in Bethlehem and Moab and back again, about a widow and her daughter-in-law and a man named Boaz, and it opens with the era's own timestamp: in the days when the judges ruled. After Gibeah, it reads like a light left on in the same dark country, and it begins with a famine and a family leaving Bethlehem, the house of bread, to go and live in Moab.
