Judges Episode 2: Othniel, Ehud, and the Left Hand
Judges 3, Berean Standard Bible

The nations left in the land had a purpose now, and the narrator states it twice so it cannot be missed. They were left "to test all the Israelites who had not known any of the wars in Canaan," and to teach warfare to a generation that had inherited battles it never watched, "to find out whether they would keep the commandments of the LORD, which He had given their fathers through Moses." The list was the Philistines' five rulers on the coast, the Canaanites, the Sidonians, and the Hivites in the Lebanon mountains. Israel's answer to the test came in three steps, each one a verse quieter than a battle: "Thus the Israelites continued to live among the Canaanites, Hittites, Amorites, Perizzites, Hivites, and Jebusites. And they took the daughters of these people in marriage, gave their own daughters to their sons, and served their gods." Lived among, married among, worshiped among. The cycle's first turn followed on schedule: "So the Israelites did evil in the sight of the LORD; they forgot the LORD their God and served the Baals and the Asherahs."

Forgotten is the exact word, and the first oppressor came from the far side of the world Israel knew. God sold them into the hand of Cushan-rishathaim, king of Aram-naharaim up in Mesopotamia, and Israel served him eight years. Eight years, then the cry the cycle runs on: "But when the Israelites cried out to the LORD, He raised up Othniel son of Caleb's younger brother Kenaz as a deliverer to save them."

Othniel is the model judge, and the book gives him almost no words because there is nothing wrong to record. The reader has met him twice already, taking Debir for Caleb in Joshua and again in Judges 1, marrying Achsah, settling by her springs. Now, a generation later: "The Spirit of the LORD came upon him, and he became Israel's judge and went out to war. And the LORD delivered Cushan-rishathaim king of Aram into the hand of Othniel, who prevailed against him." No stratagem, no speech, no flaw. "So the land had rest for forty years, until Othniel son of Kenaz died." Forty years of rest is the longest a judge's generation usually gets, and it ends the way every rest in this book ends, with the deliverer's death.

"Once again the Israelites did evil in the sight of the LORD." The second oppressor was closer and worse organized against Israel than the first. God gave Eglon king of Moab power over Israel, and Eglon enlisted the Ammonites and the Amalekites, Israel's oldest enemies, beat Israel in the field, and took the City of Palms, Jericho itself, the first city of the conquest, now a Moabite tax post. Israel served Eglon eighteen years, more than double the first oppression. The pattern in the numbers is the book's quiet argument: the relapses cost more each time.

"And again they cried out to the LORD, and He raised up Ehud son of Gera, a left-handed Benjamite, as their deliverer." Everything in that sentence is loaded. Benjamin's name means son of the right hand, and its deliverer is a man whose right hand is not his sword hand, a difference his tribe was known for; this same book will remember Benjaminite slingers as left-handed warriors, each of whom could sling a stone at a hair and not miss. Israel sent Ehud to Eglon carrying the tribute, the tax of the occupied, and Ehud prepared for the audience by making himself a weapon fitted to his difference: "Now Ehud had made for himself a double-edged sword a cubit long. He strapped it to his right thigh under his cloak." A cubit is roughly eighteen inches, a dagger more than a sword. A right-handed guard frisks the left side, where swords hang. Ehud's hung on the right, under his cloak, on the thigh nobody searched.

He presented the tribute to Eglon, "who was an obese man," and the text does not waste that detail either. The carriers were ushered out. Ehud went with them as far as the idols near Gilgal, Gilgal, of all places, where twelve stones from the Jordan still stood for the crossing, and there he turned back alone and came to the king with the oldest key in the assassin's trade, a secret: "I have a secret message for you, O king." Eglon commanded silence, and all his attendants left him.

Eglon was sitting alone in the coolness of his upper room when Ehud approached and traded the secret for a sentence: "I have a word from God for you," Ehud said, and the king rose from his seat, a mark of respect for a divine message that put his belly in reach. "And Ehud reached with his left hand, pulled the sword from his right thigh, and plunged it into Eglon's belly. Even the handle sank in after the blade, and Eglon's fat closed in over it, so that Ehud did not withdraw the sword from his belly. And Eglon's bowels emptied." The text tells it without flinching and without celebration, a king killed on his feet by the tribute-bearer he had taxed for eighteen years, dead with the weapon still in him. Ehud went out through the porch, closing and locking the doors of the upper room behind him, and walked away.

What bought him his head start was the locked door and Moabite politeness. The servants found the doors locked and supplied the respectable explanation themselves: "He must be relieving himself in the cool room," they said. So they waited. The text lingers on the waiting until it curdles: "So they waited until they became worried and saw that he had still not opened the doors of the upper room. Then they took the key and opened the doors," and there was their lord lying dead on the floor.

By then Ehud had passed the idols and reached Seirah, in the hill country of Ephraim, where he blew the ram's horn, the shofar, the sound that had circled Jericho. Israel came down from the hills behind him, and his call to arms credited the victory before it happened: follow me, he told them, "for the LORD has delivered your enemies the Moabites into your hand." They seized the fords of the Jordan leading to Moab and let no one cross. The Moabite force that had occupied the City of Palms was cut off from home on the far side of its own escape route, and the count is as blunt as the assassination: "At that time they struck down about ten thousand Moabites, all robust and valiant men. Not one of them escaped." Moab was subdued under Israel's hand that day, "and the land had rest for eighty years", the longest rest the book will record, won by the judge with the longest story and the strangest weapon.

Then the chapter ends with a judge who gets one verse. "After Ehud came Shamgar son of Anath. And he too saved Israel, striking down six hundred Philistines with an oxgoad." An oxgoad is a farmer's stick for prodding oxen, sometimes tipped with iron. Six hundred Philistines, the coastal people with the iron, killed by a man with a farm tool, and the narrator gives him no cry, no Spirit, no rest years, just "he too saved Israel," because in this book the deliverers come in every size and the pattern does not wait for a full story.

Two turns of the cycle are complete. The oppressions ran eight years and eighteen; the rests ran forty and eighty. Read optimistically, Israel is improving. Read the way chapter 2 taught, the numbers that matter are still ahead: the next oppression will run twenty years, and it will not be a king at a distance or a fat tax collector in Jericho. It will be nine hundred iron chariots in the north, and the deliverer God raises will be a woman under a palm tree, judging Israel in the open, because the men of the story will need her courage before they find their own.
