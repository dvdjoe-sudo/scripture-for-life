Judges Episode 5: Gideon's Ephod, and Abimelech
Judges 8 to 9, Berean Standard Bible

Victory produced its first conflict before the pursuit even ended, and it came from inside Israel. The men of Ephraim met Gideon with a grievance sharpened into a fight: "Why have you done this to us? Why did you fail to call us when you went to fight against Midian?" And they contended with him violently. Ephraim was the proud central tribe, called late, handed the Jordan crossings and the two princes, and still insulted at missing the opening battle. Gideon's answer is a small masterpiece of defusing, and every word of it is honor handed over in public: "Now what have I accomplished compared to you? Are not the gleanings of Ephraim better than the grape harvest of Abiezer? God has delivered Oreb and Zeeb, the two princes of Midian, into your hand. What was I able to do compared to you?" Your leftovers outweigh my whole harvest; God gave you the princes; what was I beside you? "When he had said this, their anger against him subsided." Diplomacy worked. Remember that it worked, because east of the Jordan Gideon stops using it.

He crossed the river with his three hundred, "exhausted yet still in pursuit," chasing the two kings of Midian who were still at large, Zebah and Zalmunna, and he asked the Israelite town of Succoth for bread for his men. Succoth's leaders refused with a taunt disguised as a question: "Are the hands of Zebah and Zalmunna already in your possession, that we should give bread to your army?" Show us the kings in chains, then we'll feed you. They were hedging, keeping Midian's favor available if Gideon lost, and Gideon heard the hedge and answered it with a threat he fully intended to keep, and the threat was a promise: "when the LORD has delivered Zebah and Zalmunna into my hand, I will tear your flesh with the thorns and briers of the wilderness!" Penuel, the next town up, gave the same answer and got its own sentence: "When I return in triumph, I will tear down this tower!"

The kings were at Karkor with fifteen thousand men, all that remained of a host of a hundred and thirty-five thousand swordsmen. Gideon came up by the caravan route east of Nobah and Jogbehah and hit the camp by surprise, and captured Zebah and Zalmunna alive, exactly the proof Succoth had demanded. On the way back he caught a young man of Succoth and interrogated him, and the young man wrote down the names of the town's seventy-seven leaders and elders, a list made to be used. Gideon presented the kings to the men who had refused him bread and quoted their own taunt back at them with the evidence standing beside him, and then he kept his word: "Then he took the elders of the city, and using the thorns and briers of the wilderness, he disciplined the men of Succoth." At Penuel he pulled down the tower and killed the men of the city. Read the two halves of the chapter against each other and the slide is right there in the hero. West of the Jordan, insulted by Ephraim's pride, Gideon answered with humility and made peace. East of it, refused by frightened Israelite towns, he answered with thorns and a demolished tower and dead Israelites. The deliverer is starting to punish the delivered.

One account remained, and it was personal. Gideon asked the captured kings what kind of men they had killed at Tabor, and their answer sealed them: "Men like you," they answered, "each one resembling the son of a king." "They were my brothers," Gideon replied, "the sons of my mother! As surely as the LORD lives, if you had let them live, I would not kill you." He ordered his firstborn Jether to execute them, and the boy would not draw his sword, "he was fearful because he was still a youth," a frightened son of a father who once hid in a winepress and tore down an altar at night for fear. The kings asked for the cleaner death themselves: "Get up and kill us yourself, for as the man is, so is his strength." Gideon killed them, and took the crescent ornaments from their camels' necks. It is the first gold he takes in the chapter. It is not the last.

Then Israel made the offer the whole era had been circling: "Rule over us, you and your son and grandson, for you have saved us from the hand of Midian." A hereditary kingship, offered by acclamation to the deliverer. Gideon's refusal is the theological high ground of the book: "I will not rule over you, nor will my son. The LORD shall rule over you." And in the very next breath he made his request: an earring from every man's plunder. They gave gladly, a garment spread out and gold thrown onto it, until the earrings alone weighed 1,700 shekels of gold, besides the crescents and pendants and purple garments of the kings. "From all this Gideon made an ephod, which he placed in Ophrah, his hometown. But soon all Israel prostituted themselves by worshiping it there, and it became a snare to Gideon and his household."

Hold the two acts side by side, because the text does. The man who would not be king built a golden shrine in his hometown and all Israel came to worship at it. He refused the title and took the trappings: seventy sons by many wives, a royal household in everything but name, and a concubine in Shechem who bore him a son he named Abimelech, which means my father is king. The refusal was real words. The life was a court's life. And the land rested forty years in the days of Gideon, and he died at a ripe old age and was buried in his father's tomb at Ophrah, and the machine turned instantly: "And as soon as Gideon was dead, the Israelites turned and prostituted themselves with the Baals, and they set up Baal-berith as their god. The Israelites failed to remember the LORD their God who had delivered them from the hands of all their enemies on every side. They did not show kindness to the house of Jerubbaal (that is, Gideon) for all the good things he had done for Israel."

The ingratitude had a beneficiary, and he moved fast. Abimelech went to Shechem, to his mother's clan, with a pitch built on blood and arithmetic. Ask all the leaders of Shechem, he told them: "Is it better for you that seventy men, all the sons of Jerubbaal, rule over you, or just one man?" And remind them: "Remember that I am your own flesh and blood." The leaders' hearts inclined to him for the reason he had scripted: "He is our brother." They funded the coup out of the temple treasury of Baal-berith, seventy shekels of silver, with which Abimelech "hired some worthless and reckless men to follow him." Then he went home to Ophrah and did the thing the pitch had promised to prevent anyone else from doing: "He went to his father's house in Ophrah, and on one stone murdered his seventy brothers, the sons of Jerubbaal. But Jotham, the youngest son of Jerubbaal, survived, because he hid himself." Seventy shekels, seventy brothers, one stone. The leaders of Shechem and Beth-millo crowned the survivor's murderer king beside the oak at the pillar in Shechem, in the city where Jacob had buried idols under an oak and Joshua had renewed the covenant under one.

Jotham, the one who hid, climbed Mount Gerizim, the mountain of blessing from the covenant ceremony, and shouted a parable down at the coronation. The trees went out to anoint a king. The olive tree refused: "Should I stop giving my oil that honors both God and man, to hold sway over the trees?" The fig tree refused its sweetness, the vine refused its wine, each too fruitful to rule. Finally all the trees said to the thornbush the words the fruitful trees had refused under: "Come and reign over us." And the thornbush, the one plant with nothing to give but shade it does not have and fire it does, accepted with a threat: "If you really are anointing me as king over you, come and find refuge in my shade. But if not, may fire come out of the thornbush and consume the cedars of Lebanon." Jotham then applied it without flinching, rehearsing Gideon's rescue and the stone, and laid the terms: if Shechem had acted honestly, rejoice in Abimelech. "But if not, may fire come from Abimelech and consume the leaders of Shechem and Beth-millo, and may fire come from the leaders of Shechem and Beth-millo and consume Abimelech." He ran for his life to Beer, and the curse hung over the city for three years, waiting for its fuel.

It came on schedule, and the text assigns the arsonist openly: after three years "God sent a spirit of animosity between Abimelech and the leaders of Shechem," so that the crime against the seventy sons might come to justice and their blood be avenged on both the murderer and the men who had funded him. Shechem's leaders began ambushing travelers on the hilltops to rob Abimelech's revenue, and into the rot walked Gaal son of Ebed, a loud newcomer who won the city at a harvest festival in the house of their god, drinking and cursing Abimelech between courses: "Who is Abimelech, and who is Shechem, that we should serve him? Is he not the son of Jerubbaal, and is not Zebul his officer? You are to serve the men of Hamor, the father of Shechem. Why should we serve Abimelech?" Zebul, Abimelech's governor, heard it and sent a warning by night, and Abimelech came with four companies and lay in the fields till sunrise.

The morning scene is almost comedy played over a grave. Gaal stood at the gate and saw men coming down the hills and reported it, and Zebul stalled him with a sneer: "The shadows of the mountains look like men to you." When Gaal insisted, people coming down by the Diviners' Oak, Zebul dropped the act: "Where is your gloating now?" Then he threw Gaal's own festival words back at him: "Who is Abimelech that we should serve him?" "Are these not the people you ridiculed? Go out now and fight them!" Gaal fought, broke, and fled, and Zebul drove him and his brothers out of Shechem. The next day Abimelech ambushed the city folk in their fields, took the city, killed its people, demolished it, and sowed it with salt, a curse plowed into the ground so nothing would grow there again. Jotham's fire had begun moving, and it moved in both directions exactly as pronounced.

The leaders who had crowned him fled into the inner chamber of the temple of El-berith in the tower of Shechem, about a thousand men and women, and Abimelech repeated their own coronation gift back to them as fuel. On Mount Zalmon he cut a branch with his axe, hoisted it on his shoulder, and told his men: "Hurry and do what you have seen me do." They piled the branches against the chamber and burned it over the people inside. Every leader who had said "He is our brother" died by fire from Abimelech's hand. Then he marched on Thebez and took it, and its people fled to a strong tower inside the city, and Abimelech came up to its entrance to set the same fire a second time.

This time the tower answered. "But a woman dropped an upper millstone on Abimelech's head, crushing his skull." A millstone, a kitchen stone, dropped by an unnamed woman from a roof, the second woman in the book to kill a commander from above while he attacked. Dying, Abimelech's last thought was for the wording of his death: "Draw your sword and kill me, lest they say of me," and his fear names itself in his own quoted words, a woman killed him. His armor-bearer ran him through, and he died by an Israelite sword with a woman's millstone as the cause, both halves of his shame preserved. "And when the Israelites saw that Abimelech was dead, they all went home." No rest is recorded. No judge is named. The narrator closes the account with the audit instead: "In this way God repaid the wickedness that Abimelech had done to his father in murdering his seventy brothers. And God also brought all the wickedness of the men of Shechem back upon their own heads. So the curse of Jotham son of Jerubbaal came upon them."

Israel's first king was never raised up by God, never anointed, never called a judge. He was a deliverer's son who bought a crown with temple silver and held it three years by murder, and the fire his half brother promised from Gerizim consumed the buyers and the bought alike. The book pauses now for two quiet judges whose whole records fit in a few verses, Tola and Jair, and then the cycle turns again, and this time when Israel cries, God answers the cry with an argument before He answers it with a deliverer.
