Joshua Episode 3: The Gibeonite Covenant and the Long Day
Joshua 9 to 10, Berean Standard Bible

When the kings west of the Jordan heard what had happened at Jericho and Ai, they did the expected thing: "they came together to wage war against Joshua and Israel." All of them, that is, except one city. "But the people of Gibeon, having heard what Joshua had done to Jericho and Ai, acted deceptively and set out as envoys," and their deception was staged like theater. Worn-out sacks on the donkeys. Old wineskins, cracked and mended. Patched sandals, threadbare clothes, and a whole supply of bread gone dry and moldy. Every prop said the same sentence: we have traveled very far.

At Gilgal they made their pitch: "We have come from a distant land; please make a treaty with us." Israel's first instinct was the right question: "Perhaps you dwell near us. How can we make a treaty with you?" But the Gibeonites had a script for that too. "We are your servants," they said, and when Joshua pressed them on who they were and where they had come from, they answered with the fame of Israel's God, the reports from Egypt and the two Amorite kings east of the Jordan, Sihon and Og. It was a careful speech, and a reader listening closely notices what it never mentions: Jericho. Ai. Anything recent. The news they recited was old enough to have crossed a desert.

Then came the exhibit. "This bread of ours was warm when we packed it at home on the day we left to come to you. But look, it is now dry and moldy. These wineskins were new when we filled them, but look, they are cracked. And these clothes and sandals are worn out from our very long journey."

Israel examined the evidence, and the narrator stops the story cold for the most consequential sentence in the chapter, fifteen words that explain everything that follows: "Then the men of Israel sampled their provisions but did not seek the counsel of the LORD." They tasted the bread. They did not ask God. Joshua made a treaty of peace to let them live, and the leaders of the congregation swore an oath to them by the LORD.

Three days later the truth arrived: the distant foreigners were neighbors, living at Gibeon, Chephirah, Beeroth, and Kiriath-jearim, close enough to walk to. The congregation grumbled against its leaders, and the leaders gave an answer that held the whole episode's weight: "We have sworn an oath to them by the LORD, the God of Israel, and now we cannot touch them. This is how we will treat them: We will let them live, so that no wrath will fall on us because of the oath we swore to them." A treaty obtained by lying still bound the people who had sworn it, because the Name attached to it was not theirs to cheapen. The Gibeonites would live, as woodcutters and water carriers for the congregation.

Joshua summoned them and asked the question plainly: "Why did you deceive us by telling us you live far away from us, when in fact you live among us?" Their answer was as honest as their embassy had been false: "Your servants were told clearly that the LORD your God had commanded His servant Moses to give you all the land and wipe out all its inhabitants before you. So we greatly feared for our lives because of you, and that is why we have done this. Now we are in your hands. Do to us whatever seems good and right to you." Joshua confirmed the sentence and the mercy together: they would serve "perpetually" as "woodcutters and water carriers for the house of my God". And the narrator notes they hold that place "for the congregation of the LORD and for the altar at the place He would choose", as they are "to this day." The liars of Gibeon ended up carrying water for the altar of the God they had feared from a distance. It was not the outcome any treaty in the ancient world would have predicted.

The treaty was immediately tested. Adoni-zedek, king of Jerusalem, heard that Gibeon had made peace with Israel, and he was alarmed, because Gibeon was "a great city, like one of the royal cities", larger than Ai, with mighty men. He built a coalition of five kings, Jerusalem, Hebron, Jarmuth, Lachish, and Eglon, and aimed it at the defectors: "Come up and help me. We will attack Gibeon, because they have made peace with Joshua and the Israelites."

Gibeon's first act under the treaty was to collect on it. Their message to Gilgal was urgent: "Do not abandon your servants. Come quickly and save us! Help us, because all the kings of the Amorites from the hill country have joined forces against us." And Joshua, bound by an oath his men had sworn without asking God, marched all night from Gilgal with the whole army to defend the people who had tricked him. God's word for the night march came before the battle: "Do not be afraid of them, for I have delivered them into your hand. Not one of them shall stand against you."

Joshua caught the five armies by surprise after the all-night climb, and the LORD threw them into confusion. Israel routed them at Gibeon and pursued them up the ascent to Beth-horon and down the other side as far as Azekah and Makkedah. On the descent the battle left Israel's hands entirely: "As they fled before Israel along the descent from Beth-horon to Azekah, the LORD cast down on them large hailstones from the sky, and more of them were killed by the hailstones than by the swords of the Israelites."

Then Joshua prayed the most public prayer in the book, standing before Israel in the middle of a battle that needed more daylight than the day had: "O sun, stand still over Gibeon, O moon, over the Valley of Aijalon." The text answers with the event and its source in the same breath: "So the sun stood still and the moon stopped until the nation took vengeance upon its enemies. Is this not written in the Book of Jashar?" The narrator is quoting an older book of song, and the seam is left where any reader can see it. What the quotation says is this: "So the sun stopped in the middle of the sky and delayed going down about a full day." And the narrator's own verdict on the day outranks the astronomy: "There has been no day like it before or since, when the LORD listened to the voice of a man, because the LORD fought for Israel."

The five kings, meanwhile, had run from the field and hidden in a cave at Makkedah. Joshua treated the cave as a holding cell, not a distraction: "Roll large stones against the mouth of the cave, and post men there to guard them." Then back to the pursuit: "Do not let them reach their cities, for the LORD your God has delivered them into your hand." Only when the slaughter was finished and the army had returned safely did he have the cave opened and the kings brought out. He called his commanders forward and made the victory physical and transferable: "Come here and put your feet on the necks of these kings." Then he handed them the charge he had lived under since the plains of Moab, now as something they could carry into every remaining battle: "Do not be afraid or discouraged". "Be strong and courageous, for the LORD will do this to all the enemies you fight."

The kings were killed and hung on five trees until evening, then taken down at sunset, as the law required, and thrown into the cave they had hidden in, and great stones were rolled against its mouth, and the narrator's refrain sounds: "the stones are there to this day."

What followed was a campaign at the speed of the narration itself, city after city in a wave. Makkedah fell that same day, its king treated as Jericho's had been. Libnah fell, no survivors. Lachish fell on the second day of its siege, and Horam king of Gezer, who came to help it, fell with his people. Eglon fell the day it was attacked. Hebron fell, its king and villages with it. Debir fell, the last name in the roll. The narrator keeps a single refrain running under the names, left no survivors, devoted to destruction, as he had done at the last city, and then gathers the whole sweep into one summary: "So Joshua conquered the whole region", the hill country, the Negev, the foothills, and the slopes, "together with all their kings", "leaving no survivors. He devoted to destruction everything that breathed, just as the LORD, the God of Israel, had commanded."

The geography runs from Kadesh-barnea to Gaza, and the whole region of Goshen as far as Gibeon, the city whose moldy bread had started this whole southern war. And the explanation the book gives for the speed is not Joshua's generalship: "And because the LORD, the God of Israel, fought for Israel, Joshua captured all these kings and their land in one campaign." Then Joshua returned with all Israel to the camp at Gilgal.

The south had fallen in a single campaign that began with a deception and a night march. But the kings of the north had been watching, and they were gathering a force the text will measure against the sand of the seashore.
