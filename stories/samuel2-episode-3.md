2 Samuel Episode 3: King Over All Israel, and Jerusalem
2 Samuel 5 to 6, Berean Standard Bible

Seven and a half years after Hebron, the whole nation came to David there. All the tribes of Israel stood before him with a claim and a memory. The claim was kinship: here we are, your own flesh and blood. The memory was the open secret of Saul's reign, that even while Saul was king it was David who had led Israel out and brought them back. And they quoted the promise over him, the word spoken years before by Samuel's God: "You will shepherd My people Israel, and you will be ruler over them." So the elders of Israel came to the king at Hebron, and David made a covenant with them before the LORD, a king bound by agreement and not merely by conquest, and they anointed him king over Israel. He was thirty years old when he became king, and he reigned forty years, seven and a half in Hebron over Judah and thirty-three in Jerusalem over all Israel and Judah. The shepherd of Bethlehem had the whole flock now. What he needed was a capital that belonged to no tribe, and he knew exactly where it was.

Jerusalem sat on the border between Judah and Benjamin, held by the Jebusites, unconquered since Joshua's day, so secure on its rock that its defenders taunted David from the walls: he would never get in; even the blind and the lame could repel him. The taunt became the city's epitaph. David took the fortress of Zion by its water shaft, the hidden artery the Jebusites had trusted, and the fortress became the City of David. He built it up from the terraces inward, and the text's verdict on these years is the sentence that stands under his whole reign: David became greater and greater, for the LORD God of Hosts was with him. Hiram king of Tyre, the great trading power on the coast, read the situation correctly and sent cedar logs and carpenters and masons, and a palace rose in the captured city. And David understood, the narrator says, that the LORD had established him as king over Israel and had exalted his kingdom not for David's sake but for the sake of His people Israel. It is the healthiest sentence in the book about the use of power, and it comes early, before the book tests it.

The Philistines also read the situation. Twice they came up in force into the Valley of Rephaim to end the reunified kingdom before it set, and twice the new king did the old thing he had learned in the wilderness: he inquired of the LORD. The first time, the answer was to go straight up, and David crushed them at Baal-perazim and named the place for the breakthrough: "Like a bursting flood, the LORD has burst out against my enemies before me." The second time, the answer was different instructions for the same enemy in the same valley: do not march straight up; circle behind them; wait for the sound of marching in the tops of the balsam trees, for that would mean the LORD had gone out before him. David obeyed the stranger order as exactly as he had obeyed the simple one, and struck the Philistines from Gibeon to Gezer. Israel's ancient enemy would raid again, but it would never again rule the story. The wars of survival were over. What remained was the harder work of building a kingdom that could hold God at its center without mishandling Him.

That work began with the ark. The ark of God had rested for decades in the house of Abinadab at Kiriath-jearim, ever since Beth-shemesh sent it away, and David determined to bring it up to his new capital with the whole nation watching. Thirty thousand chosen men assembled, and the ark was set on a new cart, drawn by oxen, guided by Abinadab's sons Uzzah and Ahio, and David and all Israel celebrated before the LORD with every instrument they had. A new cart was how the Philistines had once sent the ark home. It was efficient, and it was festive, and at the threshing floor of Nacon the oxen stumbled, and Uzzah reached out and took hold of the ark of God to steady it, and the anger of the LORD burned against him, and God struck him down on the spot for his irreverence, and he died there beside the ark. The text gives no softening and no explanation beyond the word irreverence: the holiest object in Israel was not cargo, and a well-meant hand was still a hand laid on what God had said was untouchable. David's anger came first, hot at the LORD who had burst forth against Uzzah the way He had burst forth against the Philistines, and David named the place Perez-uzzah, the bursting against Uzzah. Then the anger cooled into the truer emotion: that day David feared the LORD, and asked the question the whole chapter exists to ask: "How can the ark of the LORD ever come to me?" He would not move it. The ark went aside, into the house of a man named Obed-edom the Gittite, a foreigner by his name, a Gathite living in Israel, and it stayed there three months. And the LORD blessed Obed-edom and all his household, because of the ark of God.

The blessing was the report that changed David's mind, and the second procession was built on what the first had taught. This time there was no cart. The ark was carried, as it was made to be carried, and when the bearers had advanced six paces David sacrificed an ox and a fattened calf, sealing the road in worship from its first step. And David wore a linen ephod, dressed not as a king presiding but as a servant attending, and he danced with all his might before the LORD while the ark came up with shouting and the sound of the ram's horn. In a window above the street stood Michal, Saul's daughter, restored to David by treaty after her years with Paltiel, and she looked down and saw the king of Israel leaping and dancing before the LORD, stripped of every royal dignity, indistinguishable from the servants around the ark. And she despised him in her heart. The daughter of the first king watched the second king make himself small before God, and read it only as shame.

She met him at his own door with the shame spelled out, dressed in the politeness of a court and the contempt of a house that had lost everything: how the king of Israel had distinguished himself today, uncovering himself before the maidservants of his subjects like a vulgar person. David's answer reached past her to the whole history between their houses. It was before the LORD that he had danced, he told her, the LORD who chose him over her father and over all his house and appointed him ruler over the LORD's people Israel. "I will celebrate before the LORD, and I will humiliate and humble myself even more than this. Yet I will be honored by the maidservants of whom you have spoken." It is a complete exchange: her standard was the dignity of Saul's court; his standard was the God who had replaced that court. The book closes the scene and, in a sense, the house of Saul itself with one flat sentence, delivered without comment and heavy with it: Michal the daughter of Saul had no children to the day of her death. The ark was in the City of David, in a tent David had pitched for it. The king had a palace of cedar. And that contrast, a housed king and a tented God, is where the next episode begins.
