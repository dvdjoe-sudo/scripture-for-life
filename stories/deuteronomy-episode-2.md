Deuteronomy Episode 2: Hear, O Israel
Deuteronomy 5 to 11, Berean Standard Bible

Moses summoned all Israel and began the second address where the first one had pointed: back at the mountain.

"Hear, O Israel, the statutes and ordinances that I declare in your hearing this day. Learn them and observe them carefully."

The LORD our God made a covenant with us at Horeb, he told them, and he was careful about the word us. Not with our fathers did the LORD make this covenant, but with us, with all of us who are alive here today. The parents who had stood at the foot of that mountain and heard the voice out of the fire were dead in the wilderness, every one of them but Caleb and Joshua. The crowd on the plains was their children, and Moses opened the law by handing them their parents' mountain as their own. Face to face, he said, the LORD spoke with you on the mountain out of the midst of the fire. I stood between the LORD and you at that time, to declare to you the word of the LORD, because you were afraid of the fire and would not go up the mountain.

Then he read the ten words again, the same ten Israel had heard in Exodus, and a first-time reader working through the books in order can hear the seams where the second giving differs from the first.

"I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery."

You shall have no other gods before Me. You shall not make an idol in any form, or bow down to them, for I the LORD your God am a jealous God, visiting the iniquity of the fathers on their children to the third and fourth generations of those who hate Me, but showing loving devotion to a thousand generations of those who love Me and keep My commandments. You shall not take the name of the LORD your God in vain. Observe the Sabbath day by keeping it holy. You shall not murder. You shall not commit adultery. You shall not steal. You shall not bear false witness against your neighbor. You shall not covet.

The Sabbath is where the difference lives, and Moses left it where any listener could find it. In Exodus, God rested on the seventh day of creation, and that was the reason given at Sinai for Israel's rest. Here the commandment carries a second reason, an Egyptian one.

"Remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt, and that the LORD your God brought you out of there with a mighty hand and an outstretched arm. That is why the LORD your God has commanded you to keep the Sabbath day."

Rest, in the second telling, is not only God imitated. It is slavery remembered. The man who worked seven days under Pharaoh because no one would let him stop now stops, and lets his servants and his animals stop, because his God broke the hand that held the whip. The law reads differently when the reason is a memory instead of a doctrine, and Moses clearly meant his listeners, most of whom had been born free in the wilderness, to feel the weight of a slavery they had inherited as a story rather than a scar.

Moses retold the terror of that day too: the mountain burning, the voice, the people's plea that he go near and listen for them, because this great fire will consume us and we will die if we hear the voice of the LORD our God any longer. And God's answer to the plea, which Moses had never had reason to repeat until now, is one of the most human lines God speaks in the five books.

"If only they had such a heart to fear Me and keep all My commandments always, so that it might be well with them and with their children forever."

Then chapter 6, the sermon's core, the passage Israel would carry on its doorposts and its lips into every generation after. It opens with the confession that would outlive the nation, the temple, and the language it was written in.

"Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is One."

"And you shall love the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your strength."

"These words I am commanding you today are to be upon your hearts."

"And you shall teach them diligently to your children and speak of them when you sit at home and when you walk along the road, when you lie down and when you get up."

Bind them as a sign on your hand, Moses said, let them be as frontlets between your eyes, write them on the doorposts of your house and on your gates. The commandments were not given as scrolls to be archived. They were given as talk at the table, talk on the road, the last words at night and the first in the morning, words on the doors a child passes through twenty times a day. The second generation listening had been raised in tents by parents under a death sentence, and Moses was telling them how the faith survives a generation: not by monument, but by repetition, until a child who never saw the sea split can say the words as his own.

And immediately, the warning that runs under this whole section like groundwater. When the LORD brings you into the land, a land with great and splendid cities you did not build, houses full of good things you did not fill them with, wells you did not dig, vineyards and olive groves you did not plant, and when you eat and are satisfied, then:

"Be careful not to forget the LORD who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery."

"Fear the LORD your God, serve Him only, and take your oaths in His name."

Do not follow other gods, the gods of the peoples around you. Do not test the LORD your God as you tested Him at Massah. Tell your son, when he asks in time to come what these testimonies mean, the whole story in one breath, the way Moses framed it for them: we were Pharaoh's slaves in Egypt, and the LORD brought us out with a mighty hand, and He gave us this land, and He commanded these statutes for our good, to preserve us alive. Obedience, in Moses' telling, is not a tax. It is the shape of the rescue, continued.

Chapter 7 told them why they, of all peoples, were standing there. It was not their size.

"The LORD did not set His affection on you and choose you because you were more numerous than the other peoples, for you were the fewest of all peoples."

"But because the LORD loved you and kept the oath He swore to your fathers, He brought you out with a mighty hand and redeemed you from the house of slavery, from the hand of Pharaoh king of Egypt."

"Know therefore that the LORD your God is God, the faithful God who keeps His covenant of loving devotion for a thousand generations of those who love Him and keep His commandments."

They were to break down the altars of the land's peoples, smash their sacred stones, burn their idols, make no covenant and show no pity, because the snare was real: intermarriage would turn their children after other gods, and the anger of the LORD would destroy them quickly. You are a people holy to the LORD your God, Moses said, His prized possession out of all peoples on the face of the earth. And he promised the other side of the arrangement in plain terms: God would keep the loving devotion, bless the fruit of womb and ground, remove sickness, and drive the nations out little by little, so the wild animals would not multiply against Israel in the empty land. Even the conquest, in the sermon, runs on God's timing rather than Israel's strength.

Chapter 8 reached back to the forty years and told the children what the wilderness had been for. It had been a test, and the test had a grader's purpose.

"Remember that these forty years the LORD your God led you all the way in the wilderness, so that He might humble you and test you in order to know what was in your heart, whether or not you would keep His commandments."

"He humbled you, and in your hunger He gave you manna to eat, which neither you nor your fathers had known, so that you might understand that man does not live on bread alone, but on every word that comes from the mouth of the LORD."

"Your clothing did not wear out and your feet did not swell during these forty years."

As a man disciplines his son, Moses said, the LORD your God disciplined you. And the land ahead of them was described in the sermon's warmest passage: a good land, brooks of water, fountains and springs flowing in valleys and hills, wheat and barley, vines, fig trees, pomegranates, olive oil and honey, a land where bread would not be scarce and iron could be dug from the hills and copper from the mountains. Eat, be satisfied, bless the LORD. And there the warning returns, sharper for being set in plenty: when your herds multiply and your silver and gold increase and your heart is lifted up, you will forget. There is a sentence people start saying in a full house that no one says in a tent.

"You might say in your heart" the warning runs, and the sentence it pictures is this: "The power and strength of my hands have made this wealth for me." Wealth, in the saying, has a single author.

"But remember that it is the LORD your God who gives you the power to gain wealth, in order to confirm His covenant that He swore to your fathers even to this day."

If you ever forget, Moses said, and walk after other gods, I testify against you today that you will surely perish, like the nations the LORD destroys before you. The sermon has no version of events where the gifts survive the forgetting of the Giver.

Then Moses made the point the whole generation most needed to hear, in the sermon and probably in the book, and he made it three times so it could not be missed. When the nations fell before them, they were not to say the sentence already forming in human hearts.

"When the LORD your God has driven them out before you, do not say in your heart," the warning runs, and the sentence it forbids is this: "Because of my righteousness the LORD has brought me in to possess this land." Then Moses answers it himself: "Rather, the LORD is driving out these nations before you because of their wickedness."

"It is not because of your righteousness or uprightness of heart that you are going in to possess their land."

"Understand, then, that it is not because of your righteousness that the LORD your God is giving you this good land to possess, for you are a stiff-necked people."

And to prove the charge, Moses walked them through their parents' record, and his own, at the mountain. Remember, do not forget, how you provoked the LORD in the wilderness. From the day you left Egypt until you arrived here, you have been rebellious. At Horeb itself, while Moses was on the mountain forty days and forty nights without bread or water, receiving the tablets of the covenant, the people below had already built the calf. Their own history's holiest month is the indictment.

"When I went up on the mountain to receive the tablets of stone, the tablets of the covenant that the LORD made with you, I stayed on the mountain forty days and forty nights. I ate no bread and drank no water."

"And at the end of forty days and forty nights, the LORD gave me the two stone tablets, the tablets of the covenant."

"And I saw how you had sinned against the LORD your God; you had made for yourselves a molten calf. You had turned aside quickly from the way that the LORD had commanded you."

He took the two tablets in his two hands, Moses said, and threw them down and broke them before their eyes, the way Exodus records. Then, what Exodus records only in outline, Moses laid out in detail: he fell down before the LORD for forty days and forty nights a second time, eating no bread and drinking no water, because of the sin they had committed, while the LORD said He would destroy them and blot out their name. He feared the anger and the wrath, he said plainly. And he prayed for Aaron too, whom the LORD was angry enough to destroy. He took the calf, burned it, ground it to powder as fine as dust, and threw the dust into the brook that ran down the mountain. Taberah, Massah, Kibroth-hattaavah, Kadesh Barnea: the list of provocations runs through the retelling like place names on a road map of rebellion, ending at Kadesh, where the parents had refused the land and sent Moses back with the report that cost forty years.

His prayer from those forty days is preserved, and it is the same shape as his prayer at the sea of reeds in reverse: not spare me, but spare them.

"O Lord GOD, do not destroy Your people, Your inheritance, whom You redeemed through Your greatness and brought out of Egypt with a mighty hand."

"Remember Your servants Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Overlook the stubbornness of this people and the wickedness of their sin."

The argument he made to God then is the argument he was now making to Israel: they are Your people. What the nations would say if Egypt's God destroyed His own rescued people in the wilderness runs through the plea to the end.

"But they are Your people, Your inheritance, whom You brought out by Your great power and outstretched arm."

Chapter 10 answers the broken tablets. At that time, God told Moses to chisel two stone tablets like the first, come up the mountain, and make an ark of wood.

"Chisel out two stone tablets like the originals, come up to Me on the mountain, and make an ark of wood."

"And I will write on the tablets the words that were on the first tablets, which you broke; and you are to place them in the ark."

Moses made the ark of acacia wood, chiseled the tablets, went up with them in his hand, and God wrote on them the words of the first tablets, the ten commandments proclaimed at the assembly. He came down and placed them in the ark.

"And I went back down the mountain and placed the tablets in the ark I had made, as the LORD had commanded me; and there they have remained."

The broken covenant ends up carried in a box, whole again, at the center of the camp. Aaron died on the road after that, at Moserah, and Eleazar his son took his place, and the tribe of Levi was set apart to carry the ark and stand before the LORD and bless in His name, receiving no land inheritance because the LORD is their inheritance. It is the order Israel had lived under for forty years, retold in a paragraph as Moses prepares them to live under it in a land of farms instead of a moving camp.

Then the sermon's summary, the whole second address folded into one question and answer.

"And now, O Israel, what does the LORD your God ask of you but to fear the LORD your God by walking in all His ways, to love Him, to serve the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul,"

"And to keep the commandments and statutes of the LORD that I am giving you this day for your own good?"

Behold, to the LORD your God belong heaven and the highest heaven, the earth and all that is in it. Yet He set His affection on your fathers, loved them, and chose their descendants. So the command that follows is aimed at the organ every failure in the story has come from.

"Circumcise your hearts, therefore, and stiffen your necks no more."

"For the LORD your God is God of gods and Lord of lords, the great, mighty, and awesome God, showing no partiality and accepting no bribe."

"He executes justice for the fatherless and widow, and He loves the foreigner, giving him food and clothing."

Love the foreigner, Moses added, because you were foreigners in Egypt. Swear by His name. He is your praise and your God, who did the great and awesome wonders your eyes have seen.

"Your fathers went down to Egypt, seventy in all, and now the LORD your God has made you as numerous as the stars in the sky."

Chapter 11 binds the section shut with the choice in its first form. Love the LORD and keep His charge always, so you will have the strength to go in and possess the land. The land ahead is not Egypt: it does not drink from a river at your foot, it drinks rain from heaven, and a land the LORD your God cares for, His eyes always on it, from the beginning of the year to the end. If you carefully obey, He will give the rains in season, the autumn and the spring rains, and you will gather grain, new wine, and oil, and eat and be satisfied. Take heed that your hearts are not deceived and you turn aside to other gods, because then the rains stop and the good land gives up its people quickly.

The words are to be fixed and taught the same way chapter 6 commanded, and then the geography of the rest of the book is set down in a single sentence.

"See, today I am setting before you a blessing and a curse"

"A blessing if you obey the commandments of the LORD your God that I am giving you today,"

"But a curse if you disobey the commandments of the LORD your God and turn aside from the path I command you today by following other gods, which you have not known."

When the LORD brings you into the land, Moses said, the blessing is to be proclaimed on Mount Gerizim and the curse on Mount Ebal, two mountains across from each other beyond the Jordan, waiting already in the landscape for the ceremony of chapter 27. The choice will one day be read aloud off two hillsides with the whole nation answering. Here, at the end of his second address, Moses only names it.

The law itself, the statutes for courts and fields and tables, was next. But the order matters, and Moses kept it: first the mountain, first the one God and the wholehearted love, first the warning about full houses. Rules for a settled life would land differently on people who had been told, three ways, that the life was a gift.
