2 Kings Episode 3: Oil, Bread, and a Son Raised
2 Kings 4, Berean Standard Bible

Between the wars, the book follows Elisha into kitchens and sickrooms, and the first is a widow's. Her husband, one of the sons of the prophets, was dead, and his creditor was coming to take her two sons as slaves for the debt. She cried out to Elisha: “Your servant, my husband, is dead, and you know that your servant feared the LORD.” All she had left in the house, she told him, was one thing: “Your servant has nothing in the house but a jar of oil.” Elisha's answer built the miracle out of her neighbors: “borrow empty jars from all your neighbors. Do not gather just a few.” Then she was to shut the door behind her and her sons and pour. The oil flowed jar to jar until the last borrowed vessel was full. Her son said, “There are no more jars.” Then the oil stopped, not before. Elisha's instruction closed the scene like a ledger balanced: “Go, sell the oil, and pay your debt. Then you and your sons can live on the remainder.” A debt cancelled, two boys free, and a household living on what a single jar became behind a shut door.

The second household is in Shunem, where a prominent woman persuaded the passing prophet to stop for meals, then persuaded her husband to build him a small upstairs room, a bed, a table, a chair, a lamp. Elisha wanted to repay her and offered the only currency a prophet at court might have, a word spoken for her to the king or the commander of the army. Her answer is one of the quietest lines in the book: “I have a home among my own people,” she replied. She asked for nothing. It was Gehazi, the servant, who named the absence in her life: she had no son, and her husband was old. Elisha called her to the doorway and made the promise she had not dared to request: “At this time next year, you will hold a son in your arms.” Her response was not joy but fear of hope itself: “Do not lie to your maidservant, O man of God.” She conceived, and the son was born at the time appointed, just as Elisha had told her.

The child grew, and one day in the harvest field he cried out to his father, “My head! My head!” and was carried to his mother, and sat on her lap until noon, and died. What the mother did next is the spine of the story. She laid the boy on the prophet's bed, shut the door, saddled a donkey, and refused every question, including her husband's, with one sentence: “Everything is all right.” She rode to Mount Carmel and would not answer Gehazi's questions either, the same sentence again, until she reached Elisha and clung to his feet. When Gehazi moved to push her away, Elisha stopped him: “Leave her alone, for her soul is in deep distress, and the LORD has hidden it from me and has not told me.” Her words then were an accusation aimed at the gift itself: “Did I ask you for a son, my lord?” She reminded him she had begged, “Do not deceive me?” Elisha sent Gehazi ahead with his staff to lay on the boy's face, an errand of haste, but the mother would not be separated from the prophet himself, and she used Elisha's own oath, the one he had sworn at the Jordan: “As surely as the LORD lives and as you yourself live, I will not leave you.” So he got up and followed her. The staff accomplished nothing. Gehazi met them with the report: “The boy has not awakened.”

In the upper room, behind a closed door, Elisha prayed, then stretched himself over the child, mouth to mouth, eye to eye, hand to hand, until the boy's body grew warm. He paced the room, returned, stretched himself out again, and the boy sneezed seven times and opened his eyes. Elisha's whole commission to the mother was three words: “Pick up your son.” She fell at his feet, bowed to the ground, picked up her son, and went out.

The episode closes with two smaller works at the table. At Gilgal, in a famine, one of the sons of the prophets gathered wild gourds into the communal stew without knowing what they were, and the first taste brought the cry: “There is death in the pot, O man of God!” Elisha threw in flour, and there was nothing harmful in the pot. Then a man arrived from Baal-shalishah with twenty loaves of barley bread, the first ripe grain, and Elisha ordered it set before a hundred men. His servant protested the arithmetic, and Elisha answered with the word that governed the meal: “They will eat and have some left over.” They ate, and they had some left over, according to the word of the LORD. Oil that fills a street of jars, a son restored behind a shut door, poison drawn out of a pot, bread that outlasts the count: the book is building its portrait of the heir of Elijah one household at a time.
