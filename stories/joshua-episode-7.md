Joshua Episode 7: The Altar by the Jordan
Joshua 22, Berean Standard Bible

The division was finished, and one promise from the plains of Moab was still outstanding. Joshua summoned the Reubenites, the Gadites, and the half-tribe of Manasseh, the tribes whose men had crossed the Jordan armed and fought for land that was never going to be theirs, and he released them with an audit that reads like a decoration: "You have done all that Moses the servant of the LORD commanded you, and you have obeyed my voice in all that I commanded you. All this time you have not deserted your brothers, up to this very day, but have kept the charge given you by the LORD your God."

Their brothers had rest now, as promised, and the eastern men could go home. But Joshua sent them with the charge that echoes Deuteronomy's summary of the whole law: "But be very careful to observe the commandment and the law that Moses the servant of the LORD gave you: to love the LORD your God, to walk in all His ways, to keep His commandments, to hold fast to Him, and to serve Him with all your heart and with all your soul." He blessed them, and he loaded them: "Return to your homes with your great wealth, with immense herds of livestock, with silver, gold, bronze, iron, and very many clothes. Divide with your brothers the spoil of your enemies." The men who had left their families east of the river years earlier went home rich, to wives and children and herds that had grown up without them, in exchange for a war fought for other tribes' inheritances.

At Geliloth, near the Jordan, still in the land of Canaan on the crossing side, they stopped, and there the three tribes "built an imposing altar there by the Jordan." Not a small cairn. An imposing altar, big enough to be seen, big enough to be reported.

The report that reached Shiloh was accurate about the stones and assumed everything about the purpose: the eastern tribes "have built an altar on the border of the land of Canaan, at Geliloth near the Jordan on the Israelite side." The response was immediate and total: "the whole congregation of Israel assembled at Shiloh to go to war against them." One altar, seen from a distance, had Israel ready to fight its first civil war before the conquest generation had even settled.

But Israel did one thing right before it marched. It sent an embassy first: Phinehas son of Eleazar the priest, Aaron's grandson, with ten chiefs, one family head from each tribe. Phinehas had a history the book of Numbers recorded: at Peor he had stopped a plague with a spear. He was not a man sent to negotiate a compromise. He was sent to deliver an accusation, and the accusation was thorough: "What is this breach of faith you have committed today against the God of Israel by turning away from the LORD and building for yourselves an altar, that you might rebel against the LORD this day? Was not the sin of Peor enough for us, from which we have not cleansed ourselves to this day? It even brought a plague upon the congregation of the LORD."

Then the embassy did something remarkable inside its own indictment. It offered the accused an escape that would cost the accusers land: "If indeed the land of your inheritance is unclean, then cross over to the land of the LORD's possession, where the LORD's tabernacle stands, and take possession of it among us. But do not rebel against the LORD or against us by building for yourselves an altar other than the altar of the LORD our God." If your side of the river is the problem, they said, we will share our side. And they closed with the valley of Achor: "Was not Achan son of Zerah unfaithful regarding what was set apart for destruction, bringing wrath upon the whole congregation of Israel? Yet it was not only Achan who perished because of his sin!"

The eastern tribes answered with the strongest oath formula in the book, doubled: "The LORD, the Mighty One, is God! The LORD, the Mighty One, is God! He knows, and may Israel also know. If this was in rebellion or breach of faith against the LORD, do not spare us today." If they had built the altar to offer sacrifices on it, they said, "may the LORD Himself hold us accountable."

Then they explained, and the explanation reversed the whole picture. The altar was not for offerings at all. It was built out of fear, and the fear was aimed a generation ahead: "we have done this for fear that in the future your descendants might say to ours," and here the feared sentence stands as they quoted it: "What have you to do with the LORD, the God of Israel? For the LORD has made the Jordan a border between us and you Reubenites and Gadites. You have no share in the LORD!" The river that Israel had crossed on dry ground could become, in their grandchildren's mouth, a border of belonging. So they had built a replica where both sides would see it: "let it be a witness between us and you and the generations to come, that we will worship the LORD in His presence with our burnt offerings, sacrifices, and peace offerings", offered at God's own altar across the river, not on this one. And if the feared day ever came, their descendants could point at the stones and answer: "Look at the replica of the altar of the LORD that our fathers made, not for burnt offerings or sacrifices, but as a witness between us and you." They ended where the embassy had begun, with loyalty: "Far be it from us to rebel against the LORD and turn away from Him today by building an altar for burnt offerings, grain offerings, or sacrifices, other than the altar of the LORD our God, which stands before His tabernacle."

Phinehas heard it, and the priest who had once executed judgment at Peor now pronounced the all-clear: "Today we know that the LORD is among us, because you have not committed this breach of faith against Him. Consequently, you have delivered the Israelites from the hand of the LORD." The embassy rode home, the congregation heard the report and was satisfied, "they blessed God and spoke no more about going to war against them". The altar got its name from its builders: Witness. "It is a witness between us that the LORD is God."

One more pile of stones stood in Israel, then, and this one was a sermon rather than a memorial: a river is a boundary on a map, but it is not a border in the faith. The united nation had nearly gone to war over a misunderstanding, and what saved it was an embassy willing to accuse to the face, an offer willing to share land, and an explanation willing to be heard. It was the last act of the whole congregation together in the book, and it ended with a blessing instead of a battle.
