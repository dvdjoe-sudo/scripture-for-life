Leviticus Episode 4: A Holy People in Daily Life
Leviticus 17 to 22, Berean Standard Bible

Once a year, on the tenth day of the seventh month, Aaron went behind the veil and the whole camp was cleansed at the center. These six chapters are about the other days.

The cleansing moves outward now, from the tent to the slaughtering place, the table, the marriage bed, the field, the courtroom, and the market scales. It starts with meat and blood.

From this point, an Israelite who slaughtered an ox, a lamb, or a goat, in the camp or outside it, was to bring it to the entrance to the Tent of Meeting and present it as an offering to the LORD before His tabernacle. The sacrifices Israelites had been offering out in the open fields were to come in to the priest at the entrance instead, to be offered as peace offerings, the blood splattered on the altar and the fat burned as a pleasing aroma. A man who slaughtered his animal somewhere else and did not bring it to the entrance incurred bloodguilt. He had shed blood, and he was to be cut off from among his people.

"They must no longer offer their sacrifices to the goat demons to which they have prostituted themselves. This will be a permanent statute for them for the generations to come."

Blood itself was barred as food, for Israelite and foreigner alike, and chapter 17 finally says why blood carries the weight it carries in this book.

"For the life of the flesh is in the blood, and I have given it to you to make atonement for your souls upon the altar; for it is the blood that makes atonement for the soul."

Blood belonged on the altar because life was in it, and God had given it for atonement. So no one was to eat it. A hunter who killed a wild animal or a bird that could be eaten was to drain its blood and cover it with dirt. A person who ate an animal found dead or torn by wild beasts, native or foreigner, washed his clothes, bathed with water, and was unclean until evening. Then he was clean.

Chapter 18 turns to the household and the bed, and it opens by naming the two cultures Israel was standing between.

"You must not follow the practices of the land of Egypt, where you used to live, and you must not follow the practices of the land of Canaan, into which I am bringing you. You must not walk in their customs."

What follows is a list of forbidden unions. No one was to approach a close relative. The chapter names them relation by relation: a mother, a father's wife, a sister, a granddaughter, an aunt, a daughter-in-law, a brother's wife, a woman and her daughter, a wife's sister taken as a rival while the wife lived. A man was not to lie with his neighbor's wife. A man was not to lie with a man as with a woman. No one was to lie with an animal. Woven into the same list, without a change of tone, stands a command about children.

"You must not give any of your children to be sacrificed to Molech, for you must not profane the name of your God. I am the LORD."

The chapter closes with a warning that gives the land itself a stomach. The nations being driven out before Israel had defiled themselves by all these practices, the land had become defiled, and God was punishing it for its sin.

"Even the land has become defiled, so I am punishing it for its sin, and the land will vomit out its inhabitants."

Anyone in Israel who committed any of these abominations was to be cut off from among his people. The list was not given as private advice. It was the terms on which a people could stay in the land they were about to be given.

Chapter 19 is the center of this section, and in some ways the center of the whole book. It is a single chapter that jumps from altar to field to courtroom to market in a few lines at a time, and nearly every jump lands on the same four words. The line "I am the LORD" stands fifteen times in this one chapter.

It opens with the command every other command hangs on.

"Speak to the whole congregation of Israel and tell them: Be holy because I, the LORD your God, am holy."

Then the laws come in quick order, and they are worth hearing at the pace the chapter sets.

Each of you must respect his mother and father, and you must keep My Sabbaths. Do not turn to idols or make molten gods for yourselves. When you reap the harvest of your land, do not reap to the very edges of your field, and do not gather the gleanings of your harvest. Do not strip your vineyard bare or gather its fallen grapes.

"Leave them for the poor and the foreigner. I am the LORD your God."

You must not steal. You must not lie or deceive one another. You must not swear falsely by My name. You must not defraud your neighbor or rob him.

"You must not withhold until morning the wages due a hired hand."

You must not curse the deaf or place a stumbling block before the blind, but you shall fear your God. You must not pervert justice. You must not show partiality to the poor or favoritism to the rich. You are to judge your neighbor fairly. You must not go about spreading slander among your people. You must not endanger the life of your neighbor. You must not harbor hatred against your brother in your heart. Rebuke your neighbor directly, so you do not incur guilt on account of him.

"Do not seek revenge or bear a grudge against any of your people, but love your neighbor as yourself. I am the LORD."

The chapter keeps going, into things a reader might not group with love of neighbor at all. Livestock were not to be crossbred, fields not sown with two kinds of seed, clothing not worn made of two kinds of material. A slave woman designated for a man, not yet ransomed or freed, who lay with another man, brought punishment and a guilt offering, not death, because she had not been freed. A newly planted fruit tree's fruit was forbidden for three years, set apart as praise in the fourth, and eaten in the fifth. No eating anything with blood still in it. No divination or sorcery. No rounding off the hair at the sides of the head or clipping the edges of the beard. No cuts in the body for the dead, and no tattoo marks. A man must not make his daughter a prostitute, or the land would be filled with depravity. Sabbaths were to be kept and the sanctuary revered. Mediums and spiritists were not to be consulted.

"You are to rise in the presence of the elderly, honor the aged, and fear your God. I am the LORD."

Then the chapter turns to the outsider living inside Israel's borders, and gives him the same measure it gave the neighbor two dozen lines earlier.

"When a foreigner resides with you in your land, you must not oppress him. You must treat the foreigner living among you as native-born and love him as yourself, for you were foreigners in the land of Egypt. I am the LORD your God."

It ends at the market stall. No dishonest measures of length, weight, or volume. Honest scales, honest weights, an honest ephah, an honest hin. And the reason attached to a set of scales is the same reason attached to the whole law: I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt. The God of the mercy seat also stood behind the grain measure. A short measure in the market was not a small thing smuggled past a distant God. It was a lie told in front of the God who had carried these people out of slavery and now watched how they weighed each other's bread.

Chapter 20 goes back over the ground of the last three chapters and attaches penalties, and most of them are severe. An Israelite or a foreigner who gave any of his children to Molech was to be put to death, stoned by the people of the land, and God said He would set His face against that man and cut him off, and against his family too if the people hid their eyes from what he had done. Anyone who turned to mediums and spiritists was to be cut off. A man or woman who was a medium or a spiritist was to be stoned. Anyone who cursed his father or his mother was to be put to death. Adultery with a neighbor's wife meant death for both. The forbidden unions of chapter 18 carry death, or being cut off, or dying childless, stated case by case. A man who lay with a man as with a woman, and anyone who lay with an animal, were to be put to death.

The chapter is told plainly here because the text tells it plainly. This is the law as Israel received it, and the reason given is the same one chapter 18 gave. Keep all My statutes and ordinances.

"So that the land where I am bringing you to live will not vomit you out."

"You are to be holy to Me because I, the LORD, am holy, and I have set you apart from the nations to be My own."

The last two chapters narrow the focus from the whole people to the men at the altar, and the standard rises as it narrows.

An ordinary priest was not to defile himself for a dead person among his people, except for his closest relatives: his mother, his father, his son, his daughter, his brother, and his virgin sister who had no husband. Priests were not to make bald spots on their heads, shave off the edges of their beards, or make cuts in their bodies, the mourning marks the people around them made. They were to be holy to their God and not profane His name, because they presented the food offerings, the food of their God. A priest could not marry a woman defiled by prostitution or a divorced woman. If a priest's daughter profaned herself by prostitution, she profaned her father, and she was to be burned with fire.

The high priest stood under a harder version of the same law, and a reader who has come through Episode 2 has already watched it land on one man. The priest highest among his brothers, who had the anointing oil poured on his head and was ordained to wear the priestly garments, was not to let his hair hang loose or tear his garments.

"He must not go near any dead body; he must not defile himself, even for his father or mother."

He was not to leave the sanctuary of his God, and the woman he married had to be a virgin from his own people, not a widow, not divorced, not defiled. That is the written rule behind Aaron's silence at the bodies of Nadab and Abihu. What looked like one father's frozen grief in a single terrible hour was also the law of his office, and every high priest after him would live under it.

For the generations to come, no descendant of Aaron who had a physical defect was to approach to offer the food of his God. The text lists them: a man who was blind or lame, who had a disfigured face or a limb too long, a broken foot or a broken hand, a hunchback or a dwarf, an eye defect, festering sores, a scab, or crushed testicles. He had a defect, and he was not to come near to offer. He was not cast out of the priesthood either. He could eat the most holy food of his God as well as the holy food. He ate at the priests' table. He did not serve at the altar.

Chapter 22 guards the offerings from the other side. Aaron and his sons were to treat with respect the sacred offerings the Israelites consecrated, so they would not profane God's holy name. A priest who was unclean, by a skin disease or a discharge or contact with the dead or any of the other uncleannesses the book has listed, was not to eat of the holy offerings until he was clean, washed, and evening had come. No outsider, no guest, no hired hand was to eat a holy offering. A slave bought with the priest's money could eat it, and a priest's daughter married into another household could not, unless she came back widowed or divorced and without children, and then she could eat her father's food again. Stolen holiness was treated seriously too: a person who ate a holy offering by mistake had to repay it to the priest and add a fifth, the same fifth the guilt offering had demanded in Episode 1.

And what the people brought had to match the men who offered it. Any gift for a burnt offering, a vow, or a freewill offering had to be an unblemished male from the cattle, the sheep, or the goats in order to be accepted.

"You must not present anything with a defect, because it will not be accepted on your behalf."

No animal that was blind, injured, or maimed was to go on the altar, nothing with a running sore, a festering rash, or a scab. A bull or lamb with one limb too long or too short could be given as a freewill offering, but not for a vow. A newborn ox, sheep, or goat was to stay with its mother for seven days. From the eighth day on it would be acceptable as a food offering. No animal and its young were to be slaughtered on the same day. A thank offering was to be eaten the day it was sacrificed, none of it left until morning.

The section closes with God's name over the whole of daily life one more time, and with the journey underneath it. Israel was not to profane His holy name. He was to be acknowledged as holy among the Israelites.

"I am the LORD who sanctifies you, who brought you out of the land of Egypt to be your God. I am the LORD."

So by the end of chapter 22 there is no corner of an Israelite week the law has not entered. Blood is not eaten. Wages are not held overnight. Field edges are left standing for people the farmer may never meet. The foreigner is loved as a native, on the stated grounds that Israel knows from the inside what it is to be one. The high priest cannot walk to his own father's grave. The animal on the altar can have nothing wrong with it.

What the law has not yet ordered is time itself. Days, weeks, years, and generations are still unmarked on Israel's calendar, and the land waiting across the Jordan has not yet been told when it will rest, or when everything sold in hard years goes back to the families who lost it.
