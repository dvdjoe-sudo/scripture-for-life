Exodus Episode 1: Slavery and the Burning Bush
Exodus 1 to 4, Berean Standard Bible

Genesis ended with a coffin in Egypt. Exodus begins with the family that coffin left behind.

The sons of Israel who came to Egypt with Jacob are named again: Reuben, Simeon, Levi, Judah, Issachar, Zebulun, Benjamin, Dan, Naphtali, Gad, and Asher. Joseph was already in Egypt. The whole family line numbered seventy persons, and then that generation died. Joseph died. His brothers died. Their generation passed, but their descendants did not shrink into the land that had taken them in.

The Israelites were fruitful in Egypt. They increased rapidly, multiplied, and became exceedingly numerous, so that the land was filled with them.

Then the throne changed hands. "Then a new king, who did not know Joseph, came to power in Egypt." The new Pharaoh did not see a family brought there by famine and kept alive by Joseph's wisdom. He saw a people growing inside his borders. He told his people that the Israelites had become much too numerous for them. If war broke out, Pharaoh feared Israel might join Egypt's enemies, fight against Egypt, and leave the land.

Egypt answered growth with force. The Egyptians put slave masters over Israel to oppress them with forced labor. Israel built Pithom and Rameses as store cities for Pharaoh. But the more the Egyptians oppressed them, the more Israel multiplied and spread, until the Egyptians came to dread the Israelites. They made Israel work ruthlessly. They made their lives bitter with hard labor in brick and mortar and with all kinds of work in the fields. Every service they imposed was harsh.

When labor did not stop the growth, Pharaoh reached for the newborn sons.

He spoke to the Hebrew midwives, Shiphrah and Puah. When they helped the Hebrew women give birth, they were to watch on the delivery stool. If the child was a son, they were to kill him. If the child was a daughter, she could live.

"The midwives, however, feared God and did not do as the king of Egypt had instructed; they let the boys live."

Pharaoh called the midwives in and demanded to know why they had let the boys live. The midwives answered that the Hebrew women were not like the Egyptian women. They were vigorous and gave birth before a midwife arrived. God dealt well with the midwives because they feared Him, and the people multiplied and became very strong. God gave the midwives families of their own.

Pharaoh widened the order. He commanded all his people: "Every son born to the Hebrews you must throw into the Nile, but every daughter you may allow to live."

In that Egypt, a Levite man married a Levite woman. She conceived and gave birth to a son. When she saw that he was a beautiful child, she hid him for three months. She could hide him no longer, so she took a papyrus basket, coated it with tar and pitch, placed the child in it, and put it among the reeds along the bank of the Nile. The child's sister stood at a distance to learn what would happen to him.

Pharaoh's daughter came down to bathe at the Nile while her attendants walked beside the river. She saw the basket among the reeds and sent her slave girl to get it. She opened it and saw the baby. He was crying. She had pity on him and said this was one of the Hebrew children.

Then the baby's sister came forward. She asked Pharaoh's daughter whether she should go and call a Hebrew woman to nurse the baby for her. Pharaoh's daughter told her to go. The girl went and called the child's mother. Pharaoh's daughter told the mother to take the child and nurse him for her, and she would pay her wages. The woman took the child and nursed him.

The hidden baby was now being nursed by his own mother, and Pharaoh's daughter was paying her wages.

When the child grew older, his mother brought him to Pharaoh's daughter, and he became her son. She named him Moses and explained, "I drew him out of the water."

Moses grew up as the son of Pharaoh's daughter, but Exodus first shows him as a grown man when he goes out to his own people and looks on their burdens. He saw an Egyptian beating a Hebrew, one of his own people. Moses looked this way and that way. Seeing no one, he struck the Egyptian down and hid him in the sand.

The next day Moses went out again and saw two Hebrews fighting. He spoke to the one who was in the wrong and asked why he was striking his companion. The man answered by naming Moses' secret. He asked who had made Moses ruler and judge over them. He asked if Moses planned to kill him as he had killed the Egyptian. Moses was afraid. The thing he had done had become known.

When Pharaoh heard about it, he tried to kill Moses. Moses fled from Pharaoh and stayed in the land of Midian.

A runaway from Pharaoh's house sat down by a well in Midian. The priest of Midian had seven daughters, and they came to draw water and fill the troughs to water their father's flock. Shepherds came and drove them away, but Moses stood up and rescued them. Then he watered their flock himself.

The daughters returned to their father, Reuel, earlier than expected. He asked how they had come home so soon that day. They told him an Egyptian had rescued them from the shepherds and had even drawn water for them and watered the flock. Reuel asked where the man was and why they had left him outside. He told his daughters to call him so he could eat bread.

Moses agreed to stay with the man. Reuel gave Moses his daughter Zipporah in marriage. She gave birth to a son, and Moses named him Gershom, saying he had been a foreigner in a strange land.

During that long period, the king of Egypt died. The Israelites groaned because of their slavery and cried out. Their cry for help rose up to God from their slavery.

"So God heard their groaning, and He remembered His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. God saw the Israelites and took notice."

Moses was still in Midian, tending the flock of his father-in-law, Jethro, the priest of Midian. He led the flock to the far side of the wilderness and came to Horeb, the mountain of God. There the angel of the LORD appeared to Moses in a blazing fire from within a bush. "Moses saw the bush ablaze with fire, but it was not consumed."

Moses said he would go over and see this strange sight, why the bush did not burn up. When the LORD saw that Moses had gone over to look, God called to him from within the bush and said his name twice. Moses answered that he was there.

"Do not come any closer," God said. "Take off your sandals, for the place where you are standing is holy ground."

Then God named Himself as the God of Moses' father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob. Moses hid his face, because he was afraid to look at God.

The LORD said, "I have indeed seen the affliction of My people in Egypt. I have heard them crying out because of their oppressors, and I am aware of their sufferings. I have come down to rescue them from the hand of the Egyptians and to bring them up out of that land to a good and spacious land, a land flowing with milk and honey."

The mission reached Moses in the same breath. God would send him to Pharaoh so Moses could bring the Israelites out of Egypt.

Moses answered with the size of his own name. Who was he that he should go to Pharaoh and bring the Israelites out of Egypt? God answered, "I will surely be with you." The sign would come after obedience had already started: when Moses had brought the people out of Egypt, they would worship God on that same mountain.

Moses asked another question. If he went to the Israelites and told them the God of their fathers had sent him, they might ask God's name. What should he tell them?

God said to Moses, "I AM WHO I AM. This is what you are to say to the Israelites: 'I AM has sent me to you.'"

God also told Moses to say that the LORD, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, had sent him. This was God's name forever, the name by which He was to be remembered in every generation.

Moses was to assemble the elders of Israel and tell them the LORD had appeared to him. God had seen what had been done to Israel in Egypt. He promised to bring them up out of their misery into the land of the Canaanites, Hittites, Amorites, Perizzites, Hivites, and Jebusites, a land flowing with milk and honey. The elders would listen. Then Moses and the elders were to go to the king of Egypt and ask to make a three-day journey into the wilderness to offer sacrifices to the LORD their God.

God also told Moses that the king of Egypt would not let Israel go unless compelled by a mighty hand. God would stretch out His hand and strike Egypt with all His wonders. After that, Pharaoh would let them go. Israel would not leave empty-handed. God would make the Egyptians favorably disposed toward the Israelites, and the Hebrew women would ask their Egyptian neighbors for articles of silver and gold and for clothing. In that way they would plunder the Egyptians.

Moses still had objections.

He answered that the Israelites might not believe him or listen to his voice. They might say the LORD had not appeared to him. The LORD asked what was in Moses' hand. Moses said it was a staff. God told him to throw it on the ground. Moses threw it down, and it became a snake. He ran from it. God told him to reach out his hand and take it by the tail. Moses did, and the snake became a staff again in his hand. God gave the sign so Israel would believe that the LORD, the God of their fathers, had appeared to Moses.

God gave a second sign. Moses put his hand into his cloak and took it out leprous, like snow. He put it back into his cloak and took it out restored, like the rest of his flesh. If Israel did not believe the first sign, they might believe the second. If they still did not believe, Moses was to take some water from the Nile, pour it on the dry ground, and the water would become blood.

Moses answered again. He had never been eloquent, not in the past and not since God had spoken to him. He was slow of speech and tongue. The LORD answered that He had made man's mouth. He made the mute, the deaf, the seeing, and the blind. He told Moses to go, and He would help Moses speak and teach him what to say.

Moses asked God to send someone else.

The anger of the LORD burned against Moses. God named Aaron, Moses' brother, a Levite who spoke well and was already on his way to meet Moses. Aaron would be glad when he saw him. Moses was to speak to Aaron and put the words in his mouth. God would help both of them speak and teach them what to do. Aaron would speak to the people for Moses. Aaron would be Moses' spokesman, and Moses would be like God to Aaron. Moses was also to take the staff in his hand, the staff with which he would perform the signs.

Moses went back to Jethro, his father-in-law, and asked to return to his own people in Egypt to see if they were still alive. Jethro told him to go in peace.

The LORD told Moses in Midian to return to Egypt, for all the men who had wanted to kill him were dead. Moses took his wife and sons, mounted them on a donkey, and started back to Egypt. Moses took the staff of God in his hand.

On the road back, the LORD gave Moses the warning he was to carry into Pharaoh's court. Moses was to perform before Pharaoh all the wonders God had given him the power to do. But God would harden Pharaoh's heart so he would not let the people go. Moses was to tell Pharaoh that Israel was the LORD's firstborn son. If Pharaoh refused to let God's son go to serve Him, God would kill Pharaoh's firstborn son.

At a lodging place along the way, the LORD met Moses and was about to kill him. Zipporah took a flint knife, cut off her son's foreskin, and touched it to Moses' feet. She called Moses a bridegroom of blood. Then the LORD let him alone.

The LORD told Aaron to go into the wilderness to meet Moses. Aaron went and met Moses at the mountain of God and kissed him. Moses told Aaron all the words of the LORD who had sent him and all the signs He had commanded him to do.

So Moses and Aaron went and assembled all the elders of the Israelites. Aaron spoke all the words that the LORD had spoken to Moses, and Moses performed the signs in the sight of the people. And they believed.

When the Israelites heard that the LORD had attended to them and had seen their affliction, they bowed down and worshiped.

The man who had fled Egypt had come back carrying a staff, a brother spokesman, and a message for Pharaoh. The people under the brick pits had heard that God had seen their misery. In front of Moses now stood the court where he had once been a son of the house.
