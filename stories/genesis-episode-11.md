Genesis Episode 11: The Brothers Bow
Genesis 42 to 45, Berean Standard Bible

Jacob heard that there was grain in Egypt, and his sons stood looking at one another while hunger stood in the room.

Jacob told his sons to go down to Egypt and buy grain so they might live and not die. Ten of Joseph's brothers went down to buy grain. Jacob did not send Benjamin, Joseph's brother, with them. He feared that harm might happen to Benjamin. Israel's sons came among those who came to buy grain, because the famine was in the land of Canaan.

Joseph was the governor of the land and the one who sold grain to all its people. Joseph's brothers came and bowed themselves before him with their faces to the ground.

Joseph saw his brothers and recognized them, but he treated them as strangers and spoke harshly to them. He asked where they had come from. They said they had come from the land of Canaan to buy food. Joseph remembered the dreams he had dreamed about them. He accused them of being spies who had come to see the vulnerability of the land.

The brothers answered that they were all sons of one man. They were honest men, twelve brothers in all. The youngest was with their father, and one brother was no more. Joseph said their words would be tested. They would not leave Egypt unless their youngest brother came. One brother would be held in custody while the rest carried grain back for their starving households. Then they were to bring the youngest brother to Joseph. Joseph put them all in custody for three days.

On the third day Joseph told them he feared God. If they were honest men, one brother would remain confined while the rest went home with grain. They had to bring back their youngest brother, so their words would be verified and they would not die. The brothers agreed.

Then they spoke to one another while Joseph listened through an interpreter. They did not know Joseph understood them. They said, "Surely we are being punished because of our brother. We saw his anguish when he pleaded with us, but we would not listen. That is why this distress has come upon us." Reuben answered that he had told them not to sin against the boy, but they had not listened. Now his blood was being demanded.

Joseph turned away from them and wept. He returned, spoke to them, and took Simeon from them. Simeon was bound before their eyes. Joseph gave orders to fill their bags with grain, to restore every man's silver to his sack, and to give them provisions for the journey.

The brothers loaded their grain on their donkeys and departed. At the place where they spent the night, one brother opened his sack to give his donkey feed and saw his silver in the mouth of the sack. Their hearts sank, and they trembled. They asked what God had done to them.

When they came to Jacob in Canaan, they told him everything. As they emptied their sacks, each man's bundle of silver was in his sack. Jacob and his sons were afraid. Jacob named the losses one by one: Joseph was no more, Simeon was no more, and now they would take Benjamin. All these things were against him. Reuben spoke up and offered to put his own two sons to death if he did not bring Benjamin back. Jacob refused. His son would not go down with them. Joseph's brother was dead, and Benjamin alone was left. If harm happened to Benjamin on the journey, Jacob said they would bring his gray head down to Sheol in sorrow.

The famine remained severe in the land. When the family had eaten the grain brought from Egypt, Jacob told his sons to go again and buy a little food. Judah answered that the man in Egypt had solemnly warned them they would not see his face unless their brother was with them. If Jacob would send Benjamin with them, they would go and buy food. If he would not send him, they would not go.

Israel asked why they had brought trouble on him by telling the man they had another brother. The brothers answered that the man had questioned them closely about themselves and their family. He had asked whether their father was still alive and whether they had another brother. They had answered his questions. They could not know he would demand that they bring their brother down.

Judah took the responsibility Jacob needed to hear. He told his father to send the boy with him. They would arise and go, so the family might live and not die. Judah would be a pledge for Benjamin's safety. If Judah did not bring him back and set him before Jacob, Judah would bear the blame forever. If they had not delayed, they could have gone and returned twice.

Jacob relented. If it had to be this way, they were to take some of the best products of the land in their bags as a gift for the man: balm, honey, spices, myrrh, pistachio nuts, and almonds. They were to take double the silver, including the silver that had been returned in their sacks, in case it had been an oversight. They were to take Benjamin, arise, and go to the man. Jacob prayed that God Almighty would grant them mercy before the man, so he would let Simeon and Benjamin go. As for Jacob, if he was bereaved, he was bereaved.

The men took the gift, double the silver, and Benjamin. In Egypt they stood before Joseph. When Joseph saw Benjamin with them, he told the steward of his house to take the men home, slaughter an animal, and prepare a meal, for the men would eat with Joseph at noon. The steward did as Joseph said, but the brothers were afraid because they had been brought to Joseph's house. They suspected the returned silver would be used as a charge against them, to seize them as slaves and take their donkeys.

They approached the steward at the door and explained the silver. On their first journey they had opened their sacks and found each man's silver in full weight. They had brought it back, with more silver to buy food. The steward told them not to be afraid. Their God and the God of their father had given them treasure in their sacks. He had received their silver. Then he brought Simeon out to them, gave them water to wash their feet, and provided feed for their donkeys.

The brothers prepared their gift for Joseph's arrival at noon, because they had heard they would eat there. When Joseph came home, they presented the gift and bowed to the ground before him. Joseph asked about their welfare and about the old father they had spoken of. Was he still alive? They answered that their father was alive and well, and they bowed low in homage. Joseph lifted his eyes and saw Benjamin, his brother, the son of his mother. He asked whether this was their youngest brother. Joseph said that God would be gracious to him.

Then Joseph hurried out, because his compassion grew warm toward his brother. He sought a place to weep, entered his chamber, and wept there. After he washed his face, he came out and controlled himself. Food was served. Joseph was served by himself, the brothers by themselves, and the Egyptians who ate with Joseph by themselves, because Egyptians could not eat with Hebrews, an abomination to the Egyptians. The brothers were seated before Joseph in order from the firstborn to the youngest, according to birth order, and they looked at one another in astonishment. Portions were taken from Joseph's table to them, but Benjamin's portion was five times as much as any of theirs. They drank and were merry with Joseph.

Joseph commanded his steward to fill the men's sacks with as much food as they could carry, put each man's silver in the mouth of his sack, and place Joseph's silver cup in the mouth of Benjamin's sack. The steward did as Joseph said. As morning dawned, the men were sent away with their donkeys. They had gone only a short distance from the city when the steward caught up with them and accused them of returning evil for good. The cup from which his lord drank and by which he practiced divination was missing. The steward asked why they had done this evil thing.

The brothers answered that they had brought back the silver found in their sacks from Canaan. How then could they steal silver or gold from the steward's master's house? If any servant was found with the cup, he would die, and the rest would become slaves. The steward narrowed the sentence: the one found with the cup would be Joseph's slave, and the rest would be blameless.

Each brother lowered his sack quickly and opened it. The steward searched, beginning with the oldest and ending with the youngest. The cup was found in Benjamin's sack.

The brothers tore their clothes. Each man loaded his donkey, and they returned to the city. Judah and his brothers came to Joseph's house while Joseph was still there, and they fell before him to the ground. Joseph asked what deed they had done and whether they did not know he could practice divination. Judah answered that God had found out the guilt of Joseph's servants. What could they say? How could they clear themselves? They were Joseph's slaves, both they and the one in whose hand the cup had been found.

Joseph said that would be far from him. Only the man with the cup would be his slave. The rest could go up in peace to their father.

Then Judah stepped close and put his own life in the gap.

He retold the story as Joseph already knew it and as their father lived it. Judah spoke of the old man in Canaan whose life was bound up with Benjamin's life. Joseph's brother was dead. Benjamin alone was left of his mother's children, and their father loved him. If the boy did not return, their father would die. His gray head would go down to Sheol in sorrow. Judah reminded Joseph that he had guaranteed the boy's safety to his father. If Benjamin did not go back with him, Judah would bear the blame to his father all his days.

"Now please let your servant stay here as my lord's slave in place of the boy. Let him return with his brothers. For how can I go back to my father without the boy? I could not bear to see the misery that would overwhelm him."

Joseph could no longer control himself before all who stood by him. He cried for everyone to leave his presence. No Egyptian was with Joseph when he made himself known to his brothers. Joseph wept so loudly that the Egyptians heard and Pharaoh's household heard. Then Joseph said to his brothers, "I am Joseph. Is my father still alive?"

His brothers could not answer him, because they were dismayed at his presence.

Joseph told them to come near. He said he was Joseph, their brother, whom they had sold into Egypt. Then he gave them a way to survive the truth. "And now, do not be distressed or angry with yourselves that you sold me into this place, because it was to save lives that God sent me before you."

The famine had been in the land for two years, and five years remained in which there would be neither plowing nor harvest. Joseph said God had sent him before them to preserve a remnant on earth and to keep alive many survivors. "Therefore it was not you who sent me here, but God, who has made me a father to Pharaoh, lord of all his household and ruler over all the land of Egypt."

They were to hurry back to Jacob and tell him to come down without delay. Jacob would dwell in the land of Goshen, near Joseph, with his children, grandchildren, flocks, herds, and all that he had. Joseph would provide for them there, because five years of famine were still to come. They were not to fall into poverty, Jacob or his household or all that belonged to him.

Joseph fell on his brother Benjamin's neck and wept, and Benjamin wept on his neck. Joseph kissed all his brothers and wept over them. After that, his brothers talked with him.

The report reached Pharaoh's house that Joseph's brothers had come, and it pleased Pharaoh and his officials. Pharaoh told Joseph to load their animals and go to Canaan, bring their father and households, and come back. Pharaoh would give them the best of the land of Egypt, and they would eat the fat of the land. Wagons were to be taken for their wives and little ones. They were not to be concerned about their goods, for the best of all the land of Egypt was theirs.

Joseph gave them wagons according to Pharaoh's command and provisions for the journey. To each man he gave changes of clothes. To Benjamin he gave 300 pieces of silver and five changes of clothes. To his father he sent donkeys loaded with the good things of Egypt, grain, bread, and provisions. Then Joseph sent his brothers away, and as they departed, he told them not to quarrel on the way.

The brothers went up from Egypt and came to Jacob in Canaan. They told him Joseph was still alive and that Joseph was ruler over all the land of Egypt. Jacob's heart stood still because he did not believe them. But when they told him all the words Joseph had spoken, and when Jacob saw the wagons Joseph had sent to carry him, the spirit of Jacob revived. Israel said it was enough. Joseph was alive. He would go and see him before he died.
