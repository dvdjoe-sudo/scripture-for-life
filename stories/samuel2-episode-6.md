2 Samuel Episode 6: Bathsheba, and Nathan's Story
2 Samuel 11 to 12, Berean Standard Bible

In the spring, at the time when kings march out to war, David sent Joab and the whole army of Israel to finish the siege of Rabbah, and David remained in Jerusalem. The sentence is the hinge of the book, and everything that follows swings on it. One evening the king got up from his bed and strolled on the roof of the palace, and from the roof he saw a woman bathing, a very beautiful woman. The seeing was chance. Nothing after it was. David sent and inquired about her, and the answer identified her completely, in a form that gave him every fact he needed to stop: this was Bathsheba, the daughter of Eliam and the wife of Uriah the Hittite. Uriah was no stranger. He was one of David's own mighty men, one of the Thirty, a Hittite who had bound his loyalty to Israel's king and Israel's God, and at that moment he was in the field at Rabbah, fighting David's war. David sent messengers and got her, and she came to him, and he slept with her, and she returned home. The text spends no words on her choices because the text assigns the agency where the power was: a king sends, and subjects come. Some weeks later Bathsheba sent the king a message of two words, and the whole architecture of the cover-up rose on them: "I am pregnant."

The cover-up began as hospitality. David ordered Joab to send Uriah home, received him warmly, asked after Joab and the troops and the progress of the war like a king honoring a soldier, and told him to go down to his house and wash his feet, with a present from the king trailing after him as a hint. Uriah left the palace and slept at its door with the king's servants, and did not go home. Reported to David, summoned back, he gave the answer that has judged his king in every century since: "The ark and Israel and Judah are dwelling in tents, and my master Joab and his soldiers are camped in the open field. How can I go to my house to eat and drink and sleep with my wife?" He swore by the king's own life that he would not do it. The foreign soldier held a standard of solidarity with men in tents that the anointed king, in his palace above the city, had already shrugged off, and he held it drunk as faithfully as sober: David kept him another day, fed him, got him drunk, and in the evening Uriah went out to his cot among the servants and still did not go home. The cover-up had met a man it could not move, so the cover-up became a murder, and a letter. In the morning David wrote to Joab and sent the letter by Uriah's own hand, the victim carrying his own death warrant in his saddlebag: "Put Uriah at the front of the fiercest battle; then withdraw from him, so that he may be struck down and killed." Joab obeyed, placing Uriah where he knew the strongest enemy soldiers were, and the men of the city came out and fought, and some of David's servants fell, good men whose names the book does not give, dying to complete an arrangement they knew nothing about, and Uriah the Hittite also died.

Joab's report to the king is a cold masterpiece of complicity. He scripted the messenger for the king's anger, anticipating the tactical complaint, and at the end of the script he placed the one sentence that would dissolve the anger: your servant Uriah the Hittite is dead as well. When the messenger delivered it, David answered with a proverb for a conscience: the sword devours one as well as another. The thing was done, wrapped, and filed. Bathsheba mourned her husband for the proper time, and when the mourning was over David had her brought to his house, and she became his wife and bore him a son. The book then closes the chapter with the sentence that the whole palace machinery had been built to avoid, delivered in the flattest voice in Scripture: "But the thing that David had done was evil in the sight of the LORD." The Psalter ties Psalm 51 to the interview that follows, David's own words after Nathan came to him; the song belongs to the doorway this chapter opens.

Then the LORD sent Nathan to David. The prophet who had once carried God's promise to this king now came carrying God's case against him, and he brought it as a story, because a king who could not see himself might still see a stranger. There were two men in a certain city, one rich and one poor. The rich man had herds beyond counting. The poor man had nothing except one small ewe lamb he had bought, which had grown up with him and his children, sharing his food, drinking from his cup, sleeping in his arms, like a daughter to him. A traveler came to the rich man, and the rich man, unwilling to take an animal of his own, took the poor man's lamb and prepared it for his guest. Nathan stopped. David detonated. He burned with anger against the man and swore by the living LORD that the man who did this deserved to die and would pay for the lamb four times over, because he had shown no pity. The outrage was genuine, which was the tragedy: the king's moral instincts were fully alive, aimed everywhere but at the mirror. Then Nathan said the four words that have echoed ever since: "You are that man!" And the indictment unrolled, in God's voice, through the whole history: I anointed you, I delivered you from Saul, I gave you his house and his kingdom, and if that had been too little I would have given you even more. Why have you despised the command of the LORD by doing evil in His sight? "You put Uriah the Hittite to the sword and took his wife as your own. You have slain him with the sword of the Ammonites." The sentence matched the crime's architecture: because David had done this in secret against another man's house, adversity would rise against him from his own house; what he did in darkness would be done to him in broad daylight before all Israel. And beneath the sentence the permanent clause: "Now, therefore, the sword will never depart from your house".

David's answer was five words and no excuses, no list of pressures, no Saul-style footnotes: "I have sinned against the LORD." Nathan answered on the spot: "The LORD has taken away your sin," and the limit came with it: "You will not die." Forgiveness, immediate and real. And consequence, immediate and real, in the same breath: nevertheless, the son born to him would surely die. Nathan went home, and the LORD struck the child, and David pleaded with God for the boy on the ground, fasting, lying in sackcloth through the night while the elders of his household stood over him unable to raise him. On the seventh day the child died, and the servants were afraid to tell the king, until David read their whispering correctly and asked them straight out, and they answered that he was dead. Then David did the thing that confused his whole court: he rose from the ground, washed, anointed himself, changed his clothes, went into the house of the LORD and worshiped, came home, and asked for food and ate. Asked to explain, he gave them his theology of grief in two sentences. While the child lived, he had fasted and wept, because who knows, the LORD might have been gracious and let him live. "But now that he is dead, why should I fast? Can I bring him back again? I will go to him, but he will not return to me." The pleading had been real hope, not theater, and when hope closed he worshiped and ate and comforted Bathsheba, and she bore another son, and they named him Solomon, and the LORD loved the child and sent word through Nathan to give him a second name, Jedidiah, beloved of the LORD. Meanwhile Joab finished the siege David's sin had interrupted, took the royal fortress and the water supply of Rabbah, and sent for the king to come and take the city himself, so that it would not be named after Joab. David went, and the crown of the Ammonite king, a talent of gold, was set on his head. The reign went on, blessed and sentenced at once. The sword that would never depart from his house was already being sharpened, and it was being sharpened, as promised, inside his own family.
