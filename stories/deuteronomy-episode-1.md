Deuteronomy Episode 1: Moses Looks Back
Deuteronomy 1 to 4, Berean Standard Bible

"These are the words that Moses spoke to all Israel in the wilderness east of the Jordan"

That is the book's own first line, and it is a title as much as a sentence. What follows is not a new journey. It is a speech, the longest goodbye in the Bible, given by a man of 120 who knows the land in front of his audience is closed to him.

"In the fortieth year, on the first day of the eleventh month, Moses proclaimed to the Israelites all that the LORD had commanded him concerning them."

The people listening were not, most of them, the people who had lived the story. Their parents had come out of Egypt, stood at Sinai, refused the land, and died in the wilderness over forty years. The crowd on the plains of Moab was their children, grown, armed, counted, and facing the Jordan. Egypt was a story their mothers told. Sinai was a mountain they had never seen. So before Moses gave them a single law, he gave them their own history, told by the only man standing who had been an adult for all of it.

He started with the distance.

It is an eleven day journey from Horeb, the mountain of God, to Kadesh Barnea, on the border of the land. Eleven days. The people Moses addressed had spent forty years covering it, and he did not let that arithmetic sit in the background of the speech. It was the speech's whole point.

At Horeb, God had said to them: you have stayed at this mountain long enough. Break camp and advance into the hill country of the Amorites and all their neighbors, the whole land of Canaan spread out in the command, the Arabah, the hills, the lowland, the Negev, the coast, the great river Euphrates.

"See, I have placed the land before you. Enter and possess the land that the LORD swore He would give to your fathers Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and to their descendants after them."

But a nation of that size could not be judged by one man, and Moses told them how the burden had been shared before the march began. The LORD your God has multiplied you, he said, until you are as numerous as the stars in the sky. How can I bear your burdens and your disputes by myself? So the tribes chose wise, understanding, respected men, and Moses set them as commanders of thousands, of hundreds, of fifties, and of tens, and charged their judges with the rule he now repeated for their children to hear.

"Show no partiality in judging; hear both small and great alike. Do not be intimidated by anyone, for judgment belongs to God. And bring to me any case too difficult for you, and I will hear it."

Then Moses came to the event the whole speech turns on, and he told it with an angle the children in front of him may never have heard stated plainly. In Numbers, the sending of the spies begins with God's command to Moses. In Moses' own retelling, it begins with the people.

"Then all of you approached me and said," Moses recalled, "Let us send men ahead of us to search out the land and bring us word of what route to follow and which cities to enter."

The idea seemed good to Moses, he said, and he took twelve men, one from each tribe. They went up into the hill country, came to the valley of Eshcol, took some of the fruit of the land in their hands, and brought it down with the report the new generation had grown up hearing: it is a good land the LORD our God is giving us. The grapes were real. So was everything that happened next.

"But you were unwilling to go up; you rebelled against the command of the LORD your God."

"You grumbled in your tents and said," Moses told them: "Because the LORD hates us, He has brought us out of the land of Egypt to deliver us into the hand of the Amorites to be annihilated." That was the charge their parents had made in their tents: not fear of giants, but hatred, laid on the God who had carried them.

Moses told them what he had said to their parents on that night, and part of it is as tender as anything in the five books. Do not be terrified, do not be afraid of them. The LORD your God, who goes before you, will fight for you, just as He did for you in Egypt before your very eyes.

"And in the wilderness, where the LORD your God carried you, as a man carries his son, all the way by which you traveled until you reached this place."

They would not trust Him, not even then, not with Egypt behind them and the evidence in their own memories. So the LORD heard the sound of their words and swore the oath their children were now living inside: not one of the men of this evil generation shall see the good land I swore to give your fathers, except Caleb son of Jephunneh, who followed the LORD wholeheartedly, and Joshua son of Nun, standing beside Moses now, who would lead the children in.

And then Moses said the sentence his audience most needed to hear him say, because it closed the question of why the old man speaking would not be crossing the river with them. He said it his way, and the book leaves it standing in his words.

Moses put his own case in one sentence, and he put it in the form he would use again in this book: "The LORD was also angry with me on your account, and He said," and then the sentence itself, "Not even you shall enter the land."

Numbers tells the waters of Meribah from the narrator's side, Moses striking the rock he was told to speak to, and the sentence falling on Moses and Aaron there. Deuteronomy is Moses' own account of his case, given in his own farewell, and he lays his exclusion at the door of the people's rebellion, here and twice more in the book. The two records are not reconciled for the reader, because the text does not reconcile them. What is certain in both is the outcome: Moses would see the land. Moses would not enter it.

The retelling did not stop at the sentence. Moses told them how their parents had tried to reverse it the next morning, going up into the hill country against the word, after being told the LORD was not with them.

"But the LORD said to me," Moses recalled, "Tell them not to go up and fight, for I am not with you to keep you from defeat by your enemies." They had the warning in full before they climbed.

They went anyway, and the Amorites came out like a swarm of bees and chased them as far as Hormah. The people stayed at Kadesh many days. Then began the long detour that ate the rest of the generation, and Moses walked his listeners through its rules, because the rules of the detour were themselves a lesson about land and promise. God had given Mount Seir to Esau's descendants and would not let Israel take a foot of it. He had given Ar to the descendants of Lot, the Moabites, and later the Ammonites their land, and Israel was to pass by in peace, buying food with silver and water with silver, disturbing nothing. The lands east of the promised land already had owners, and their titles came from the same God. Giants had lived in those countries before, the Emim in Moab, the Zamzummim in Ammon, tall peoples the ancients counted alongside the Anakim, and God had dispossessed them for Lot's children the way He was about to dispossess nations for Israel. The gift to Israel was not the only gift on the map.

Through all of it, Moses said, God had kept them.

"Indeed, the LORD your God has blessed you in all the work of your hands. He has watched over your journey through this vast wilderness. The LORD your God has been with you these forty years, and you have lacked nothing."

"The time we spent traveling from Kadesh-barnea until we crossed over the Brook of Zered was thirty-eight years, until that entire generation of fighting men had perished from the camp, as the LORD had sworn to them."

Thirty-eight years between one brook and the sentence's end. The hand of the LORD was against that generation until it had perished from the camp, and only when the last of the counted men had died did the road turn north again, toward kings who would fight.

Moses retold Sihon briefly, the way a man summarizes a battle his audience has already heard sung. Israel had sent messengers with words of peace, asking the highway, offering to buy food and water. Sihon refused, because the LORD had hardened his spirit and made his heart obstinate, in order to deliver him into Israel's hand, and he marched out to Jahaz and lost everything: his cities taken, his people devoted to destruction, the livestock and plunder kept. God's word to Moses at the start of that campaign is the line Moses chose to repeat.

"See, I have begun to deliver Sihon and his land over to you. Now begin to conquer and possess his land."

"From Aroer on the rim of the Arnon Valley, along with the city in the valley, even as far as Gilead, not one city had walls too high for us. The LORD our God gave us all of them."

Og of Bashan followed: sixty cities taken, the whole region of Argob, fortified cities with high walls, gates, and bars, and no survivor, while the cities without walls and the livestock were taken as plunder. Moses had given the conquered land to Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh, with the oath their fathers swore and the new generation had heard rehearsed in Numbers: cross armed ahead of your brothers. Then he had charged Joshua in their hearing, and he repeated the charge now, in front of the son's generation. Your eyes have seen all that the LORD your God has done to these two kings. So the LORD will do to all the kingdoms over there. Do not be afraid of them, for the LORD your God Himself will fight for you.

Then the farewell turned personal, and Moses let them hear the one prayer of his that God refused.

"At that time I also pleaded with the LORD," he said. "O Lord GOD, You have begun to show Your greatness and power to Your servant. For what god in heaven or on earth can perform such works and mighty acts as Yours?" And then the request itself: "Please let me cross over and see the good land beyond the Jordan," Moses begged, "that pleasant hill country as well as Lebanon!"

"But the LORD was angry with me on account of you, and He would not listen to me." Then came the refusal, in words that end all argument: "That is enough," the LORD said to me. "Do not speak to Me again about this matter." The matter was closed, by name.

"Go to the top of Pisgah and look to the west and north and south and east. See the land with your own eyes, for you will not cross this Jordan."

"But commission Joshua, encourage him, and strengthen him, for he will cross over ahead of the people and enable them to inherit the land that you will see."

The man who had spent forty years successfully arguing God out of destroying Israel could not argue his own sentence down by one river's width. God gave him the mountain view instead, and a successor to strengthen, and silence on the subject from then on.

Chapter 4 turns the history into its lesson, and it is the first sermon proper of the book. Now, Israel, hear the statutes and ordinances I am teaching you. Do not add to them. Do not subtract from them.

"You must not add to or subtract from what I command you, so that you may keep the commandments of the LORD your God that I am giving you."

Moses pointed their memory at the most recent proof. Your eyes have seen what the LORD did at Baal Peor: every man who followed Baal of Peor, the LORD destroyed from among you, but you who held fast to the LORD your God are all alive today. The children listening were the survivors of that plague's generation, and some of them had been there. Then he set the keeping of the law in front of the nations watching: keep the statutes, for this is your wisdom and understanding in the sight of the peoples, who will say, surely this great nation is a wise and understanding people. What other great nation has God so near, or statutes as righteous as this whole law?

Then the guard at the center of it.

"Only be on your guard and diligently watch yourselves, so that you do not forget the things your eyes have seen, and so that they do not slip from your heart as long as you live. Teach them to your children and grandchildren."

Remember the day you stood before the LORD your God at Horeb, Moses said, reaching back past the children's births to the mountain their parents had trembled at, when God had gathered the people to hear His words so that they would learn to fear Him and teach their children. They had heard a voice out of the fire and seen no form. That formlessness became the sermon's hammer. Because you saw no form when the LORD spoke at Horeb, watch yourselves carefully: do not act corruptly by making an idol in any likeness, male or female, animal, bird, crawler, or fish, and do not be drawn to worship the sun, the moon, and the stars, the host of heaven allotted to all the peoples under heaven. Egypt's gods had shapes. The God who spoke from the fire had a voice and no shape, and the children were to keep Him that way in their minds.

"For the LORD your God is a consuming fire, a jealous God."

And then Moses did something no summary of the sermon should skip, because it is the first time in the five books that the whole future is laid out in one paragraph. If, after settling in the land and growing old there, they corrupted themselves with images and did evil, heaven and earth would witness against them, and they would perish quickly from the land and be scattered among the peoples, few in number, serving gods of wood and stone that neither see nor hear nor eat nor smell. All of it, Moses said in advance: the conquest, the corruption, the exile, and then the turn.

"But if from there you will seek the LORD your God, you will find Him if you seek Him with all your heart and with all your soul."

"When you are in distress and all these things have happened to you, then in later days you will return to the LORD your God and listen to His voice."

"For the LORD your God is a merciful God; He will not abandon you or destroy you or forget the covenant with your fathers, which He swore to them by oath."

The sermon closed where the whole book aims. Ask about the days of old, Moses said, since God created man on the earth, from one end of heaven to the other: has anything this great ever happened, or has anything like it been heard of? Has a people ever heard the voice of God speaking from the fire and lived? Has God ever taken a nation for Himself from the midst of another nation, by trials, signs, wonders, war, a mighty hand and an outstretched arm?

"You were shown these things so that you would know that the LORD is God; there is no other besides Him."

"Know therefore this day and take to heart that the LORD is God in heaven above and on the earth below; there is no other."

Moses set apart three cities east of the Jordan for the manslayer to flee to, Bezer, Ramoth in Gilead, and Golan in Bashan, the other side of the refuge system Numbers had built. And the narrator closes the first address with a sentence that sets the stage for the second: this is the law that Moses set before the Israelites, the testimonies, statutes, and ordinances he spoke when they came out of Egypt, in the land they had already taken, at the edge of the land they had not.

The old man had covered forty years in one speech, and he had not yet opened the law itself. That was next, and he began it the only place it could begin: back at the mountain, with the ten words.
