Exodus Episode 4: Bread, Water, and Amalek
Exodus 16 to 18, Berean Standard Bible

Elim had water and shade, but Elim was not the destination. Israel moved on from the springs and palm trees into the Wilderness of Sin, between Elim and Sinai. It was the fifteenth day of the second month after they had come out of Egypt, and the whole congregation was hungry.

Hunger did what Pharaoh's army had not done. It turned Israel back toward Egypt in their own words. The people grumbled against Moses and Aaron in the desert. They said they wished the LORD had killed them in Egypt. In Egypt, they said, they sat around pots of meat and ate all the food they wanted. Now Moses and Aaron had brought them into the desert to starve the whole assembly to death.

Moses and Aaron told all the Israelites that in the evening they would know the LORD had brought them out of Egypt, and in the morning they would see the glory of the LORD. The LORD had heard their grumbling. Their grumbling was not against Moses and Aaron. It was against the LORD.

While Aaron spoke to the whole assembly, they looked toward the wilderness, and there the glory of the LORD appeared in the cloud.

The LORD told Moses, "I have heard the grumbling of the Israelites. Tell them, 'At twilight you will eat meat, and in the morning you will be filled with bread. Then you will know that I am the LORD your God.'"

That evening quail came up and covered the camp. In the morning a layer of dew lay around the camp. When the dew lifted, thin flakes like frost were on the desert ground. "When the Israelites saw it, they asked one another, 'What is it?' For they did not know what it was. So Moses told them, 'It is the bread that the LORD has given you to eat.'"

The LORD had already named the test inside the gift. "Behold, I will rain down bread from heaven for you. Each day the people are to go out and gather enough for that day. In this way I will test whether or not they will follow My instructions."

Each person was to gather an omer for each person in his tent. The Israelites did so. Some gathered much, some little. When they measured, the one who gathered much had nothing left over, and the one who gathered little had no shortage. Each had gathered as much as he needed. Moses told them not to keep any of it until morning.

Some did not listen. They kept part of it until morning, and it was full of maggots and stank. Moses was angry with them. Each morning everyone gathered as much as he needed, and when the sun grew hot, what was left on the ground melted away.

On the sixth day the people gathered twice as much, two omers for each person. The leaders came and reported this to Moses. Moses told them what the LORD had commanded. Tomorrow would be a day of solemn rest, a holy Sabbath to the LORD. They were to bake what they wanted to bake and boil what they wanted to boil that day, and save whatever was left to be kept until morning. They did as Moses said, and the saved manna did not stink or get maggots in it. On the seventh day the people were to eat what had been saved, because that day was a Sabbath to the LORD. They would find no manna in the field. They were to gather for six days. On the seventh day, the Sabbath, there would be none.

Some people went out on the seventh day to gather anyway, but they found nothing. The LORD asked Moses how long the people would refuse to keep His commands and instructions. The LORD had given them the Sabbath. That was why on the sixth day He gave them bread for two days. Everyone was to stay where he was. No one was to go out on the seventh day. So the people rested on the seventh day.

The house of Israel called the bread manna. "It was white like coriander seed and tasted like wafers made with honey." Moses told Aaron, "Take a jar and fill it with an omer of manna. Then place it before the LORD to be preserved for the generations to come." Aaron placed it before the testimony, to be kept, as the LORD commanded. The Israelites ate manna forty years, until they came to a land that was settled. They ate manna until they reached the border of Canaan.

From the Wilderness of Sin the whole congregation traveled by stages, as the LORD commanded, and camped at Rephidim. There was no water for the people to drink.

The people quarreled with Moses. They demanded that he give them water to drink. Moses asked why they quarreled with him and why they tested the LORD. But the people thirsted there for water, and they grumbled against Moses. They asked why he had brought them up out of Egypt to kill them, their children, and their livestock with thirst.

Moses cried out to the LORD. What was he to do with these people? They were almost ready to stone him.

The LORD told Moses to go on ahead of the people and take some of the elders of Israel with him. He was to take in his hand the staff with which he struck the Nile, and go. "Behold, I will stand there before you by the rock at Horeb. And when you strike the rock, water will come out of it for the people to drink." Moses did this in the sight of the elders of Israel.

"He named the place Massah and Meribah because the Israelites quarreled, and because they tested the LORD, saying, 'Is the LORD among us or not?'"

Then Amalek came and fought with Israel at Rephidim.

Moses said to Joshua to choose men for Israel and go out to fight Amalek. The next day Moses would stand on top of the hill with the staff of God in his hand. Joshua did as Moses told him and fought Amalek, while Moses, Aaron, and Hur went up to the top of the hill. As long as Moses held up his hand, Israel prevailed. When he lowered his hand, Amalek prevailed.

Moses' hands grew heavy. Aaron and Hur took a stone and put it under him, and he sat on it. Aaron held up one hand and Hur held up the other, so Moses' hands remained steady until sunset. Joshua overwhelmed Amalek and his people with the edge of the sword.

The LORD told Moses to write this on a scroll as a memorial and recite it in the hearing of Joshua: He would utterly blot out the memory of Amalek from under heaven. Moses built an altar and named it The LORD Is My Banner. He said, "a hand was lifted up toward the throne of the LORD. The LORD will war against Amalek from generation to generation."

Now Jethro, the priest of Midian and Moses' father-in-law, heard of everything God had done for Moses and for Israel, how the LORD had brought Israel out of Egypt. Jethro brought Moses' wife Zipporah, who had been sent back, and her two sons. One son was named Gershom, for Moses said he had become a foreigner in a foreign land. The other was named Eliezer, for Moses said the God of his father was his helper and had delivered him from Pharaoh's sword.

Jethro came with Moses' wife and sons into the wilderness where Israel was camped near the mountain of God. Word came to Moses that Jethro was coming, and Moses went out to meet his father-in-law. He bowed down and kissed him. They greeted each other and went into the tent.

Moses told Jethro all that the LORD had done to Pharaoh and Egypt for Israel's sake, all the hardships that had met them along the way, and how the LORD had saved them. Jethro rejoiced over all the good things the LORD had done for Israel in rescuing them from Egypt.

Jethro declared, "Blessed be the LORD, who has delivered you from the hand of the Egyptians and of Pharaoh, and who has delivered the people from the hand of the Egyptians. Now I know that the LORD is greater than all other gods, for He did this when they treated Israel with arrogance."

Then Jethro brought a burnt offering and other sacrifices to God. Aaron and all the elders of Israel came to eat bread with Moses' father-in-law in the presence of God.

The next day Moses sat to judge the people. The people stood around Moses from morning until evening. When Jethro saw all that Moses was doing for the people, he asked what this was and why Moses sat alone while all the people stood around him from morning to evening.

Moses answered that the people came to him to seek God's will. When they had a dispute, they came to him, and he decided between one person and another. He informed them of God's decrees and laws.

Jethro told Moses that what he was doing was not good. Moses and the people who came to him would only wear themselves out. The work was too heavy for Moses. He could not handle it alone.

Jethro gave counsel. Moses was to be the people's representative before God and bring their disputes to Him. He was to teach the Israelites the decrees and laws and show them the way to live and the duties they were to perform. But he was also to select capable men from all the people, men who feared God, trustworthy men who hated dishonest gain, and appoint them as officials over thousands, hundreds, fifties, and tens. "Have these men judge the people at all times. Then they can bring you any major issue, but all minor cases they can judge on their own, so that your load may be lightened as they share it with you."

Jethro told Moses that if he did this, and God so commanded, Moses would be able to stand the strain, and all these people would go home satisfied. Moses listened to Jethro and did everything he said. He chose capable men from all Israel and made them heads of the people, officials over thousands, hundreds, fifties, and tens. They judged the people at all times. The difficult cases they brought to Moses, but the simple ones they decided themselves.

Then Moses sent his father-in-law on his way, and Jethro returned to his own country.

Israel had bread from heaven, water from a rock, victory over Amalek, and judges beside Moses. Ahead of them rose the mountain where the cloud had first spoken to Moses alone.
