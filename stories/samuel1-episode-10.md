1 Samuel Episode 10: Ziklag in Ashes, and the Fall of Saul
1 Samuel 29 to 31, Berean Standard Bible

The Philistine army reviewed at Aphek with David marching in it, at the rear, behind Achish, six hundred Israelites in the ranks of Israel's enemy, on their way to fight Israel. It was the trap his whole double life had been building toward, and it was sprung not by God and not by Saul but by the Philistine high command, who looked at the Hebrew contingent and refused it. What about these Hebrews? they demanded, and Achish defended his vassal with an oath that lands strangely from a Philistine king: "As surely as the LORD lives, you have been upright, and it seems right in my sight that you should march in and out with me in the army, because I have found no fault in you from the day you came to me until this day." The commanders were unmoved. They quoted Israel's own victory song back at him, the one about the thousands and the tens of thousands, and asked the practical question: what better way for this man to regain his master's favor than with the heads of Philistine soldiers? Send him back. Achish, outvoted, apologized to David in terms no Israelite had used about him in years: "I know that you are as pleasing in my sight as an angel of God." David protested his dismissal in form, asking what fault had been found in him, to keep him "from going along to fight against the enemies of my lord the king", and then he got up before dawn and marched home to Ziklag, out of the war entirely. The book never says what David would have done on the field at Gilboa had he stayed. It simply removes him, by enemy distrust, from the possibility, and lets his dismissal preach: the commanders of the Philistines built the wall that kept God's anointed from raising a sword against God's people.

Three days' march brought his men home to a smoking ruin. While David had been reviewing at Aphek, Amalek had come up from the south, the same people Saul had once been sent to destroy and had half-destroyed, and had raided Ziklag in its emptiness, burned it, and carried off everyone in it, the women and children of all six hundred families. They had killed no one; the text buries that mercy in a subordinate clause, and it is the hinge the whole rescue hangs on. David and his men came into the ashes and found the town burned and their wives and sons and daughters gone, and they "lifted up their voices and wept until they had no strength left to weep". Both of David's wives were among the captives. And when the weeping spent itself it turned to the politics of grief: the men, every one of them bereaved, began to speak of stoning David, the leader whose alliance had left the town uncovered and whose families were suffering for it. It is the lowest sentence of his life in this book, lower than the cave, lower than the madman's drool at Gath, and the book sets against it one line that has steadied readers ever since: "But David found strength in the LORD his God." He did not argue with the men, organize, or despair. He called for the ephod. The fugitive years had taught him the sequence his despair at Ziklag's founding had skipped: ask first. "Should I pursue these raiders? Will I overtake them?" "Pursue them," the LORD replied, "for you will surely overtake them and rescue the captives."

At the Brook of Besor, two hundred of his six hundred were too exhausted to cross, and David left them with the supplies and went on with four hundred, and the story bends toward one of its gentlest details. On the way they found an Egyptian half dead in a field, a slave abandoned by his Amalekite master when he fell sick, and David's men, hunting a raiding party on a promise, stopped and fed him, bread and fig cake and raisins and water, three days' worth of hunger answered before a single question was asked. Revived, he told them everything: he had been on the raid; he knew where the raiders were; on an oath that David would neither kill him nor return him to his master, he would lead them there. He led them down at twilight onto the whole Amalekite force spread out over the land, eating and drinking and dancing over the plunder of two countries, and David struck them from twilight until the evening of the next day, and four hundred young men on camels were all that escaped. And the recovery was total, and the text totals it like a man counting children: "So David recovered everything the Amalekites had taken, including his two wives. Nothing was missing, young or old, son or daughter, or any of the plunder the Amalekites had taken. David brought everything back."

Then the plunder nearly started a civil war inside his own camp. The men who had fought wanted the recovered goods kept among the fighters; the two hundred who had stayed at Besor should get their families and nothing more. The speakers are named by the narrator with an old word, the wicked and worthless men, and David overruled them with a ruling he made statute in Israel for good: "My brothers, you must not do this with what the LORD has given us. He has protected us and delivered into our hands the raiders who came against us. Who will listen to your proposal? The share of the one who went to battle will match the share of the one who stayed with the supplies. They will share alike." The man who would not take Saul's life by the sword also would not let victory become prize money. Grace, distributed by rule. And out of the surplus he sent gifts ahead of him to the elders of Judah, town by town, Bethel and Ramoth and Jattir and Hebron and all the places he had roamed, saying, "Here is a gift for you from the plunder of the LORD’s enemies." The fugitive was doing the work of a king in Judah while the king was still on his throne.

In those same days, the army David had been dismissed from met the Philistines at Mount Gilboa, and the word spoken at Endor kept its appointment. The men of Israel fled, and many fell, and the Philistines closed on the royal family: "The Philistines hotly pursued Saul and his sons, and they killed Saul’s sons Jonathan, Abinadab, and Malchishua." Jonathan died first in the list, as he had lived: beside his father, in the war his father had made, the finest man of the reign buried in its wreckage. The archers found Saul and wounded him badly, and the king's last command was to his armor-bearer: "Draw your sword and run me through with it, or these uncircumcised men will come and run me through and torture me!" The young man refused, terrified, and Saul took his own sword and fell on it, and the armor-bearer fell on his beside him. "So Saul, his three sons, his armor-bearer, and all his men died together that same day." Tomorrow, Samuel had said, you and your sons will be with me. The cities of the valley emptied at the news, and the Philistines moved into Israelite houses.

The next day they came to strip the dead and found the king, and they treated his body as his kingdom had taught them trophies were treated. They cut off his head and sent it with his armor through the land as a proclamation in the temples of their idols; the armor they displayed in the temple of the Ashtoreths, and his body they fastened to the wall of Beth-shan. It is the scene from the ark's captivity played in reverse: Israel's anointed hung as a Philistine trophy, and this time no god fell on his face in the night. But the book's first debt was still outstanding, and it was collected by the town that owed it. When the men of Jabesh-gilead heard what had been done to Saul, the town Saul had rescued in his first and finest campaign, the town of the gouged eyes and the dawn deliverance, their men of valor marched all night, took the bodies of Saul and his sons down from the wall, brought them home to Jabesh, burned them there, buried the bones under the tamarisk tree, and fasted seven days. The book ends there, in Jabesh, with the first king of Israel buried by the first people he saved, mourned properly by the only town that never forgot him.

What David did when the news reached Ziklag, and the song he sang over Saul and Jonathan, the book of Samuel holds for its second half, where it belongs. This book closes on the burial mound and the empty throne, with the word of the LORD having fallen nowhere: everything Samuel spoke from the night God called the boy had come to pass, down to the day. And in Bethlehem's line, still carrying Amalek's plunder as gifts to Judah's elders, the man after God's own heart was waiting, as he had waited in caves and courts for a decade, for the kingdom to come to him the way the anointing had: from God's hand, at God's time, with his own hands kept clean of the taking.
