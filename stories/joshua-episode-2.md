Joshua Episode 2: Gilgal, Jericho, and the Sin in the Camp
Joshua 5 to 8, Berean Standard Bible

The first news out of Gilgal belonged to the enemy. "Now when all the Amorite kings west of the Jordan and all the Canaanite kings along the coast heard how the LORD had dried up the waters of the Jordan before the Israelites until they had crossed over, their hearts melted and their spirits failed for fear of the Israelites." What Rahab had reported from one rooftop was true of a whole coastline. Israel had crossed without striking a blow, and the land was already afraid.

What Israel did next was not attack. "At that time the LORD said to Joshua, "Make flint knives and circumcise the sons of Israel once again."" The reason takes the story back forty years: every man circumcised in Egypt had died in the wilderness, and the sons born on the journey had never been circumcised along the way. So at a hill the text names Gibeath-haaraloth, Joshua circumcised the new generation with knives of stone, and the whole nation stayed in camp, inside enemy country, until they were healed. God's comment on the rite reached back past the wilderness to the slavery itself: "Today I have rolled away the reproach of Egypt from you." The place took its name from the sentence: Gilgal. It was a strange first act for an invasion. Before Israel had won a single battle, its army lay wounded in an open camp within reach of Jericho's walls, marked with the covenant sign before it was armed with a victory.

Four days after the crossing date, on the evening of the fourteenth day of the month, Israel kept the Passover at Gilgal on the plains of Jericho, the first in the land. The next day they ate the land's own produce, unleavened bread and roasted grain. And then, quietly, a forty-year era ended at breakfast: "And the day after they had eaten from the produce of the land, the manna ceased. There was no more manna for the Israelites, so that year they began to eat the crops of the land of Canaan." No announcement, no ceremony. The bread from heaven stopped because the bread of Canaan had started.

Before Jericho, Joshua met someone. Near the city he looked up and saw a man standing in front of him with a drawn sword, and Joshua, who did not yet know who he was facing, asked the soldier's question: "Are You for us or for our enemies?" The answer refused the terms of the question: "Neither," He replied. "I have now come as Commander of the LORD's army." Joshua fell facedown and asked what his Lord had to say to His servant, and the Commander gave him the sentence Moses had heard at a burning bush: "Take off your sandals, for the place where you are standing is holy." And Joshua did so. The assistant had become the man standing on holy ground, and the army he led had just been reclassified: it belonged to a Commander above him.

"Now Jericho was tightly shut up because of the Israelites. No one went out and no one came in." God's plan for the shut city sounded like no siege ever fought: march around it once a day for six days, seven priests with seven rams' horns before the ark, and on the seventh day circle it seven times, then a long horn blast and a mighty shout from everyone at once: "Then the wall of the city will collapse and all your people will charge straight into the city."

Joshua added one order of his own, and it may have been the hardest to keep: "Do not give a battle cry or let your voice be heard; do not let one word come out of your mouth until the day I tell you to shout. Then you are to shout!" So for six days an army marched around a walled city in silence, horns sounding, saying nothing, and went back to camp. On the seventh day they rose at dawn and circled seven times, and after the seventh circuit Joshua gave the word and the terms together: "Shout! For the LORD has given you the city! Now the city and everything in it must be devoted to the LORD for destruction. Only Rahab the prostitute and all those with her in her house will live, because she hid the spies we sent."

And he gave a warning that the rest of this episode hangs on: "But keep away from the things devoted to destruction, lest you yourself be set apart for destruction. If you take any of these, you will set apart the camp of Israel for destruction and bring disaster upon it. For all the silver and gold and all the articles of bronze and iron are holy to the LORD; they must go into His treasury."

The horns blew, the people shouted, and the wall collapsed, and the army charged straight in. What followed is told in one unflinching verse: "With the edge of the sword they devoted to destruction everything in the city", and the text lists man and woman, young and old, oxen, sheep, and donkeys. Nothing is softened and nothing is celebrated; the sentence stands as the record of what devoted to destruction meant in practice.

The spies kept their oath in the middle of it. They brought Rahab out, her parents and brothers and all who belonged to her, and settled the family outside the camp. The city was burned, the metals went into the treasury of the LORD's house, and Joshua pronounced a curse over the ruins: "Cursed before the LORD is the man who rises up and rebuilds this city, Jericho; at the cost of his firstborn he will lay its foundations; at the cost of his youngest he will set up its gates." Of Rahab the narrator adds the longest view in the book so far: she "has lived among the Israelites to this day." And of Joshua: "So the LORD was with Joshua, and his fame spread throughout the land."

Then the story turns, and it turns in the narrator's first sentence, which tells the reader a secret the army did not know: "The Israelites, however, acted unfaithfully regarding the things devoted to destruction. Achan son of Carmi, the son of Zabdi, the son of Zerah, of the tribe of Judah, took some of what was set apart. So the anger of the LORD burned against the Israelites."

No one in the camp knew. Joshua sent spies to the next target, Ai, a small town near Bethel, and they came back with the confidence Jericho had bought: "There is no need to send all the people; two or three thousand men are enough to go up and attack Ai. Since the people of Ai are so few, you need not wear out all our people there." About three thousand went up, and Ai's men routed them and killed about thirty-six, chasing them down the slopes. The narrator reaches for the word that has belonged to Jericho all book long and puts it on Israel: "So the hearts of the people melted and became like water."

Joshua tore his clothes and lay facedown before the ark until evening, the elders with dust on their heads beside him, and his prayer was an accusation wrapped in grief: "O, Lord GOD," he said, "why did You ever bring this people across the Jordan to deliver us into the hand of the Amorites to be destroyed? If only we had been content to stay on the other side of the Jordan!" And at its end, the appeal Moses used to make: "When the Canaanites and all who live in the land hear about this, they will surround us and wipe out our name from the earth. Then what will You do for Your great name?"

God's answer began by standing him up. "Stand up! Why have you fallen on your face? Israel has sinned; they have transgressed My covenant that I commanded them, and they have taken some of what was devoted to destruction. Indeed, they have stolen and lied, and they have put these things with their own possessions." The defeat at Ai had not been a failure of tactics. "I will no longer be with you unless you remove from among you whatever is devoted to destruction."

The removal was a narrowing, done by lot in the morning, tribe by tribe, clan by clan, family by family, man by man. Judah was taken. Then the Zerahites. Then the family of Zabdi. Then one man stood where the narrowing ended: Achan. Joshua's words to him were not a shout but an appeal, and they gave the guilty man the dignity of a confession instead of a denial: "My son, give glory to the LORD, the God of Israel, and make a confession to Him. I urge you to tell me what you have done; do not hide it from me."

Achan did not hide it. "It is true," he said. "I have sinned against the LORD, the God of Israel. This is what I did: When I saw among the spoils a beautiful cloak from Shinar, two hundred shekels of silver, and a bar of gold weighing fifty shekels, I coveted them and took them. They are hidden in the ground inside my tent, with the silver underneath." Messengers ran to the tent, and it was all there, exactly as confessed. They spread the goods before the LORD, and Joshua and all Israel took Achan to the Valley of Achor with the silver, the cloak, the gold, his sons and daughters, his animals, his tent, everything he owned. Joshua's sentence named the exchange: "Why have you brought this trouble upon us?" And then: "Today the LORD will bring trouble upon you!" All Israel stoned him, and over him they raised a large pile of rocks, and the narrator's refrain sounds again: the pile "remains to this day", and the valley is called Achor, trouble, "to this day."

Ai still had to be taken, and this time God gave a strategy instead of a ceremony and changed one term of engagement: "Do not be afraid or discouraged. Take the whole army with you, and go up and attack Ai. See, I have delivered into your hand the king of Ai, his people, his city, and his land." This city, unlike Jericho, Israel could plunder: "you may carry off their plunder and livestock for yourselves." The devoted things had never been about greed management; they had been about whose the first city was.

The plan was an ambush, thirty thousand men by night behind the city in the traditional reading of the muster, five thousand more between Bethel and Ai, and Joshua's force pretending to break and run as it had in the real defeat. It worked in every detail: the king of Ai emptied his city in pursuit, and God told Joshua, "Hold out your battle lance toward Ai, for into your hand I will deliver the city." The ambush fired the empty city, the fleeing column turned on its pursuers, and the men of Ai were caught between the two forces. Twelve thousand fell that day. "Joshua did not draw back the hand that held his battle lance until he had devoted to destruction all who lived in Ai." The king was hanged on a tree until evening and taken down at sunset, as Moses' law required for a hanged man, and his body was thrown at the city gate under another great pile of rocks, which "remains to this day."

Then, with the war barely started, Israel stopped to do the thing Moses had commanded from across the river years before. On Mount Ebal Joshua built an altar of uncut stones, "an altar of uncut stones on which no iron tool has been used", and offered burnt offerings and peace offerings. He inscribed a copy of the law of Moses on stones in the presence of the people. And the nation split in two halves on the facing slopes, half before Mount Gerizim and half before Mount Ebal around the ark, while Joshua read the whole law aloud, all of it: "Afterward, Joshua read aloud all the words of the law", the text says, "the blessings and the curses", "according to all that is written in the Book of the Law." The last word on the day belongs to the completeness of it: "There was not a word of all that Moses had commanded that Joshua failed to read before the whole assembly of Israel, including the women, the little ones, and the foreigners who lived among them."

Three piles of stones now stood in the land: twelve at Gilgal for a crossing, one in the Valley of Achor for a theft, and one at Ai's gate for a hanged king, and the book would keep stacking them. Two cities had fallen and the covenant had been renewed in the middle of the land. What Israel did not know, camped between the mountains, was that the next move in this war would not be made by Joshua at all. It would be made by frightened men from a city a day's walk away, carrying moldy bread.
