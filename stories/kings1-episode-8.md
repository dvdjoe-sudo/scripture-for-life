1 Kings Episode 8: Elijah: Drought and Fire
1 Kings 17-18, Berean Standard Bible

He arrives with no genealogy, no hometown biography, no buildup at all. One verse after Hiel buries his second son in Jericho's new gates, Elijah the Tishbite, a settler of Gilead, is standing before Ahab with a sentence: “As surely as the LORD, the God of Israel, lives, before whom I stand, there will be neither dew nor rain in these years except at my word!” That is the whole introduction. A man from across the Jordan walks into the court of the king who built Baal a temple and announces that the sky now answers to the word of the LORD spoken through him: no rain, and not even dew, until he says otherwise. Baal was the storm god, the rider of the clouds, the one whose whole portfolio was rain. Elijah's sentence was not a weather forecast. It was a declaration of war on the god in the new temple, delivered in the palace, and then the prophet disappears and God hides him. East of the Jordan, at the Brook of Cherith, ravens brought him bread and meat morning and evening, unclean birds catering for the prophet of God, and he drank from the brook. And some time later the brook dried up, because there had been no rain in the land. The drought Elijah had announced did not exempt Elijah. The word of God had closed the sky over the prophet's own water supply, and he sat and watched his brook shrink like everyone else's.

Then God sent him to the last place a hunted Israelite prophet would be provisioned: Zarephath, in Sidon, Jezebel's own country, Baal's home ground, to a widow. He found her at the city gate gathering sticks, and he asked her for water, and as she went for it he called after her and asked for bread too. Her answer is the sound of the bottom of the famine: “I am gathering a couple of sticks to take home and prepare a meal for myself and my son, so that we may eat it and die.” She had a handful of flour in a jar and a little oil in a jug, one last meal for two, and she was collecting the fuel to cook it when the stranger asked her to feed him first. Elijah told her not to be afraid, and to make his cake first, and then her own, on the strength of a promise from a God she had called, politely, “the LORD your God”: “The jar of flour will not be exhausted and the jug of oil will not run dry until the day the LORD sends rain upon the face of the earth.” She went and did it. And the jar and the jug held. “The jar of flour was not exhausted and the jug of oil did not run dry, according to the word that the LORD had spoken through Elijah.” Day after day of famine, the supply renewed itself at the bottom of the containers, in a Sidonian widow's house, while the king of Israel searched every nation with oaths for the man eating there.

Then her son fell ill, and worsened, until no breath remained in him, and the miracle house became an accusation. The widow turned on her guest: “Have you come to remind me of my iniquity and cause the death of my son?” Elijah's answer to that was not an argument. He took the boy from her arms, carried him up to his own room, laid him on his own bed, and prayed the most honest prayer in the chapter, half complaint and half plea: “have You also brought tragedy on this widow who has opened her home to me, by causing her son to die?” Then he stretched himself out over the child three times, and cried, “O LORD my God, please let this boy's life return to him!” And the LORD listened to the voice of Elijah, the text says, in a reversal worth noticing, the prophet's voice heard in heaven as God's voice had been heard on earth, and the child's life returned. Elijah carried him down and handed him back: “Look, your son is alive,” Elijah declared. And the Sidonian widow gave the verdict that Israel's king had not given: “Now I know that you are a man of God and that the word of the LORD from your mouth is truth.”

Three years of drought passed over that verdict. In Samaria the famine was severe, and Ahab's court was reduced to the king and his palace steward dividing the withered land between them to search for enough grass to keep the horses and mules alive. The steward was Obadiah, and the text stops to tell us who he was: a man who greatly feared the LORD, who, when Jezebel was slaughtering the prophets of the LORD, had hidden a hundred of them, fifty to a cave, and fed them bread and water at the risk of his life, inside the palace of the woman hunting them. Elijah met him on the road, alone, and told him to go announce to Ahab: Elijah is here. Obadiah's terror at the errand fills half the chapter's front, and it is worth hearing for what it reveals about the court Elijah was walking back into: the king had made nation after nation swear they were not hiding the prophet, and Obadiah, who had saved a hundred prophets, believed with good reason that announcing Elijah and then failing to produce him would cost his head. Elijah swore by the LORD of Hosts that he would present himself that day, and Obadiah went, and Ahab came out, and the two men finally stood face to face. “Is that you, O troubler of Israel?” Elijah gave it back without blinking: “I have not troubled Israel”, but Ahab and his father's house had, forsaking the commandments of the LORD and following the Baals. Then Elijah set the terms of the contest, and he set them on Baal's mountain, before all Israel: Carmel, four hundred and fifty prophets of Baal, four hundred prophets of Asherah who ate at Jezebel's table, two bulls, two altars, no fire allowed. “The God who answers by fire, He is God.” And all the people answered, “What you say is good.”

On the mountain Elijah first addressed not the prophets but the crowd, and the crowd is the point of the whole day. “How long will you waver between two opinions? If the LORD is God, follow Him. But if Baal is God, follow him.” The text records the answer of all Israel to the simplest question in the book: “But the people did not answer a word.” So the contest went forward over the heads of the silent. Baal's prophets chose their bull, built their altar, and called on the name of Baal from morning until noon, leaping around the altar, crying “O Baal, answer us!” There was no sound, and no one answered. At noon Elijah began to taunt them, and the taunt is deliberately crude, a court jester dismantling a god in front of his priests: “Shout louder, for he is a god! Perhaps he is deep in thought, or occupied, or on a journey. Perhaps he is sleeping and must be awakened!” They shouted louder, and cut themselves with knives and lances until the blood ran, as was their custom, and raved on past midday to the evening sacrifice. “But there was no response; no one answered, no one paid attention.” Six hours of frenzy against a silent sky, and the silence was the sermon. Baal, the storm god, in the third year of a drought, on his own mountain, with his prophets bleeding for him, did not so much as flicker.

Then Elijah called the silent people close, and the first thing he did was repair. The altar of the LORD on Carmel had been torn down, in the purge years, and he rebuilt it out of twelve stones, one for each tribe of the sons of Jacob, for the divided people who were watching: twelve, not ten and two. He dug a trench around it, laid the wood, cut the bull, and then he made the answer impossible to fake, dousing the offering and the wood with jar after jar of water in the middle of a drought, three times over, until the water ran down and filled the trench. And at the hour of the evening sacrifice he stepped forward and prayed, and the prayer is as short as the day had been long, with no leaping and no blood: “O LORD, God of Abraham, Isaac, and Israel, let it be known this day that You are God in Israel and that I am Your servant and have done all these things at Your command. Answer me, O LORD! Answer me, so that this people will know that You, the LORD, are God, and that You have turned their hearts back again.” The stated aim was never the fire. It was the hearts of the wavering crowd. “Then the fire of the LORD fell and consumed the sacrifice, the wood, the stones, and the dust, and it licked up the water in the trench.” It burned the stones. And the people who had not answered a word all day fell on their faces and found their voice at last: “The LORD, He is God! The LORD, He is God!” Elijah had them seize the prophets of Baal, and he brought them down to the Kishon Valley and slaughtered them there, all four hundred and fifty, the text says, not one escaped. The drought's cause, in the book's logic, was purged with the drought's priests.

Then, with the altar still smoking, Elijah told Ahab to go up and eat and drink, “for there is the sound of a heavy rain”. No cloud yet, no wind, just the sound, heard by faith while the sky was still empty. Ahab went to his dinner. Elijah climbed to the summit of Carmel, bent down to the ground, put his face between his knees, and sent his servant to look toward the sea. Nothing. Six times the servant went, and six times there was nothing there, and six times Elijah sent him back. On the seventh time the servant came back with a report: “There is a cloud as small as a man’s hand rising from the sea.” That was enough. Elijah sent word to Ahab to harness his chariot and get down to Jezreel before the rain stopped him, and the sky grew dark with clouds and wind, and the heavy rain came down at last on the land that had not seen it in three years, and Ahab rode for Jezreel. And the hand of the LORD came on the prophet who had spent the day standing still before kings and crowds, and he tucked his cloak into his belt and ran, ran ahead of the king's chariot through the storm, all the way to Jezreel, like a herald outrunning the royal procession, announcing with his own feet that the troubler of Israel had been right about everything, and the rain of the LORD was behind him.
