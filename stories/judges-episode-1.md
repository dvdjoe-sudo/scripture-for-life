Judges Episode 1: A Generation That Did Not Know
Judges 1 to 2, Berean Standard Bible

The book of Joshua closed with three burials and a generation that had seen everything. Judges opens with the words Joshua's ending held back. "After the death of Joshua, the Israelites inquired of the LORD," and their question was a soldier's question: "Who will be the first to go up and fight for us against the Canaanites?" The answer came back with a tribe's name and a promise: "Judah shall go up", answered the LORD. "Indeed, I have delivered the land into their hands."

Judah did not go alone. It invited Simeon, whose small inheritance lay inside Judah's own borders, with a trade of swords: come up with us to our territory, and we likewise will go with you to yours. The partnership worked. At Bezek they struck down ten thousand Canaanites and Perizzites, and there they caught the king whose name the place bore, Adoni-bezek, lord of Bezek. He fled, they pursued, and when they seized him they cut off his thumbs and big toes, the mutilation that ends a warrior and a king, no grip left for a sword and no footing left to stand in battle. And the prisoner read his own sentence as justice, in words the text preserves whole: "Seventy kings with their thumbs and big toes cut off have gathered the scraps under my table. As I have done to them, so God has repaid me." Seventy kings had once eaten scraps under this man's table with their hands maimed. They brought him to Jerusalem, where he died.

Judah fought Jerusalem itself and captured it, put it to the sword, and set it on fire. Then the army marched out across its whole allotment, hill country, Negev, and foothills, and at Hebron it struck down the same three names the reader met when Caleb claimed the city: Sheshai, Ahiman, and Talmai, the sons of Anak. Judges retells Caleb's story here rather than assuming it, because the young man in it matters to this book. Caleb offered his daughter Achsah to whoever took Debir; his nephew Othniel took it; Achsah rode to her father and asked, "Give me a blessing". "Since you have given me land in the Negev, give me springs of water as well." Caleb gave her the upper springs and the lower springs. Keep Othniel's name. He is about to become the first judge.

The victories ran on. Moses' father-in-law's descendants, the Kenites, came up with Judah from the City of Palms and settled in the Negev near Arad. Judah and Simeon together devoted Zephath to destruction and renamed it Hormah, destruction itself. Judah captured Gaza, Ashkelon, and Ekron on the coast, each with its territory.

Then the book turns, and the turn is one honest sentence long: "The LORD was with Judah, and they took possession of the hill country; but they could not drive out the inhabitants of the plains because they had chariots of iron." The LORD was with them, and the chariots were still there. On the plains where iron decided things, the strongest tribe in Israel stopped. Joshua had told the house of Joseph, facing the same chariots, that they could drive them out. Judges records that Judah could not, and moves on without explaining how both stand. Both stand.

And Jerusalem, the city Judah had just burned? "The Benjamites, however, failed to drive out the Jebusites living in Jerusalem. So to this day the Jebusites live there among the Benjamites." The city lay in Benjamin's allotment, not Judah's, and a captured city is not a kept city. The Jebusites outlasted the fire and lived in Jerusalem for generations more, among the tribe that held the deed.

The house of Joseph attacked Bethel, formerly Luz, and the story there repeats the Jericho pattern with the roles thinned down to one man. Their spies caught a man leaving the city and made the old bargain: "Please show us how to get into the city, and we will treat you kindly." He showed them the entrance. The city fell to the sword, and the man and all his family were released, exactly as promised. He went to the land of the Hittites, built a city, and called it Luz, "which is its name to this day." A Canaanite city died in Canaan and was reborn under its own name somewhere else, because Israel kept its word to the one man who helped it. It is the last clean story of the chapter.

Understand what the compromise actually was, because the next chapter will name it. Forced labor is a treaty by another name: the Canaanite cities kept their walls, their fields, their altars, and their gods, and in exchange they worked. Israel got labor without obedience, income without inheritance, and neighbors whose shrines stood untouched a walk from its own doors. The command had been to tear the altars down. Labor contracts left every altar standing.

From there the chapter becomes a catalog, and the catalog is the point. Manasseh failed to drive out Beth-shean, Taanach, Dor, Ibleam, Megiddo, and their villages, "for the Canaanites were determined to dwell in that land." When Israel grew stronger it settled for forced labor, "but they never drove them out completely." Ephraim failed at Gezer, so the Canaanites lived among them in Gezer. Zebulun failed at Kitron and Nahalol; the Canaanites served as laborers and stayed. Asher failed along the whole northern coast, and the wording deepens: "So the Asherites lived among the Canaanite inhabitants of the land, because they did not drive them out." Read it twice. In Joshua the question was whether Canaanites would live among Israel. In Asher's allotment it is Israel living among Canaanites. Naphtali failed the same way. And Dan, the last name in the roll, is the only tribe the chapter shows losing ground: "The Amorites forced the Danites into the hill country and did not allow them to come down into the plain." The tribe that had marched out to take an inheritance was pushed up into the hills and penned there.

Then a messenger arrived who had not been seen since Egypt. The angel of the LORD went up from Gilgal, where the stones stood, to a place that got its name from this day, and he spoke God's charge against the whole nation: "I brought you up out of Egypt and led you into the land that I had promised to your fathers," and I said, "I will never break My covenant with you, and you are not to make a covenant with the people of this land, but you shall tear down their altars." Then the indictment, one line: "Yet you have not obeyed My voice. What is this you have done?"

The verdict reversed the conquest's direction. "So now I tell you that I will not drive out these people before you; they will be thorns in your sides, and their gods will be a snare to you." Joshua, dying, had warned Israel that the nations left among them would become exactly this, a snare and a trap, thorns in their eyes, if Israel clung to them. The warning had come due. "When the angel of the LORD had spoken these words to all the Israelites, the people lifted up their voices and wept." They named the place Bochim, Weeping, and offered sacrifices there. It is a real grief, and the book will test whether grief without obedience changes anything.

The narrator then reaches back and buries Joshua a second time, as if the first burial at the end of his own book were not enough to close the account. "the people served the LORD throughout the days of Joshua and of the elders who outlived him, who had seen all the great works that the LORD had done for Israel." Joshua died at 110, "the servant of the LORD," and was buried at Timnath-heres, the same inheritance town that Joshua's book spelled Timnath-serah. And then the sentence the entire book of Judges exists to explain: "After that whole generation had also been gathered to their fathers, another generation rose up who did not know the LORD or the works that He had done for Israel."

They did not know. The stones at Gilgal were built for exactly this, so that children would ask and fathers would answer. Within one generation the asking stopped working, and the narrator lays out the machine that would run for the rest of the book, piece by piece, like a diagram.

First the turn: "And the Israelites did evil in the sight of the LORD and served the Baals." The Baals were the storm and fertility gods of the land Israel now shared, and the Ashtoreths beside them, and the pull was agricultural and ordinary, rain and crops and the neighbors' shrines still standing because their altars had never been torn down. "Thus they forsook the LORD, the God of their fathers, who had brought them out of the land of Egypt, and they followed after various gods of the peoples around them."

Then the consequence, in the language of a market: God's anger burned, and "He delivered them into the hands of those who plundered them. He sold them into the hands of their enemies all around, whom they were no longer able to resist." The nation that had crossed the Jordan against chariots now could not stand anywhere: "Wherever Israel marched out, the hand of the LORD was against them to bring calamity, just as He had sworn to them. So they were greatly distressed."

Then the rescue, which is not earned and not explained by anything in Israel: "Then the LORD raised up judges, who saved them from the hands of those who plundered them." The judges were deliverers raised up one crisis at a time, and God was with each of them, the text says, because "the LORD was moved to pity by their groaning under those who oppressed them and afflicted them." Groaning moved Him, as it had in Egypt.

And then the relapse, the piece that makes the machine a downward spiral instead of a circle: "Israel, however, did not listen to their judges. Instead, they prostituted themselves with other gods and bowed down to them." And at each judge's death, "But when the judge died, the Israelites became even more corrupt than their fathers, going after other gods to serve them and bow down to them. They would not give up their evil practices and stubborn ways." Even more corrupt than their fathers. Every turn of the cycle lands lower than the last, and the deliverers raised up inside it will show the same slide, from the clean Othniel to men Israel would barely recognize as heroes.

God's closing word in the chapter turns the leftover nations into an instrument: "Because this nation has transgressed the covenant I laid down for their fathers and has not heeded My voice, I will no longer drive out before them any of the nations Joshua left when he died. In this way I will test whether Israel will keep the way of the LORD by walking in it as their fathers did." That is why the nations remained, the narrator finishes, and why they had not been handed to Joshua all at once.

The machine is assembled. The nations left in the land have names and kings, and the first of them is already gathering an army in the east. What Israel needs is a deliverer, and the first one it gets is the young man from Debir who married Caleb's daughter, the last clean figure of Joshua's generation still standing.
