Genesis Episode 8: Jacob Runs, Dreams, and Wrestles
Genesis 28 to 33, Berean Standard Bible

Jacob left home with Esau behind him and Haran ahead of him, and the first night found him sleeping under open sky.

He came to a certain place and stopped for the night because the sun had set. He took one of the stones from that place, put it under his head, and lay down to sleep. Then Jacob dreamed. A stairway rested on the earth, and its top reached to heaven. The angels of God were ascending and descending on it. The LORD stood above it and spoke the promise Jacob had taken by deceit, only now God gave it directly to Jacob.

God promised the land on which Jacob lay to Jacob and his offspring. His offspring would be like the dust of the earth and would spread out to the west, east, north, and south. In Jacob and in his seed all the families of the earth would be blessed. Then God spoke to the runaway himself. "Look, I am with you, and I will watch over you wherever you go, and I will bring you back to this land. For I will not leave you until I have done what I have promised you."

Jacob woke and knew the place had changed for him. He had thought he was alone on the road, and he said the LORD was in that place and he had not known it. He was afraid. He called the place the house of God and the gate of heaven. Early in the morning he took the stone that had been under his head, set it up as a pillar, and poured oil on top of it. He named the place Bethel, though the city had been called Luz. Jacob made a vow. If God would be with him, watch over him, give him food and clothing, and bring him back in peace to his father's house, then the LORD would be his God. The stone pillar would be God's house, and Jacob would give God a tenth of all God gave him.

Jacob continued on his journey and came to the land of the people of the east. A well stood in the field with a large stone over its mouth. Flocks gathered there, but shepherds waited until all the flocks came before rolling the stone away. Jacob asked the shepherds where they were from. They said Haran. They knew Laban, the grandson of Nahor, and they pointed to Rachel, Laban's daughter, coming with the sheep.

While Jacob was still speaking with them, Rachel arrived with her father's sheep, for she was a shepherdess. Jacob went up and rolled the stone from the mouth of the well and watered his uncle Laban's flock. Then Jacob kissed Rachel and began to weep aloud. He told her he was her father's relative, Rebekah's son. Rachel ran and told her father.

Laban hurried out to meet Jacob, embraced him, kissed him, and brought him to his house. Jacob told Laban all that had happened. After Jacob had stayed with him a full month, Laban told him he should not serve for nothing just because he was a relative. Jacob was to name his wages.

Laban had two daughters. Leah was the older. Rachel was younger, lovely in form and beautiful. Jacob loved Rachel, and he offered to serve Laban seven years for her. Laban agreed that it was better to give Rachel to Jacob than to another man. Jacob served seven years for Rachel, and they seemed like a few days to him because of the love he had for her.

When the seven years were complete, Jacob asked Laban to give him his wife. Laban gathered all the men of the place and made a feast. In the evening he took Leah his daughter and brought her to Jacob, and Jacob went in to her. Laban gave his servant Zilpah to Leah as her maidservant. In the morning, Jacob discovered it was Leah.

Jacob confronted Laban for deceiving him. He had served for Rachel. Laban answered that in their country the younger daughter was not given before the firstborn. Jacob was to finish Leah's bridal week, and then Rachel would also be given to him if he served another seven years. Jacob did so. Laban gave Rachel to him as wife, with Bilhah as her maidservant, and Jacob loved Rachel more than Leah.

When the LORD saw that Leah was unloved, He opened her womb, while Rachel remained barren. Leah bore a son and named him Reuben, because the LORD had looked on her affliction. She conceived again and bore Simeon. She conceived again and bore Levi. She conceived again and bore Judah, saying this time she would praise the LORD. Then she stopped bearing children for a time.

Rachel saw that she bore Jacob no children, and she envied her sister. She told Jacob to give her children, or she would die. Jacob's anger burned against Rachel, and he answered that he was not in the place of God, who had withheld children from her. Rachel gave him Bilhah, her servant, so Bilhah could bear children on Rachel's behalf and Rachel could build a family through her. Bilhah bore Jacob a son, and Rachel named him Dan, saying God had vindicated her. Bilhah bore a second son, and Rachel named him Naphtali.

Leah saw that she had stopped bearing, so she gave Zilpah, her servant, to Jacob. Zilpah bore Gad. Zilpah bore a second son, Asher. During the wheat harvest, Reuben found mandrakes in the field and brought them to Leah. Rachel asked Leah for some of the mandrakes. Leah answered that Rachel had taken her husband, and now she wanted her son's mandrakes too. Rachel offered Jacob to Leah for that night in return. Leah met Jacob when he came in from the field and told him he must come to her, because she had hired him with the mandrakes. God listened to Leah. She bore Issachar. She bore Zebulun. Afterward she bore a daughter, Dinah.

Then God remembered Rachel. God listened to her and opened her womb. She conceived and bore a son, Joseph. Rachel said God had taken away her disgrace, and she named him Joseph, saying the LORD would add another son to her.

After Rachel had borne Joseph, Jacob asked Laban to send him away to his own place and country. He had served for his wives and children, and Laban knew the service Jacob had given him. Laban asked him to stay. He had learned that the LORD blessed him because of Jacob. They set new wages. Jacob would take every speckled and spotted sheep, every dark lamb, and every spotted goat as his pay. Laban agreed, but that same day he removed the striped and spotted male goats, the speckled and spotted female goats, and the dark lambs, and put them in the care of his sons. He put a three-day journey between himself and Jacob.

Jacob still prospered. Genesis says he took fresh rods from poplar, almond, and plane trees, peeled white stripes in them, and set the rods in the watering troughs where the flocks came to drink and mate. The flocks bore striped, speckled, and spotted young. Jacob separated the animals and used the stronger flocks for his plan, while Laban's weaker animals stayed Laban's. Jacob grew exceedingly prosperous and came to own many flocks, female and male servants, camels, and donkeys.

Then Jacob heard the words of Laban's sons, who said Jacob had taken all that belonged to their father and gained his wealth from it. Jacob saw that Laban's attitude toward him had changed. The LORD told Jacob to return to the land of his fathers and to his relatives, and God would be with him.

Jacob sent for Rachel and Leah in the field where his flock was. He told them their father's attitude had changed, but the God of his father had been with him. He told them Laban had cheated him and changed his wages ten times, yet God had not allowed Laban to harm him. In a dream during breeding season, the angel of God had shown Jacob that the rams were striped, speckled, and mottled, and had called Jacob to leave: "I am the God of Bethel, where you anointed the pillar and made a solemn vow to Me. Now get up, leave this land at once, and return to your native land."

Rachel and Leah answered that their father had treated them like foreigners. He had sold them and used up what was paid for them. All the wealth God had taken from Laban belonged to Jacob and their children. They told Jacob to do all God had said.

Jacob put his children and wives on camels and drove all his livestock ahead of him, all the property he had accumulated in Paddan-aram, to go to Isaac his father in Canaan. While Laban had gone to shear his sheep, Rachel stole her father's household gods. Jacob deceived Laban the Aramean by not telling him he was fleeing. They crossed the Euphrates and set out for the hill country of Gilead.

Laban learned of the flight on the third day and pursued with his relatives. After seven days he caught up with Jacob in Gilead. But God came to Laban in a dream by night and warned him not to say anything to Jacob, either good or bad.

Laban confronted Jacob for leaving secretly, carrying away his daughters like captives taken in war, and stealing his gods. Jacob answered the charge of flight with fear. He had been afraid Laban would take his daughters by force. As for the gods, Jacob said whoever was found with them should not live, unaware that Rachel had taken them. Laban searched Jacob's tent, Leah's tent, and the tents of the two maidservants. He came to Rachel's tent. Rachel had put the household gods in the camel's saddle and sat on them. She told her father she could not rise before him because the way of women was upon her. Laban searched and did not find them.

Jacob's anger burned. He recounted twenty years under Laban: fourteen years for Laban's two daughters and six years for his flock. His wages had been changed ten times. Jacob had borne the loss of animals torn by beasts. In the day heat consumed him, and in the night the frost, and sleep fled from his eyes. If the God of Abraham and the Fear of Isaac had not been with Jacob, Laban would have sent him away empty-handed.

Laban claimed the daughters, children, and flocks were his, yet he proposed a covenant so he would not harm Jacob and Jacob would not harm him. Jacob set up a stone pillar. The heap and pillar were named witness between them. The place was called Mizpah, because Laban said the LORD would watch between them while they were absent from one another. Early the next morning Laban kissed his grandchildren and daughters, blessed them, and returned home.

Jacob went on his way, and angels of God met him. He called the place Mahanaim, two camps. From there he sent messengers ahead to his brother Esau, in the land of Seir, the country of Edom. The messengers returned with a report that tightened Jacob's whole body around the old danger. Esau was coming to meet Jacob, and four hundred men were with him.

Jacob was greatly afraid and distressed. He divided the people with him, the flocks, herds, and camels, into two camps. If Esau attacked one camp, the other might escape. Then Jacob prayed. He called on the God of Abraham and the God of Isaac. He reminded God of the command to return and of the promise that his offspring would be like the sand of the sea, which cannot be counted. Jacob said he was unworthy of all the kindness and faithfulness God had shown him. With only his staff he had crossed the Jordan, and now he had become two camps. He asked God to deliver him from the hand of Esau.

That night Jacob prepared a gift for his brother: goats, sheep, camels, cows, bulls, and donkeys, arranged in droves with space between them. Servants went ahead in waves. Each servant was to tell Esau the animals were a gift from his servant Jacob and that Jacob was coming behind them. Jacob hoped to pacify Esau with the gift going before him, and afterward see his face. Perhaps Esau would accept him.

Jacob sent his family over the ford of the Jabbok and remained alone on the other side.

A man wrestled with Jacob until the breaking of the day. When the man saw that he did not prevail against Jacob, he touched Jacob's hip socket, and Jacob's hip was put out of joint as he wrestled. The man asked to be released because the day was breaking. Jacob answered that he would not let go unless he was blessed.

The man asked Jacob's name, and Jacob gave it. Then the man said, "Your name will no longer be Jacob, but Israel, because you have struggled with God and with men, and you have prevailed."

Jacob asked for the man's name, but the man asked why Jacob asked and blessed him there. Jacob called the place Peniel, because he had seen God face to face, and yet his life had been preserved. The sun rose above him as he passed Penuel, and he was limping because of his hip. Genesis adds that to this day the Israelites do not eat the tendon of the hip socket, because the man touched Jacob's hip socket there.

Jacob lifted his eyes and saw Esau coming with his four hundred men. He divided the children among Leah, Rachel, and the two maidservants. He placed the maidservants and their children in front, Leah and her children next, and Rachel and Joseph last. Jacob himself went on ahead and bowed to the ground seven times as he approached his brother.

"Esau, however, ran to him and embraced him, threw his arms around his neck, and kissed him. And they both wept."

Esau asked about the women and children. Jacob answered that they were the children God had graciously given his servant. The maidservants and their children approached and bowed down. Leah and her children approached and bowed down. Joseph and Rachel approached and bowed down. Esau asked about the droves he had met. Jacob said they were to find favor in Esau's sight. Esau said he already had plenty and told Jacob to keep what was his. Jacob urged him to accept, saying he had seen Esau's face as one sees the face of God, and Esau had received him favorably. Esau accepted the gift.

Esau offered to journey with Jacob or leave men with him. Jacob pointed to the children and the nursing flocks and herds. If they were driven hard for one day, the flocks would die. Esau should go ahead to Seir, and Jacob would lead on slowly, at the pace of the children and the livestock. Esau returned that day on his way to Seir. Jacob journeyed to Succoth, where he built a house for himself and booths for his livestock.

Then Jacob came safely to the city of Shechem in the land of Canaan. He camped within sight of the city and bought the plot of land where he had pitched his tent from the sons of Hamor, Shechem's father, for one hundred pieces of silver. There he erected an altar and called it El Elohe Israel.

Jacob was back in the land his grandfather had bought only as a burial field. This time he bought ground for his living household and set an altar on it.
