2 Kings Episode 10: The Quick Kings and the Foreign Altar
2 Kings 14-16, Berean Standard Bible

From here the book changes pace, and the change is the message: reigns that once filled chapters now get paragraphs, because there is less and less left to say. Judah first. Amaziah, Joash's son, “did what was right in the eyes of the LORD, but not as his father David had done”, and the grading itself has become a sliding scale. His reign holds one act of law worth pausing on. He executed his father's assassins but spared their sons, obeying the written limit: “Fathers must not be put to death for their children, and children must not be put to death for their fathers; each is to die for his own sin.” Then he beat Edom in the Valley of Salt, and the victory swelled him into challenging Israel: “Come, let us meet face to face,” he said. Jehoash of Israel answered with a fable sharp enough to be quoted whole: a thistle in Lebanon proposed marriage to a cedar, and a wild beast trampled the thistle. The moral he drew for Amaziah was plain: “You have indeed defeated Edom, and your heart has become proud. Glory in that and stay at home.” “Why should you stir up trouble so that you fall”, you and Judah with you? Amaziah would not listen. Judah was routed at Beth-shemesh, Amaziah was captured, and Jehoash broke down four hundred cubits of Jerusalem's wall and emptied the temple and palace treasuries. The king who had spared the assassins' sons outlived his conqueror by fifteen years and was himself killed by conspirators at Lachish.

Israel, meanwhile, had its longest and most prosperous reign of the whole sad sequence, and the book refuses to call it a success. Jeroboam, Jehoash's son, reigned forty-one years in Samaria, did evil like every king before him, and under him the borders swelled back to their Solomonic reach, from Lebo-hamath to the Sea of the Arabah. It happened, the text says, “according to the word that the LORD, the God of Israel, had spoken through His servant Jonah son of Amittai, the prophet from Gath-hepher.” Jonah's own book waits ahead in this series, in the prophets' run; here he is one line of credit in a king's record. The reason for the prosperity is given, and it is not the king: “the LORD saw that the affliction of the Israelites, both slave and free, was very bitter. There was no one to help Israel”, and He had not said He would blot out Israel's name, “He would not blot out the name of Israel from under heaven”, so He saved them by the hand of Jeroboam. Deliverance as an act of memory, delivered through a king who learned nothing from it.

When Jeroboam died, the north fell apart at the speed of its own violence. His son Zechariah reigned six months and was struck down in front of the people by Shallum, and with him ended the house of Jehu, at exactly the length the LORD had promised it: “Four generations of your sons will sit on the throne of Israel.” Shallum reigned one month before Menahem killed him, and Menahem announced his reign at Tiphsah, a town that would not open its gates: “he attacked Tiphsah and ripped open all the pregnant women.” The atrocity Elisha had wept over in Hazael now had an Israelite author. In Menahem's reign the shadow that will swallow the book first touched the land: Pul, king of Assyria, invaded, and Menahem bought him off with a thousand talents of silver taxed at fifty shekels from every wealthy man in Israel. Pekahiah followed his father for two years and was murdered by his own officer Pekah, and in Pekah's days the Assyrian came back as Tiglath-pileser and stopped accepting money. He took Ijon, Abel-beth-maacah, Janoah, Kedesh, Hazor, Gilead, and Galilee, all the land of Naphtali, “and he took the people as captives to Assyria.” The first deportation, a whole region carried off, and Hoshea closed the sequence by murdering Pekah. Six kings in this episode's Israel thread, four of them by assassination.

Judah's thread runs slower and in some ways worse. Azariah reigned fifty-two years in Jerusalem, did right, kept the high places, and spent his reign a leper in a separate house, “the LORD afflicted the king with leprosy until the day he died”, while his son Jotham governed. Jotham did right too, and rebuilt the Upper Gate of the temple. Then came Ahaz, and the book's verdict drops the sliding scale entirely: unlike David, he did not do right. “he walked in the ways of the kings of Israel and even sacrificed his son in the fire”, and “he sacrificed and burned incense on the high places, on the hills, and under every green tree.” Besieged by Aram and Israel together, Ahaz did what Menahem had done, at a higher price and in fuller submission. His message to Tiglath-pileser is the most abject sentence any king of Judah speaks in the book: “I am your servant and your son. Come up and save me from the hands of the kings of Aram and Israel, who are rising up against me.” Assyria came, destroyed Damascus, killed Rezin, and Ahaz went up to meet his rescuer in the conquered city, where he saw an altar he admired. He sent its measurements and plans home to Uriah the priest, who built the copy before the king returned. Ahaz offered on the new altar, moved the bronze altar of the LORD to its north side, demoted to a private oracle, and gave the order that defines his reign: “But I will use the bronze altar to seek guidance.” Then, on account of the king of Assyria, he cut up the temple's bronze stands, took the great Sea down off its oxen, removed the Sabbath canopy, and closed the royal entrance. Solomon's house was being dismantled piece by piece by David's own heir, as tribute furniture for a foreign throne. And Ahaz rested with his fathers, and his son Hezekiah reigned in his place, the book's next great surprise waiting in a single sentence.
