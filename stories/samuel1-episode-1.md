1 Samuel Episode 1: Hannah, and the Boy Who Heard God
1 Samuel 1 to 3, Berean Standard Bible

Ruth's story ended in Bethlehem with a genealogy. This book opens a few miles north, in the hill country of Ephraim, in a house divided against itself by love and arithmetic. Elkanah had two wives. Peninnah had children. "And Peninnah had children, but Hannah had none", and the text lays the cause where Hannah herself laid it: the LORD had closed her womb. Year after year the family went up to Shiloh to worship the LORD of Hosts at the sanctuary, where Eli the priest served and his two sons, Hophni and Phinehas, served as priests. Year after year Elkanah gave Hannah a double portion of the sacrifice, because he loved her, and year after year her rival provoked her viciously over the children she did not have, until she wept and could not eat. Her husband tried to comfort her with the worst comforter's line in the Bible, kind and useless at once: "Am I not better to you than ten sons?"

One year at Shiloh, after the meal, Hannah stood up and went to pray, and what she did in the sanctuary was new enough that the priest misunderstood it completely. She prayed in her heart. Her lips moved and no sound came out, because the prayer was not performance, it was a negotiation conducted in tears: "O LORD of Hosts, if only You will look upon the affliction of Your maidservant and remember me, not forgetting Your maidservant but giving her a son, then I will dedicate him to the LORD all the days of his life, and no razor shall ever touch his head." A son for a son. Give me a boy and I will give him back, whole, for life, marked as a Nazirite from birth. Eli, watching her mouth from his chair by the doorpost, concluded she was drunk and rebuked her for bringing wine into the sanctuary, and Hannah's answer has the dignity of a woman who has been misread by everyone: "I am a woman troubled in spirit. I have not had any wine or strong drink, but I have poured out my soul before the LORD." And she added: "for all this time I have been praying out of the depth of my anguish and grief." Eli believed her, and blessed her, and sent her away with the words she had come for: "Go in peace," Eli replied, "and may the God of Israel grant the petition you have asked of Him." She went and ate, and her face was no longer downcast, before a single fact of her life had changed.

The facts changed at home in Ramah. "Elkanah had relations with his wife Hannah, and the LORD remembered her", and she conceived, and bore a son, and named him Samuel, saying, "Because I have asked for him from the LORD." Remembered is one of the Bible's great quiet words; it is what God did for Noah in the ark and for Rachel in her own closed womb, and it never means He had forgotten. It means the waiting turned. But Hannah's vow had a second half, and she kept it with both hands. She did not go up to Shiloh again until the boy was weaned, until she could take him and leave him. Then she brought him, with a bull and flour and wine, to the old priest who had once accused her of drunkenness, and she reintroduced herself by her answered prayer, and gave the boy away in one sentence: "I prayed for this boy, and since the LORD has granted me what I asked of Him, I now dedicate the boy to the LORD. For as long as he lives, he is dedicated to the LORD."

Before she left him there, she prayed again, and this time it came out as a song, and the song is far bigger than her nursery. Hannah sang of a God who reverses everything: "My heart rejoices in the LORD; my horn is exalted in the LORD." "There is no one holy like the LORD. Indeed, there is no one besides You! And there is no Rock like our God." And then the reversals roll: "The bows of the mighty are broken, but the feeble are equipped with strength." "The LORD brings death and gives life; He brings down to Sheol and raises up." "He raises the poor from the dust and lifts the needy from the ash heap. He seats them among princes and bestows on them a throne of honor." The barren woman sings it as theology, not just testimony, the whole world's order turned by a God who lifts the low. And at the very end her song does something astonishing. It looks clean past her own story, past Eli, past everything in Israel at that moment, and lands on a figure who does not exist yet: God "will give power to His king. He will exalt the horn of His anointed." A king. An anointed one. Israel had no king, had not asked for one, and the first mention of God's anointed in this book comes out of the mouth of a formerly barren woman leaving her toddler at the sanctuary. The whole two books of Samuel are inside that line, sung before any of it happened.

At Shiloh, the song's world and the sanctuary's world collided daily, because the boy now lived inside the contrast the rest of the chapters are built on. On one side, Samuel: "the boy began ministering to the LORD before Eli the priest", a child in a linen ephod, and each year his mother made him a little robe and brought it up with the annual sacrifice, and the boy grew, in the text's repeated phrase, in the presence of the LORD. On the other side, the priest's own sons, and the text does not hedge: "Now the sons of Eli were wicked men; they had no regard for the LORD". Their racket was the sacrifice itself. A servant ran a three-pronged fork into the boiling pot and the priests kept whatever it brought up, and before the fat was even burned, the fat that belonged to God, they demanded raw meat by threat: give it now, or it will be taken by force. "Thus the sin of these young men was severe in the sight of the LORD, for they were treating the LORD’s offering with contempt." They were also sleeping with the women who served at the entrance of the Tent of Meeting. Eli heard all of it. Eli was very old. Eli rebuked them the way tired men rebuke grown sons, with a question and a proverb: "but if a man sins against the LORD, who can intercede for him?" They did not listen, and the narrator adds, in one of the coldest asides in the book, that they would not listen "since the LORD intended to put them to death."

Judgment, when it came to Eli's house, came first by a stranger. A man of God arrived with a message that reviewed the whole history of the priesthood, the honor of being chosen, and then turned: "You have honored your sons more than Me by fattening yourselves with the best of all the offerings of My people Israel." That is the charge against Eli himself, not theft, not lust, but priority: he honored his sons more than God, and fed on the proceeds. The verdict revoked a promise and laid down a principle in its place: "For I will honor those who honor Me, but those who despise Me will be disdained." His house would be cut down, no old men in it, and the sign would be brutally specific: of Hophni and Phinehas, "They will both die on the same day." Inside the same judgment sat a promise the book will spend chapters fulfilling: "Then I will raise up for Myself a faithful priest. He will do whatever is in My heart and mind. And I will build for him an enduring house, and he will walk before My anointed one for all time." The anointed again. Even the sentence on the old order ends looking at the king to come.

And then, in the middle of that hushed and corrupt sanctuary, God started speaking to the boy. "Now in those days the word of the LORD was rare, and visions were scarce", and the scene is set like a held breath: Eli nearly blind in his room, and Samuel lying down in the temple near the ark of God, before the lamp of God had gone out. Three times the voice called his name, and three times the boy ran to Eli, because he did not yet know the LORD, and three times the old man sent him back. It was Eli, the failing priest under judgment, who finally understood what was happening in his own sanctuary and taught the boy the sentence that would govern his whole life. If He calls you again, Eli said, answer Him: "Speak, LORD, for Your servant is listening." The fourth time, the LORD came and stood there, and called the name twice, and Samuel answered with a servant's grammar he kept for sixty years: "Speak, for Your servant is listening."

His first message as a prophet was the judgment on the house of the man who had taught him how to answer. God told him the sentence on Eli would be carried out from beginning to end, because Eli knew what his sons did and did not restrain them, and He sealed it with words no boy should have to carry to breakfast: "The iniquity of Eli’s house shall never be atoned for by sacrifice or offering." Samuel lay awake until morning and opened the doors of the sanctuary as usual and was afraid to speak. Eli made him tell it, all of it, swearing him to hide nothing, and then the old man did the finest thing he does in this book. He heard his own house's death sentence from the lips of a child, and he bowed to it: "He is the LORD," replied Eli. "Let Him do what is good in His eyes."

The word of the LORD had been rare in Israel. By the end of the chapter it had a voice the whole country could name: "And Samuel grew, and the LORD was with him, and He let none of Samuel’s words fall to the ground. So all Israel from Dan to Beersheba knew that Samuel was confirmed as a prophet of the LORD." But the boy was still serving at Shiloh, in Eli's house, beside Eli's sons, and the sign God had given was both brothers dead on the same day. The Philistines were about to provide the day.
