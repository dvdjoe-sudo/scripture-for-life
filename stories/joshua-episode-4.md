Joshua Episode 4: The North Taken, and the List of Kings
Joshua 11 to 12, Berean Standard Bible

The south had fallen fast. The north would not. When Jabin king of Hazor heard what had happened, he built the largest coalition in the book: Jobab king of Madon, the kings of Shimron and Achshaph, the kings of the northern mountains and the Arabah and the foothills and Naphoth-dor, Canaanites east and west, Amorites, Hittites, Perizzites, Jebusites in the hill country, and Hivites at the foot of Hermon. Hazor was "formerly the head of all these kingdoms", and its king could summon a continent. What came out to meet Israel at the waters of Merom was "a multitude as numerous as the sand on the seashore, along with a great number of horses and chariots." The chariots matter. Israel had crossed the Jordan on foot; the north had the war technology of the age, and it had more of it than anyone could count.

God's word to Joshua the night before the battle carried a deadline and a strange order: "Do not be afraid of them, for by this time tomorrow I will deliver all of them slain before Israel. You are to hamstring their horses and burn up their chariots." Hamstring and burn. Israel was not to capture the north's military machine and keep it. The weapons that could have made Israel a power like its neighbors were to be destroyed on the field, by command.

Joshua attacked suddenly at Merom, and the coalition broke. The pursuit ran west "all the way to Greater Sidon and Misrephoth-maim, and eastward as far as the Valley of Mizpeh", and the text closes the battle the way it has closed them all: "They struck them down, leaving no survivors." Then Joshua did the strange order exactly: "Joshua treated them as the LORD had told him; he hamstrung their horses and burned up their chariots."

He turned back and took Hazor itself, put its king to the sword, and devoted everyone in it to destruction. "Nothing that breathed remained, and Joshua burned down Hazor itself." Of all the cities built on their mounds, Hazor alone was burned; the rest Israel occupied and plundered. The narrator pauses the campaign to certify the whole chain of command, and the sentence is the book's own audit of its hero: "As the LORD had commanded His servant Moses, so Moses commanded Joshua. That is what Joshua did, leaving nothing undone of all that the LORD had commanded Moses."

The summary that follows is careful with the map and with the calendar. The region taken ran from Mount Halak toward Seir in the south to Baal-gad in the Valley of Lebanon under Mount Hermon in the north, the hill country, the Negev, Goshen, the foothills, the Arabah, the mountains. And then, in one plain line that corrects any impression the southern blitz left: "Joshua waged war against all these kings for a long period of time." One campaign in the south. A long war in the north. The book keeps both tempos on the record.

It also records why no other city did what Gibeon had done: "No city made peace with the Israelites except the Hivites living in Gibeon; all others were taken in battle." And it gives the reason in its own theological voice, without flinching from it: "For it was of the LORD to harden their hearts to engage Israel in battle, so that they would be set apart for destruction and would receive no mercy, being annihilated as the LORD had commanded Moses." The Gibeonites feared and came with moldy bread. The rest hardened and came with chariots. The book sees God's hand in the difference, and says so.

One more enemy falls in this chapter, and they are the oldest fear in the story. The Anakim, the giants whose size melted the spies' hearts two generations earlier, were eliminated from the hill country of Hebron, Debir, and Anab, and from all the hill country of Judah and Israel. "No Anakim were left in the land of the Israelites; only in Gaza, Gath, and Ashdod did any survive", three Philistine cities on the coast, where a remnant of the giants kept a foothold the book leaves for later generations to meet.

Then comes the sentence the whole first half of Joshua has been driving toward: "So Joshua took the entire land, in keeping with all that the LORD had spoken to Moses. And Joshua gave it as an inheritance to Israel according to the allotments to their tribes. Then the land had rest from war."

Chapter 12 is the monument to that rest, and it is built like the piles of stones at Gilgal: a list meant to be read as evidence. It begins east of the Jordan, with Moses' two kings. Sihon of Heshbon, whose territory ran from Aroer on the Arnon up to the Jabbok, half of Gilead, the Arabah down to the Salt Sea below Pisgah. And Og of Bashan, "one of the remnant of the Rephaim," ruling Hermon and Salecah and all Bashan to the borders of the Geshurites. Their land was already given away, to Reuben, Gad, and the half-tribe of Manasseh.

Then the roll of the west, thirty-one names, each with its single word: the king of Jericho, one; the king of Ai, one; the king of Jerusalem, one; the king of Hebron, one; Jarmuth, one; Lachish, one; Eglon, one; Gezer, one; Debir, one; Geder, one; Hormah, one; Arad, one; Libnah, one; Adullam, one; Makkedah, one; Bethel, one; Tappuah, one; Hepher, one; Aphek, one; Lasharon, one; Madon, one; Hazor, one; Shimron-meron, one; Achshaph, one; Taanach, one; Megiddo, one; Kedesh, one; Jokneam in Carmel, one; the king of Dor, one; the king of Goiim in Gilgal, one; and Tirzah, one. The list ends with its own count: "So there were thirty-one kings in all."

Read the roll slowly and it is a map and a memorial at once, city by city, the whole west bank measured in fallen thrones. But rest from war is not the same as a finished inheritance. The kings were dead; most of the land had not yet been divided, occupied, or even entered. And the next voice in the book is God's, speaking to an old man about everything that remains.
