Deuteronomy Episode 5: Life in the Land, Case by Case
Deuteronomy 21 to 25, Berean Standard Bible

The speech now left the courts, the king, and the army behind and came down to the size of a field, a roof, a day's wage. Moses opened with a corpse.

"If one is found slain, lying in a field in the land that the LORD your God is giving you to possess, and it is not known who killed him," the elders and judges were to come out and measure. The town nearest the body took the case, whether it wanted it or not. Its elders brought a heifer that had never been yoked or worked to a valley with running water, ground never plowed or sown, and broke its neck there by the stream. The priests stood by. Then the elders washed their hands over the heifer and spoke their oath aloud: "Our hands did not shed this blood, nor did our eyes see it." And they prayed the prayer Moses gave them: "Accept this atonement, O LORD, for Your people Israel whom You have redeemed, and do not hold the shedding of innocent blood against them." An unsolved murder was not filed away. It was answered for, by the nearest town, in public, until the guilt of innocent blood was purged from the land.

The cases that followed ran the whole width of a life. A beautiful captive taken in war was to be given a full month in her captor's house to mourn her father and mother, her head shaved and her captivity clothes put aside, before she could become a wife. And if the man later lost his delight in her, he could not sell her or treat her as a slave. She went free, "since you have dishonored her."

Inheritance law protected the son nobody would have picked. A man with two wives, one beloved and one unloved, whose firstborn came from the unloved wife, could not hand the firstborn's double portion to the loved wife's son. "For that son is the firstfruits of his father's strength; the right of the firstborn belongs to him." A father's feelings did not rewrite a son's rights.

The hardest household case stood next. A stubborn and rebellious son, who would not obey his father and mother or listen when disciplined, was to be taken by both parents together to the elders at the gate. Their charge stands in the text: "This son of ours is stubborn and rebellious; he does not obey us. He is a glutton and a drunkard." Then all the men of the city stoned him, and the refrain of this section of the speech sounded for the fourth time: purge the evil from among you, and all Israel will hear and be afraid. And a body hung on a tree for a capital sin was never to hang overnight, "because anyone who is hung on a tree is under God's curse". Bury him that day, Moses said, and do not defile the land.

Chapter 22 opened with a sentence Moses repeated until it sounded like a drumbeat: you must not ignore it. A brother's straying ox or sheep was to be brought home, not admired and left. If the owner was unknown or far away, the animal lived at the finder's house until its owner came looking, and the same held for a donkey, a cloak, "or anything your brother has lost and you have found." A donkey or ox fallen on the road was to be lifted, with its owner's help, not passed by. A lost animal, a fallen animal, a lost cloak: the law's eye kept landing on the moment a man decides his neighbor's trouble is not his business, and forbidding the decision.

Small laws followed, each with a world inside it. A woman was not to wear men's clothing, nor a man women's, for whoever does these things is detestable to the LORD. A bird's nest found along the road could be taken for its young or its eggs, but the mother was to be let go: "be sure to let the mother go, so that it may be well with you and that you may prolong your days." Even a nest on the ground had an owner whose future the law guarded. A new house needed a railing around its roof, "so that you do not bring bloodguilt on your house if someone falls from it." The vineyard was not to be sown with two kinds of seed, nor an ox and a donkey yoked together, nor wool and linen woven into one garment, and tassels were to hang on the four corners of a man's cloak, the same tassels the wilderness generation had worn.

The rest of chapter 22 dealt with marriage and its violations, and Moses set the cases down plainly. A husband who slandered his bride's name, claiming she had not been a virgin, faced her parents and their proof before the elders; if the charge was a lie, he was punished, fined a hundred shekels of silver to her father, and kept the wife he had shamed, with no right to divorce her as long as he lived. If the charge was true, the woman died at her own father's door. A man found with another man's wife died, and so did the woman. The betrothed girl taken in the city, where a cry would have been heard, died with the man; the betrothed girl overpowered in the open country was guilty of nothing, for "the betrothed woman cried out, but there was no one to save her". Only the man died, her case weighed the same as a murder victim's. A virgin not pledged who was seized was to become the man's wife, her father paid fifty shekels, and that man too lost the right of divorce for life. No invention, no easing: the chapter graded harm by evidence, consent, and power, in a world with no police but the gate.

Chapter 23 drew the boundary of the assembly itself. Some were barred from it by birth or by injury, and Moab and Ammon were barred to the tenth generation, for a reason the story had already told: they met Israel on the road with no food and no water, and they hired Balaam to curse. But the exclusion carried its own memory inside it: "the LORD your God turned the curse into a blessing for you, because the LORD your God loves you." Edom, the brother nation, and Egypt, the old slave house, were treated more gently. "Do not despise an Egyptian, because you lived as a foreigner in his land." By the third generation, their children could enter.

The war camp itself was to be kept holy, down to a spade in a soldier's kit and a place outside the camp, covered over, because "the LORD your God walks throughout your camp to protect you and deliver your enemies to you". A slave who escaped to Israel was not to be handed back to his master; he was to live where he chose, unoppressed, in the town of his choosing. No son or daughter of Israel was to be a shrine prostitute, and no prostitute's wages could pay a vow in God's house. A brother was not to be charged interest on money, food, or anything lent. A vow once spoken was not to be slow in payment, though a vow never made carried no guilt at all. And a man walking through his neighbor's vineyard could eat his fill of grapes where he stood, so long as none went into his basket; in the grainfield he could pluck heads by hand, but the sickle stayed off his neighbor's grain.

Chapter 24 opened with the divorce case, and gave it in full legal dress: a man who finds some indecency in his wife writes her a certificate of divorce, hands it to her, and sends her from his house; if she remarries and that marriage ends by divorce or death, the first husband may never take her back, for that is an abomination to the LORD. Then the chapter turned, almost abruptly, to a wedding the law refused to interrupt. "If a man is newly married, he must not be sent to war or be pressed into any duty. For one year he is free to stay at home and bring joy to the wife he has married."

From there the cases tightened around the vulnerable. A millstone could not be taken as security for a debt, "because that would be taking one's livelihood as security." A man caught kidnapping an Israelite brother, to enslave or sell him, was to die. Skin diseases were to be handled exactly as the priests instructed, and Miriam's name was set down as the reminder of what pride cost on the road. A lender was not to walk into a borrower's house to seize the pledge; he stood outside, and the borrower brought it out. If the pledge was a poor man's cloak, it went back before sunset regardless of the debt: "be sure to return it to him by sunset, so that he may sleep in his own cloak and bless you". And the lender's restraint, not his generosity, was what heaven counted: this, Moses said, "will be credited to you as righteousness before the LORD your God".

Then came the wage law, among the most practical sentences in the whole speech. "Do not oppress a hired hand who is poor and needy, whether he is a brother or a foreigner residing in one of your towns. You are to pay his wages each day before sunset, because he is poor and depends on them." A day laborer has no reserves; his wage that night is his supper. Hold it back, Moses warned, "he may cry out to the LORD against you, and you will be guilty of sin."

Justice between generations was cut clean: "Fathers shall not be put to death for their children, nor children for their fathers; each is to die for his own sin." No justice was to be denied the foreigner or the fatherless, no widow's cloak taken as a pledge, and under both rulings ran the reason this book never lets go of: "Remember that you were slaves in Egypt, and the LORD your God redeemed you from that place."

The chapter closed in the harvest field. A sheaf forgotten in the field was not to be fetched back. The olives were beaten once, not gone over a second time; the vines were picked once, not stripped clean. What remained belonged to "the foreigner, the fatherless, and the widow," and the blessing promised for leaving it was the blessing on all the work of a man's hands. The law built its poor relief into the harvest itself: the margin of every field was, by statute, somebody else's supper.

Chapter 25 set the limits of the rod. A guilty man flogged in the judge's presence could receive no more than forty lashes, "lest your brother be beaten any more than that and be degraded in your sight." Even a condemned man stayed a brother. And an ox treading grain was not to be muzzled: the worker, animal or man, ate from the work under his feet.

Then the levirate case. When brothers lived together and one died without a son, his widow was not to marry outside the family; the surviving brother was to take her, and "the first son she bears will carry on the name of the dead brother, so that his name will not be blotted out from Israel." A man could refuse. But the refusal was public: the widow told the elders her charge, "My husband's brother refuses to preserve his brother's name in Israel", the elders reasoned with him, and if he held to his refusal he stood barefoot before them while she removed his sandal and spat in his face, with the sentence spoken over him: "This is what is done to the man who will not maintain his brother's line." His family's name in Israel, from that day, was "The House of the Unsandaled." A name kept for a dead brother was worth more to the law than a living man's pride. In a fight, a wife who rescued her husband by seizing his opponent's genitals lost her hand for it, with no pity shown; the law guarded even a brawler's body in the moment of the grapple.

The last laws of the section reached into a merchant's bag. "You shall not have two differing weights in your bag, one heavy and one light." Not two measures in the house, one large and one small, a big one for selling and a small one for buying. "You must maintain accurate and honest weights and measures, so that you may live long in the land that the LORD your God is giving you." Fraud at the scales was not sharp practice. "For everyone who behaves dishonestly in regard to these things is detestable to the LORD your God."

And the speech's long case list ended where Deuteronomy keeps ending, in memory. "Remember what the Amalekites did to you along your way from Egypt". Moses had not forgotten the first enemy Israel ever faced, the raiders who fell on the march at its weakest point: "how they met you on your journey when you were tired and weary, and they attacked all your stragglers; they had no fear of God." The weak at the back of the column, cut down by men who feared nothing. So when God gave Israel rest from the enemies around them in the land, one sentence stood waiting to be carried out: "you are to blot out the memory of Amalek from under heaven. Do not forget!"

A heifer by a stream for a killer nobody caught. A railing on a roof. A cloak returned by sunset. A wage paid before the sun went down. A sheaf left standing in the corner of a field. The cases were the covenant at the size of a household, and they rested, nearly all of them, on one sentence Moses had said so many times his hearers could say it with him: you were slaves in Egypt, and the LORD your God redeemed you. What came next was the part of the speech no one could file under daily life at all: firstfruits carried to the altar, two mountains facing each other across a valley, and a choice that would be read aloud to a nation.
