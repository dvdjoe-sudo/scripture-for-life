2 Samuel Episode 5: Kindness for Jonathan's Sake
2 Samuel 9 to 10, Berean Standard Bible

At the height of the kingdom, David asked a question that reached backward instead of forward: "Is there anyone left from the house of Saul to whom I can show kindness for the sake of Jonathan?" Kings in that world did not ask that question. Kings asked it as a targeting exercise, to find the surviving claimants and end them. David asked it as a debt. He had sworn a covenant to Jonathan at the stone Ezel and again in the wilderness of Ziph, kindness to Jonathan's house forever, and a covenant in these books is not a memory, it is a bill that comes due. A servant of Saul's house named Ziba was summoned, a steward of the old regime with fifteen sons and twenty servants of his own, and the king put the question to him in its full form: was there anyone left of the house of Saul to whom he could show the kindness of God? Ziba's answer was careful and small. There was still a son of Jonathan, he said, who was lame in both feet. He gave the disability before he gave the man.

The boy from the narrator's aside in the war years was in Lo-debar, a place whose name suggests nowhere, east of the Jordan, sheltered in the house of Machir son of Ammiel. He had been five when the news of Gilboa reached Jezreel and his nurse dropped him in the flight, and he had lived ever since as the hidden remnant of a fallen dynasty, which in that century meant living as a man waiting to be found by the wrong king. David had him brought. When Mephibosheth son of Jonathan, the son of Saul, came into the presence of the man who had replaced his grandfather, he fell facedown in reverence, in terror or submission or both, and David called him by name, and he answered as a man answering for his life. David's first words to him were the covenant paying out: "Do not be afraid," and then the promise itself: "for surely I will show you kindness for the sake of your father Jonathan. I will restore to you all the land of your grandfather Saul, and you will always eat at my table." Not a pension. A seat. Mephibosheth bowed and answered with the vocabulary of a man who had priced his own worth in hiding: "What is your servant, that you should show regard for a dead dog like me?" David did not argue with the valuation. He simply arranged the facts against it: Saul's whole estate restored, Ziba and his sons and servants ordered to farm it and bring in the harvest for their master's grandson, and Mephibosheth himself placed permanently at the king's table in Jerusalem, eating, the text says, like one of the king's own sons. The chapter says it twice, and ends by saying the quiet part last, as this book loves to do: he lived in Jerusalem, because he always ate at the king's table, and he was lame in both feet. Grace in this story does not fix the lameness. It seats the lame man at the table anyway, every day, for life. It is the purest act of David's reign, and the narrative will not let it rest there, because Ziba has now been introduced, with his twenty servants and his careful answers, and Ziba will be back.

Kindness across a border fared worse. The king of the Ammonites died, and David sent servants to console his son Hanun, because Nahash had once shown David kindness, another old debt honored. Hanun's princes read the embassy as espionage, comforters sent to spy out the city for overthrow, and Hanun chose the reading that humiliated Jerusalem: he seized David's envoys, shaved off half of each man's beard, cut their garments off at the hips, and sent them home in disgrace. In that culture a beard was a man's honor worn on his face, and the half-shaved, half-dressed envoys were a declaration of contempt meant to be unanswerable. David's handling of his shamed men is worth noticing in a book that will soon show his handling of other men's wives and lives: he sent word to meet them on the road and told them to stay in Jericho until their beards grew back, and then return. He absorbed the insult's cost himself rather than parade their shame through his capital.

The Ammonites knew what they had done and bought an army: twenty thousand Aramean foot soldiers from Beth-rehob and Zobah, a thousand from the king of Maacah, twelve thousand from Tob. Joab marched out with the whole army of the mighty men and found the enemy arranged in two forces, Ammonites at their city gate and Arameans in the open country, with Israel caught between. Joab split his command on the spot, taking the best of Israel against the Arameans and giving Abishai the rest against Ammon, and the brothers made the pact that defines them at their best: whichever front broke first, the other would come to its rescue. Then Joab gave the order that has been quoted by soldiers ever since, and it is worth hearing whole, because it is this book's theology of courage in one breath: "Be strong and let us fight bravely for our people and for the cities of our God. May the LORD do what is good in His sight." Effort at full stretch, outcome surrendered. The Arameans fled before Joab's charge; the Ammonites, seeing their hirelings run, fled before Abishai into their city; and the campaign ended, for the moment, with Joab back in Jerusalem.

It did not hold. The Arameans, shamed by the defeat, regrouped under Hadadezer, drawing fresh armies from beyond the Euphrates to Helam under their commander Shobach, and this time David did not delegate. He gathered all Israel, crossed the Jordan, and fought in person, and Israel routed them so completely that the kings subject to Hadadezer made peace and served Israel, and the Arameans were afraid to help the Ammonites anymore. Ammon stood alone behind the walls of its capital, Rabbah, and Israel's army settled into the siege that frames the next episode. Remember where the king is in this chapter: at the front, in person, crossing rivers to finish a war his enemy started. Remember it because chapter 11 opens with the same war, the same siege, and one devastating difference in where the king has chosen to be.
