Numbers Episode 1: Counted and Camped in Order
Numbers 1 to 4, Berean Standard Bible

One month after the tabernacle went up at the foot of Mount Sinai, God spoke to Moses from inside it again. The laws of Leviticus had filled the space between the mountain and the camp. Now the camp was going to move, and before a people can march, someone has to know how many of them there are.

"On the first day of the second month of the second year after the Israelites had come out of the land of Egypt, the LORD spoke to Moses in the Tent of Meeting in the Wilderness of Sinai. He said:"

The command was a census. Moses and Aaron were to number the whole congregation of Israel by their clans and families, listing every man by name, one by one: every male twenty years of age or older who could serve in Israel's army. One leader from each tribe was named to stand with them and help, the heads of the clans of Israel. The book gives all twelve names. Reuben's leader was Elizur son of Shedeur. Judah's was Nahshon son of Amminadab. Dan's was Ahiezer son of Ammishaddai, and so on through the tribes, twelve men the reader will meet again at the head of the march and at the altar with their offerings.

This count was not a count of everyone. It was a count of an army. The land God had promised had people living in it, and Israel was not being led there as a crowd of refugees. It was being organized as a force that could take it.

The counters went tribe by tribe, clan by clan, name by name, and the totals came back uneven, the way real families are uneven. Judah was the largest, 74,600 men. Manasseh was the smallest, 32,200. Dan stood second at 62,700, and little Benjamin counted 35,400. Every tribe's number ends the same way in the text, given to the last fifty men, because every man had been named.

"And all those counted totaled 603,550."

One tribe is missing from that number, and the text stops to explain why. The Levites were not numbered with the others.

"Do not number the tribe of Levi in the census with the other Israelites. Instead, you are to appoint the Levites over the tabernacle of the Testimony, all its furnishings, and everything in it. They shall carry the tabernacle and all its articles, care for it, and camp around it."

When the camp moved, the Levites took the tent down. When the camp stopped, they put it up. No one else was to come near the tabernacle when it was being moved, on pain of death. And their camping place was the ring closest to the tent on every side.

"But the Levites are to camp around the tabernacle of the Testimony and watch over it, so that no wrath will fall on the congregation of Israel."

The Levites were a buffer of men standing between a holy tent and a careless nation. Leviticus had shown what happened when men came near God's fire the wrong way, in the deaths of Aaron's two oldest sons. That lesson is built into the geography now. The whole congregation would camp around the Levites. Only the Levites would camp around God.

Chapter 2 lays the whole camp out like a map, and it is the same square a reader has seen before, walked around one piece at a time in Exodus. Now it is filled with people.

On the east side, toward the sunrise, under one standard: Judah, Issachar, and Zebulun, 186,400 men in all. They were to set out first. On the south side, under the standard of Reuben: Reuben, Simeon, and Gad, 151,450. They were to set out second. In the middle of the camps, the Tent of Meeting was to travel with the camp of the Levites, each tribe setting out in the order it had encamped, every man in his place under his standard. On the west side, under the standard of Ephraim: Ephraim, Manasseh, and Benjamin, 108,100. They were to set out third. On the north side, under the standard of Dan: Dan, Asher, and Naphtali, 157,600. They were to set out last, under their standards.

The chapter ends by counting the census a second time, from the other direction: the total of those counted in the camps, by their divisions, was 603,550. The number that came out of the name-by-name count and the number that fills the four sides of the square are the same number. Then the sentence this book will say again and again in its good moments: the Israelites did everything the LORD commanded Moses. They camped under their standards in this way, and they set out in the same way, each man with his clan and his family.

Chapter 3 counts the one tribe the first count left out, and counts it differently. The Levites were numbered from a month old, every male, because their service was not war. It was inheritance by birth into the work of the tent.

Aaron's family comes first, and the record is honest about it. Aaron's sons were Nadab, Abihu, Eleazar, and Ithamar. Nadab and Abihu died in the presence of the LORD when they offered unauthorized fire in the Wilderness of Sinai, and since they had no sons, Eleazar and Ithamar served as priests in their father's lifetime. The census chapter does not skip over the family's dead. It counts the living with their names standing in the record next to them.

Then the three Levitical families, and each one gets a side of the tent and a job. The Gershonites, 7,500 males, camped on the west, behind the tabernacle, and their charge was the tent itself: its curtains, the tent and its covering, the curtain for the entrance, the hangings of the courtyard and its entrance, and the cords. The Kohathites, 8,600, camped on the south side, and theirs were the duties of the sanctuary: the ark, the table, the lampstand, the altars, the articles of the sanctuary used in ministering, and the curtain. The Merarites, 6,200, camped on the north side, and theirs was the frame: the boards of the tabernacle, its bars, pillars, bases, and all its equipment. Over all the Levites stood Eleazar, son of Aaron the priest, chief of their leaders, with oversight of those responsible for the duties of the sanctuary.

"The total number of Levites that Moses and Aaron counted by their clans at the LORD's command, including all the males a month old or more, was 22,000."

Then the count turns into an exchange, and the exchange reaches back to the night Israel left Egypt. On the day God struck down every firstborn in the land of Egypt, He consecrated every firstborn in Israel to Himself, man and beast. The firstborn belonged to Him. Now He was taking substitutes.

"Behold, I have taken the Levites from among the children of Israel in place of every firstborn Israelite from the womb. The Levites belong to Me,"

So Moses counted the firstborn too, every male of the Israelites a month old or more, by name: 22,273. The Levites numbered 22,000. The firstborn outnumbered their substitutes by 273 men, and a substitute system in this book does not round off. Each of the 273 was to be redeemed at five shekels a head, by the sanctuary shekel.

"To redeem the 273 firstborn Israelites who outnumber the Levites, you are to collect five shekels for each one, according to the sanctuary shekel of twenty gerahs."

Five times 273 is 1,365 shekels, and that is the figure the chapter gives for the money Moses collected from the firstborn of Israel. He gave the redemption money to Aaron and his sons, in obedience to the word of the LORD. Two hundred seventy-three men walked through the camp that year carrying a price tag of five shekels each, the difference between what God had claimed on the night of the Exodus and the men He had accepted in their place.

Chapter 4 counts the Levites a third time, and this time the filter is working age. The men from thirty to fifty years old, everyone qualified to serve in the work at the Tent of Meeting, were numbered family by family for the carrying.

The Kohathites had the most holy things, and their work was the most dangerous work in the camp. When the camp was ready to move, Aaron and his sons went in first, alone, and covered everything. They took down the veil and covered the ark of the Testimony with it, then layers over that, a covering of fine leather and a cloth of blue, and set its poles in place. They wrapped the table, the lampstand, the altars, and every article in their own coverings and cloths, blue, purple, and scarlet. Only when Aaron and his sons had finished covering the holy objects and all their equipment could the Kohathites come in and lift them.

"But they must not touch the holy objects, or they will die."

Aaron and his sons assigned each Kohathite his task and what he was to carry, man by man, so that they would live and not die when they came near the most holy things. And even so, one more warning stands at the end of their instructions.

"But the Kohathites are not to go in and look at the holy objects, even for a moment, or they will die."

The holiest work in Israel was carrying furniture you were forbidden to see.

The Gershonites, 2,630 men of working age, carried the curtains, coverings, hangings, and cords, under the direction of Ithamar, Aaron's younger son. The Merarites, 3,200, carried the boards, bars, pillars, and bases, also under Ithamar. The Kohathites numbered 2,750. Eleazar, who stood over the Levites as a whole, had his own charge written down beside theirs: the oil for the light, the fragrant incense, the daily grain offering, and the anointing oil, and oversight of the entire tabernacle and everything in it.

"And the number of men was 8,580." Eight thousand five hundred eighty men, each one numbered by name and assigned his work and his burden, as the LORD had commanded Moses.

So the first four chapters of Numbers end with everything in its place, and nothing yet in motion. An army of 603,550 stands counted around a tent, in four camps under four standards, in an order it will both march in and sleep in. The tent at the center can be taken down, carried, and raised again by 8,580 named men who know exactly which pole, board, curtain, and vessel is theirs, down to the 273 men who were bought back at five shekels a head to balance the books of the firstborn.

It is the most organized Israel has ever been. What the camp does not have yet is a blessing spoken over it, rules for pushing uncleanness outside its borders before the march, or any orders to move.

And it has not yet been tested by a single mile of road.
