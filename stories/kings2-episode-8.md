2 Kings Episode 8: The Purge, and the Hidden Child
2 Kings 10-11, Berean Standard Bible

Jehu's purge was conducted by letter before it was conducted by sword. Ahab had seventy sons in Samaria, guarded by the city's officials and elders, and Jehu wrote to their guardians with a challenge dressed as a choice: “select the best and most worthy son of your master, set him on his father’s throne, and fight for your master’s house.” The men who received it did the arithmetic of the last chapter: “If two kings could not stand against him, how can we?” They surrendered in writing: “We are your servants, and we will do whatever you say. We will not make anyone king. Do whatever is good in your sight.” Jehu's second letter named the price of that surrender: “If you are on my side, and if you will obey me, then bring the heads of your master’s sons to me at Jezreel by this time tomorrow.” The letter arrived, and the leading men of Samaria slaughtered all seventy and sent the heads in baskets. Jehu ordered them piled in two heaps at the city gate until morning, then stood before the people and placed the act where he wanted it placed, in prophecy: “You are innocent. It was I who conspired against my master and killed him. But who killed all these?” “Know, then, that not a word the LORD has spoken against the house of Ahab will fail, for the LORD has done what He promised through His servant Elijah.” He finished the work at Jezreel himself, killing everyone who remained of Ahab's house there, his great men, his close friends, his priests, without a survivor.

On the road to Samaria the purge widened past its commission. At Beth-eked of the Shepherds, Jehu met forty-two relatives of Ahaziah of Judah, traveling innocently to pay a family visit: “We are relatives of Ahaziah,” they answered, “we have come down to greet the sons of the king and of the queen mother.” They had not heard that the sons of the king and the queen mother were dead. Jehu's order was: “Take them alive.” His men took them alive, then slaughtered them at the well, all forty-two, sparing none. Judah's royal family was now bleeding for a visit made in ignorance. Then Jehu picked up Jehonadab son of Rechab, a man of famous severity, with a handshake test: “Is your heart as true to mine as my heart is to yours?” “Come with me and see my zeal for the LORD!” At Samaria the zeal finished Ahab's house there, and then turned to Ahab's god. Jehu announced a great sacrifice to Baal, greater than Ahab's: “Ahab served Baal a little, but Jehu will serve him a lot.” Every prophet, servant, and priest of Baal in Israel was summoned on pain of death: “Whoever is missing will not live.” The temple filled from end to end, because Jehu was acting deceptively, building a trap out of their own festival. He verified the congregation held none but Baal's own: “Look around to see that there are no servants of the LORD here among you”, “only servants of Baal.” Then he stationed eighty men outside with their lives pledged against a single escape, let the sacrifice begin, and gave the order: “Go in and kill them. Do not let anyone out.” The sacred pillar was dragged out and burned, the temple torn down, and the book records its final use with relish: “they tore down the temple of Baal and made it into a latrine, which it is to this day.” “Thus Jehu eradicated Baal from Israel,” the record says, and keeps going in the same breath.

Then the book delivers its double verdict on the purger, and both halves stand. The word of the LORD came to Jehu with a dynasty: “Because you have done well in carrying out what is right in My sight and have done to the house of Ahab all that was in My heart, four generations of your sons will sit on the throne of Israel.” And in the next breath: “Yet Jehu was not careful to follow the instruction of the LORD, the God of Israel, with all his heart.” He kept the golden calves at Bethel and Dan, Jeroboam's sin, untouched. The zeal had a border, and the border was everything in Israel's worship that did not belong to Ahab. Already in his reign the LORD began to cut Israel down to size, and Hazael of Aram struck the whole territory east of the Jordan. Jehu reigned twenty-eight years and died, and the dynasty promised him began its four generations with the land already shrinking.

While Samaria was being purged, Ahab's bloodline in Jerusalem tried to finish David's. Athaliah, Ahaziah's mother, Ahab's daughter, saw her son dead and moved to annihilate every royal heir of Judah, so the throne would have no claimant but her. She nearly succeeded. One child was stolen out of the killing: Joash, Ahaziah's infant son, taken by his aunt Jehosheba, a king's daughter married to the high priest, and hidden with his nurse in a bedroom, then in the house of the LORD, for six years, while Athaliah ruled the land. In the seventh year, Jehoiada the priest staged the counter-coup with temple guards, David's own spears and shields from the sanctuary, and a ring of armed men around a seven-year-old. He brought the boy out, set the crown on him, gave him the Testimony, and anointed him, and the people clapped and cried: “Long live the king!” Athaliah heard the trumpets, came into the temple court, saw the king standing by the pillar with the land rejoicing around him, tore her clothes, and screamed: “Treason! Treason!” Jehoiada had her taken out between the ranks and killed at the horses' entrance, because, he had said, “She must not be put to death in the house of the LORD.” The people then tore down Jerusalem's temple of Baal and killed its priest, Jehoiada bound king and people in covenant to be the LORD's people, and the child was seated on the throne while, the book says, “the city was quiet, because Athaliah had been put to the sword at the royal palace.” The lamp promised to David had survived its closest call in a bedroom and a temple storeroom: “Joash was seven years old when he became king.”
