Numbers Episode 8: A New Generation Counted
Numbers 26 to 36, Berean Standard Bible

God told Moses and Eleazar to count Israel again.

The Aaron in that sentence is gone. The word came to Moses and to Eleazar son of Aaron the priest, after the plague at Peor, on the plains of Moab by the Jordan across from Jericho: take a census of the whole congregation of Israel, all those twenty years of age or older who can serve in the army of Israel. It was the same command, in almost the same words, that had opened the book at Sinai. The counters were different. So were the counted.

The tribes came in at familiar sizes. Reuben, 43,730. Judah again the largest, 76,500. Zebulun, 60,500. Dan second this time, 64,400. Simeon fell hardest of all, from 59,300 in the first count to 22,200 in the second, a drop no other tribe comes close to matching, in the tribe whose prince had walked into camp with a Midianite woman on his arm while the plague was still burning. Ephraim dropped too, and Benjamin rose. The book does not explain the movements tribe by tribe. It gives the totals and lets them stand.

"These men of Israel numbered 601,730 in all."

Then the census says the thing the whole book has been walking toward.

"Among all these, however, there was not one who had been numbered by Moses and Aaron the priest when they counted the Israelites in the Wilderness of Sinai. For the LORD had told them that they would surely die in the wilderness. Not one was left except Caleb son of Jephunneh and Joshua son of Nun."

Six hundred three thousand, five hundred fifty men had been counted at Sinai. Six hundred one thousand, seven hundred thirty were counted now, nearly the same army to within two thousand men, and of the first army only two names appear on both lists. Every other man on the new roll was the child of a man buried in the wilderness, or was himself one of the under-twenties the sentence had spared. The count that opened the book as a muster for war closed it as a memorial. The land was to be divided among the new names by lot, in proportion to the numbers: the larger tribe a larger inheritance, the smaller a smaller one, each tribe's inheritance in proportion to its number of names.

The new roll had hardly been finished when five women came forward and changed the law of the land before the land had been entered.

The daughters of Zelophehad son of Hepher, of the tribe of Manasseh, had no brothers. Their names are recorded twice in these chapters, as if the text wants them remembered individually: Mahlah, Noah, Hoglah, Milcah, and Tirzah. They stood before Moses, before Eleazar the priest, before the leaders and the whole congregation, at the entrance to the Tent of Meeting, and made their case in the form of a question about their father's name.

"Our father died in the wilderness, but he was not among the followers of Korah who gathered together against the LORD. Instead, he died because of his own sin, and he had no sons."

"Why should the name of our father disappear from his clan because he had no sons? Give us property among our father's brothers."

Note what they did and did not claim. They did not claim their father had been innocent. He had died because of his own sin, in the wilderness, like his whole generation, and they said so in their first sentence, taking care only to separate him from Korah's company. Their claim was about his name and his portion: a man's inheritance should not vanish from his clan because his children were daughters. Moses did not decide it. Moses brought their case before the LORD, the way he had waited for the word on the men who wanted a second Passover.

"The daughters of Zelophehad speak correctly. You certainly must give them property as an inheritance among their father's brothers and transfer their father's inheritance to them."

And God did not stop at their case. He built the general law out of it, an order of inheritance for Israel from that day forward: if a man dies and leaves no son, his inheritance goes to his daughter. If he has no daughter, to his brothers. If he has no brothers, to his father's brothers. If his father had no brothers, to the nearest relative in his clan. A petition from five sisters with no land yet to inherit became the succession law of the Promised Land, and it came before the land was conquered, before a single tribe had a single field to pass down. The book pauses for it at length, and Deuteronomy and Joshua will both have reason to come back to these five names.

In the same chapter, Moses' own succession was settled, and it was settled publicly because Moses asked for it to be. God told Moses to go up the mountain and see the land, and be gathered to his people, as Aaron had been, because of the waters of Meribah. Moses' response was not an argument. It was a request for the flock.

"May the LORD, the God of the spirits of all flesh, appoint a man over the congregation who will go out and come in before them, and who will lead them out and bring them in, so that the congregation of the LORD will not be like sheep without a shepherd."

And the LORD replied to Moses with Joshua's name: "Take Joshua son of Nun, a man with the Spirit in him, and lay your hands on him."

Joshua was to stand before Eleazar the priest and the whole congregation, and Moses was to lay his hands on him in their sight and commission him, and give him some of Moses' authority, so that the whole congregation would obey. Joshua would not receive the word of God the way Moses had. He would stand before Eleazar, who would seek counsel for him before the LORD by the judgment of the Urim, and at his command the people would go out and come in. Even the succession was a step down in office and no step down in care: the face-to-face prophet would be followed by a leader who inquired, in the open, with the priest beside him. And Moses did as the LORD commanded. He took Joshua, had him stand before Eleazar and the whole congregation, laid his hands on him, and commissioned him. The attendant who had wanted Eldad and Medad stopped, forty years earlier, now had hands laid on him in front of everyone, from the man he had defended. Numbers ends its leadership story not with Moses' death, which Deuteronomy holds, but with Moses still alive, presenting his replacement.

Chapters 28 and 29 hand the new generation its calendar of offerings, and it is Leviticus' material restated for people about to have their own fields and flocks: the daily offering, two lambs a year old, morning and evening, a continual burnt offering first ordained at Mount Sinai. Two more on the Sabbath. At the start of every month, bulls, a ram, seven lambs, and a goat, with their grain and drink offerings. Then the feasts, Passover and Unleavened Bread, the Festival of Weeks, the day of trumpet blasts, the Day of Atonement with its command to deny yourselves and do no work, and the Festival of Tabernacles running eight days with the largest offerings of the year, bulls by the dozen across the week. Chapter 30 follows with the law of vows: a man's vow stands, and he must not break his word. A young woman's vow in her father's house, or a wife's vow, could be annulled by her father or husband on the day he heard of it, and his silence confirmed it. A widow's or divorced woman's vow stood on her own word. The calendar and the vow laws are the fabric of a settled year, given to a camp that had not yet slept a single night in the land they described.

Then the book returned its one unfinished account. God had commanded it after Peor: avenge the Israelites on the Midianites. A thousand men from every tribe, twelve thousand armed for war, went out with Phinehas son of Eleazar carrying the holy vessels and the trumpets for the battle cry. They waged war against Midian as the LORD had commanded Moses, and killed every male. Among the slain were the five kings of Midian: Evi, Rekem, Zur, Hur, and Reba. Zur was Cozbi's father.

"They also killed Balaam son of Beor with the sword."

The prophet who could not curse Israel for money died by Israel's sword in Midian's camp, and chapter 31 explains at last what his business there had been. The officers brought back the women and children of Midian alive, and Moses was angry, and his anger named the connection between the mountains and the plague for the first time in the text.

"Look, these women caused the sons of Israel, through the counsel of Balaam, to turn unfaithfully against the LORD at Peor, so that the plague struck the congregation of the LORD."

The man who would not curse Israel from the mountaintops had counseled the one attack that worked on it: not a spell, a seduction. The war's aftermath was hard, and the text does not soften it. Moses ordered the male children and every woman who had known a man killed, and the soldiers purified themselves outside the camp for seven days under the red heifer's water from chapter 19, every man who had killed or touched the slain. The plunder was divided by count, half to the army and half to the congregation, with a tribute for the LORD taken from each half, and the officers who had commanded the thousands and hundreds came to Moses with a count of their own.

"Your servants have counted the soldiers under our command, and not one of us is missing."

Twelve thousand men had gone into Midian and twelve thousand had come back, and the officers brought an offering of gold, armlets and bracelets, signet rings, earrings, and necklaces, nearly seventeen thousand shekels of it counted out, to make atonement for themselves before the LORD. Moses and Eleazar took the gold and brought it into the Tent of Meeting as a memorial for the Israelites before the LORD. In a book that has counted the dead by the tens of thousands, chapter 31 pauses over a battle where the count of the missing came back at zero.

The last movement of the book looks across the Jordan from behind. The Reubenites and Gadites had very large herds and flocks, and they surveyed the lands of Jazer and Gilead, Sihon and Og's conquered country, and saw that the region was suitable for livestock. They came to Moses, Eleazar, and the leaders with a request: let this land be given to your servants as a possession. Do not make us cross the Jordan.

Moses heard in that request the exact sound of Kadesh.

"Shall your brothers go to war while you sit here? Why are you discouraging the Israelites from crossing into the land that the LORD has given them?"

He rehearsed the whole sad precedent: this is what your fathers did when I sent them from Kadesh Barnea to spy out the land. They discouraged the Israelites, God's anger burned, and that whole generation swore its way into wilderness graves. And here you are, a brood of sinners standing in your fathers' place, adding to the LORD's fierce anger against Israel. If these tribes turned away now, Moses warned, God would leave the whole nation in the wilderness again, and they would be the destruction of all these people.

The men of Gad and Reuben answered the charge by accepting its terms and reversing its direction. They would build sheepfolds for their livestock and cities for their little ones here, they said.

"But we will arm ourselves and be ready to go ahead of the Israelites until we have brought them into their place. Meanwhile, our little ones will remain in the fortified cities for protection from the inhabitants of the land."

They would not return to their homes until every Israelite had received his inheritance. Moses took the offer and bound it with the warning that has echoed out of this book ever since: if you will do this, if you will arm yourselves before the LORD for battle and cross the Jordan until He has driven out His enemies, then when the land is subdued you may return free of obligation, and this land will be yours before the LORD.

The oath's other side was spoken just as plainly. "But if you do not do this, you will certainly sin against the LORD" and, Moses added, "be assured that your sin will find you out."

The tribes swore it, and Moses gave the Gadites, the Reubenites, and the half-tribe of Manasseh the kingdom of Sihon and the kingdom of Og, the land with its cities and territory. Gad built its fortified cities, Reuben rebuilt Heshbon and its towns, and the half-tribe of Manasseh took Gilead and drove out the Amorites who were there. Two and a half tribes would live east of the river because their herds needed grass, and they would cross the river armed, ahead of their brothers, to earn it. The first settlements of the Promised Land were planted by men who promised to leave them until the rest of the promise was kept.

Chapter 33 rolls the whole journey out in one list, stage by stage, because Moses had recorded their stages at the LORD's command: out of Rameses in Egypt on the fifteenth day of the first month, the day after Passover, marching out triumphantly in the sight of all the Egyptians while Egypt buried its firstborn. Through the wilderness of Sin, where the manna came. To the foot of Mount Sinai, where the list pauses over a year of law without marking the time. Past Kibroth-hattaavah, where the quail graves were. To Kadesh, and Mount Hor, where the list gives Aaron his age: Aaron was 123 years old when he died on Mount Hor. Down around Edom, past the bronze serpent's country, to the Arnon, and at last to the plains of Moab by the Jordan across from Jericho. The journey list is Episode 1's census written as geography: forty years of camping grounds, every one of them named, almost none of them explained, because the explanation had filled the book the reader just finished. After the list come the orders for the land ahead: drive out the inhabitants, destroy their carved images, divide the land by lot, and a warning that any people left in the land would become barbs in Israel's eyes and thorns in its sides.

Chapters 34 and 35 draw the land's borders, from the Dead Sea and the wilderness of Zin in the south to Lebo Hamath in the north, the Great Sea on the west, the Jordan and the Sea of Galilee on the east, and name the leaders who would divide it, Eleazar the priest and Joshua son of Nun at their head. Then the Levites, who had no inheritance, were given towns out of every tribe's territory, forty-eight towns with their pasturelands, and six of them were marked for a purpose no other ancient law in the book quite matches: cities of refuge.

A person who killed someone unintentionally, without intent, without enmity, without lying in wait, could flee to one of the six cities, three east of the Jordan and three in Canaan, and be safe from the avenger of blood until the assembly judged him. These six cities would serve as a refuge for the Israelites and for the foreigner or stranger among them. If the assembly found the killing was manslaughter, it returned him to his city of refuge, and he lived there under its protection, unable to go home, until one specific event released him.

"Because the manslayer must remain in his city of refuge until the death of the high priest. Only after the death of the high priest may he return to the land he owns."

No ransom could buy him out earlier, and no ransom could be taken for the life of a murderer, who was to be put to death, because bloodshed pollutes the land, and no atonement could be made for the land except by the blood of the one who shed it.

"Do not defile the land where you live and where I dwell. For I, the LORD, dwell among the Israelites."

The book's last chapter comes back, the way the book keeps coming back, to the daughters of Zelophehad. The heads of their clan in Manasseh raised the other side of their victory: if the sisters married men from another tribe, their inherited land would go with them, and Manasseh's inheritance would shrink by fields its daughters had won. Moses gave the LORD's ruling, and the ruling protected both the women and the inheritance in the same breath.

"This is what the LORD has commanded concerning the daughters of Zelophehad: They may marry anyone they please, provided they marry within a clan of the tribe of their father."

"No inheritance in Israel may be transferred from tribe to tribe, because each of the Israelites is to retain the inheritance of the tribe of his fathers."

And the five sisters married within the clan, their cousins on their father's side, sons of their father's brothers, and their inheritance remained in the tribe of their father's clan. The women who opened the argument about a father's name closed the book by keeping their father's land in their father's tribe, on terms the whole nation could live under.

"These are the commandments and ordinances that the LORD gave the Israelites through Moses on the plains of Moab by the Jordan across from Jericho."

That is where Numbers ends: not across the river, but facing it. The first generation is buried. The second is counted, led, blessed, legislated for, and camped in the plains of Moab within sight of Jericho's country. Moses is still with them, and his replacement stands commissioned beside the priest. The next sound in the story is Moses' own voice, speaking to this new generation about everything behind them, on the last ground he will ever stand on.
