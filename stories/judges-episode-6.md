Judges Episode 6: Jephthah and the Vow
Judges 10 to 12, Berean Standard Bible

After Abimelech the book breathes twice, quietly. Tola of Issachar rose up to save Israel and judged twenty-three years from Shamir in Ephraim's hills, and no war of his is recorded at all. Jair the Gileadite followed for twenty-two years, and his record is a family's prosperity: thirty sons who rode on thirty donkeys and held thirty towns in Gilead called Havvoth-jair, the towns of Jair, to this day. Forty-five years of judges with no oppressor named. Then Jair died, and the cycle turned to its lowest point yet.

"And again the Israelites did evil in the sight of the LORD. They served the Baals, the Ashtoreths, the gods of Aram, Sidon, and Moab, and the gods of the Ammonites and Philistines. Thus they forsook the LORD and did not serve Him." Count the gods. Earlier turns named Baals and Ashtoreths; this one catalogs the whole neighborhood's pantheon, seven directions of idolatry at once, as if Israel were collecting. The consequence matched: God sold them into two hands at once, the Philistines on the coast and the Ammonites in the east, and the Ammonites "harassed and oppressed the Israelites" in Gilead for eighteen years, then crossed the Jordan to fight Judah, Benjamin, and Ephraim too, "and Israel was in deep distress."

Israel cried out with the standard confession, "We have sinned against You, for we have indeed forsaken our God and served the Baals," and this time God argued with the cry. His answer is a courtroom recital of the whole book so far, oppressor by oppressor: "When the Egyptians, Amorites, Ammonites, Philistines, Sidonians, Amalekites, and Maonites oppressed you and you cried out to Me, did I not save you from their hands? But you have forsaken Me and served other gods, so I will no longer save you." Then the sentence no other generation in the book had heard: "Go and cry out to the gods you have chosen. Let them save you in your time of trouble."

What Israel did next is the one genuinely new thing in the cycle. The confession deepened past formula: "We have sinned," the Israelites said to the LORD. "Deal with us as You see fit; but please deliver us today!" Accept any punishment, only save us. And then they acted on it before any deliverer appeared: "So they put away the foreign gods from among them and served the LORD, and He could no longer bear the misery of Israel." Every previous turn had God moved to pity by groaning. This one says He could no longer bear it, after they had first borne the cost of putting the gods away. The Ammonites mustered in Gilead; Israel camped at Mizpah; and Gilead's rulers posted the command like a notice: "Whoever will launch the attack against the Ammonites will be the head of all who live in Gilead."

The man they found for the job was the one they had thrown away. "Now Jephthah the Gileadite was a mighty man of valor; he was the son of a prostitute, and Gilead was his father." His father's legitimate sons had grown up and driven him out with a sentence that cut him off from the family estate: "You shall have no inheritance in our father's house, because you are the son of another woman." Jephthah fled to the land of Tob, and there "worthless men gathered around him and traveled with him," the same word the book used for Abimelech's hired swords, except Abimelech bought his worthless men with temple silver and Jephthah's gathered to him for nothing, because an outcast with a sword draws outcasts.

When Ammon made war, the elders of Gilead went to Tob to fetch him, and their offer was blunt: come, they said, "be our commander, so that we can fight against the Ammonites." Jephthah made them own their history before he took the job: "Did you not hate me and expel me from my father's house? Why then have you come to me now, when you are in distress?" They raised the offer to the exact terms of the Mizpah notice, commander and head over all Gilead, and Jephthah locked it with an oath: "If you take me back to fight the Ammonites and the LORD gives them to me, will I really be your leader?" "The LORD is our witness if we do not do as you say." Then he went with them, and at Mizpah he "repeated all his terms in the presence of the LORD," a contract sworn at the same camp where God's refusal speech was still ringing. Gilead's rejected son came home as its head, with God as the witness of the bargain.

Jephthah's first weapon was not a sword. It was a history brief, sent by messengers to the king of Ammon, answering Ammon's land claim with three hundred years of receipts. Israel had not taken Moab's or Ammon's land; it had asked Edom and Moab for passage and been refused; it had taken the Amorite king Sihon's land only after Sihon attacked, land God had given: "Now since the LORD, the God of Israel, has driven out the Amorites from before His people Israel, should you now possess it?" He pressed the theology of the region back at the king without flinching: "Do you not possess whatever your god Chemosh grants you? So also, we possess whatever the LORD our God has granted us." He cited Balak, who had never fought Israel over this land. He cited the calendar: "For three hundred years Israel has lived in Heshbon, Aroer, and their villages, as well as all the cities along the banks of the Arnon. Why did you not take them back during that time?" And he closed by handing the case upward: "I have not sinned against you, but you have done me wrong by waging war against me. May the LORD, the Judge, decide today between the Israelites and the Ammonites." The king of the Ammonites paid no heed. But the record stands: the outcast from Tob argued Israel's title like a statesman before he fought like a soldier, and when the fighting came, the text gives the sequence its full weight: "Then the Spirit of the LORD came upon Jephthah, and he passed through Gilead and Manasseh, then through Mizpah of Gilead. And from there he advanced against the Ammonites."

Then, in the advance, Jephthah made the vow. It is quoted here exactly, because everything after it turns on its wording: "If indeed You will deliver the Ammonites into my hand, then whatever comes out the door of my house to greet me on my triumphant return from the Ammonites will belong to the LORD, and I will offer it up as a burnt offering." Whatever comes out the door. In a house with servants, animals, and one child, the vow left the greeter to chance, or to God, which is the same gamble Gideon never took: Gideon asked God for signs; Jephthah offered God a blank check drawn on whoever loved him enough to run out first.

God delivered the Ammonites into his hand. The campaign was a rout, twenty cities devastated "from Aroer to the vicinity of Minnith, as far as Abel-keramim," and the Ammonites were subdued before Israel. Jephthah came home to Mizpah a victor and a head of Gilead, and the door of his house opened: "And when Jephthah returned home to Mizpah, there was his daughter coming out to meet him with tambourines and dancing! She was his only child; he had no son or daughter besides her." The exclamation point is the narrator's own. Tambourines and dancing were how Israel's women had always greeted victory, Miriam at the sea, the women for Saul and David in a later day. Jephthah's only child came out doing the most natural thing in the world, and her father tore his clothes: "No! Not my daughter! You have brought me to my knees! You have brought great misery upon me, for I have given my word to the LORD and cannot take it back."

Her answer is the most composed speech in the chapter, and it comes from the person with the least power in it: She replied to her father: "you have given your word to the LORD. Do to me as you have said, for the LORD has avenged you of your enemies, the Ammonites." She asked one thing, and the thing she asked names what the vow was costing her: "Let me do this one thing: Let me wander for two months through the mountains with my friends and mourn my virginity." Not her life in the abstract, her unlived life, the marriage and children that would now never happen, the end of her father's line in her, the outcast's house closing with her. He told her to go, and she went to the mountains with her friends for two months.

"After two months, she returned to her father, and he did to her as he had vowed. And she had never had relations with a man." That is all the text says, and its restraint deserves to be met with restraint. The narrative does not describe the act, does not praise it, and does not condemn it in so many words; it records that Jephthah did as he had vowed, and then it records what Israel did with the memory: "it has become a custom in Israel that each year the young women of Israel go out for four days to lament the daughter of Jephthah the Gileadite." Lament, not celebrate. The daughters of Israel kept four days a year for her, for generations, and the book moves on leaving the verdict to that lament and to the vow's own arithmetic: a leader who had bargained brilliantly with elders and kings bargained carelessly once with God, and the price came out his door dancing. Readers have argued for three thousand years about what exactly happened on that return, and the honest place to leave it is where the text leaves it: he did to her as he had vowed, and she had never known a man, and Israel's daughters wept for her every year.

Ephraim arrived after the victory, as Ephraim now reliably did, and this time there was no gleaning speech. They crossed to Zaphon with a threat uglier than Gideon's day: "Why have you crossed over to fight the Ammonites without calling us to go with you? We will burn your house down with you inside!" Jephthah, who knew something about being burned out of a father's house, answered with facts instead of flattery: "My people and I had a serious conflict with the Ammonites, and when I called, you did not save me out of their hands. When I saw that you would not save me, I risked my life and crossed over to the Ammonites, and the LORD delivered them into my hand. Why then have you come today to fight against me?" Ephraim fought anyway, and added an insult to the charge, calling the Gileadites fugitives living in Ephraim and Manasseh's territory, and Gilead struck them down and took the fords of the Jordan, Ehud's old tactic turned inward on Israel itself.

At the fords they ran a test of accent. Whenever a fugitive Ephraimite asked to cross, the Gileadites asked if he was an Ephraimite, and if he said no, they told him to say Shibboleth. If he said Sibboleth, because he could not pronounce it correctly, they seized him and killed him at the fords. One consonant was the difference between the two sides of the river, and "at that time 42,000 Ephraimites were killed." Forty-two thousand Israelites dead in a civil quarrel, at the same river crossings where God had once opened the water for the whole nation together. Jephthah judged Israel six years, the shortest tenure of any major judge so far, and died and was buried in one of the cities of Gilead.

Three quiet men close the section. Ibzan of Bethlehem judged seven years, and his record is a web of marriages, thirty sons and thirty daughters, the daughters married out of the clan and thirty wives brought in for the sons, alliance by wedding on a scale no warrior in the book matches. Elon the Zebulunite judged ten years and has nothing recorded but his burial. Abdon son of Hillel judged eight years, with forty sons and thirty grandsons who rode on seventy donkeys, Jair's prosperity pattern repeated. Then the cycle is due to turn again, and the deliverer for the next turn will be the most gifted and least governed man in the book, announced by an angel before his birth, set apart from the womb, and strong enough to tear a lion apart with his hands. The tragedy the book is building toward is not that Israel lacks a strong deliverer. It is that strength is all the next one has.
