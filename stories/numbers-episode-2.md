Numbers Episode 2: Blessing, Trumpets, and the First March
Numbers 5 to 10, Berean Standard Bible

The camp was counted and placed. Before it could move, it had to be cleaned, blessed, and given its marching orders, and that is what these six chapters do. Some of it is law the reader will recognize from Leviticus, wearing traveling clothes.

First, the borders of the camp were drawn the way Leviticus had drawn them around the tent. God told Moses to send away from the camp anyone with a skin disease, anyone with a bodily discharge, and anyone defiled by a dead body, male and female alike, so that they would not defile the camp where God dwelled in their midst. They were sent outside, the same outside the cleansed man of Leviticus waited in, hoping to be examined and brought back.

Then came a law that reaches back into Episode 1 of the last book and picks up its thread. When a man or woman committed any sin against another person, acting unfaithfully against the LORD, that person was guilty.

"And must confess the sin he has committed. He must make full restitution, add a fifth to its value, and give all this to the one he has wronged."

The fifth again: confession, repayment with more added, and if the wronged person had died with no close relative to receive it, the restitution went to the priest, along with the ram of atonement. Sin against a neighbor in this camp had paperwork, and the paperwork ended at the altar.

Chapter 5 also holds one of the strangest proceedings in the Bible, and the book tells it without explaining it, so it will be told here the same way. When a husband suspected his wife of unfaithfulness and no witness existed, he brought her to the priest with an offering of barley flour, no oil and no frankincense on it, because it was a grain offering for jealousy. The priest had the woman stand before the LORD with her hair unbound, and he made a water no kitchen in the camp would recognize: holy water in a clay jar with dust taken from the floor of the tabernacle stirred into it. He wrote the curses of the law in a book and washed the ink off into the water. The woman swore an oath accepting the outcome, and drank. If she had defiled herself, the water that brings a curse would enter her and cause bitter suffering, her belly would swell and her thigh would shrivel, and she would become a curse among her people. If she had not, she would be cleared and able to bear children. The husband was free from guilt in bringing the charge. The woman, if guilty, bore her iniquity. The text gives the rite and moves on. It does not say how often it was used, and no story in the Bible shows it happening.

Chapter 6 opens the door to a vow any Israelite could take, man or woman, rich or poor: the vow of a Nazirite, to separate himself to the LORD for a set time. For the whole period of the vow, no product of the grapevine, not wine, not vinegar, not grapes, not raisins, not even the seeds or the skins. No razor was to touch his head; he was to let the hair of his head grow long, because the symbol of his consecration to God was on his head. He could not go near a dead body, not even if his father or his mother or his brother or his sister died, a rule that put the Nazirite, for the length of his vow, under a version of the high priest's own restriction. If someone died suddenly beside him, his consecrated head was defiled, and the count started over after shaving and offerings on the seventh and eighth days. When the days were completed, he came to the entrance of the Tent of Meeting with a lamb, a ewe lamb, and a ram, with bread and drink offerings. His long hair was shaved at the entrance and put in the fire under the peace offering, and then he could drink wine again: the vow was over, and the hair that had marked it was ash on the altar.

Then the chapter changes key entirely. God gave Aaron and his sons the words they were to speak over Israel, and the words are not a law.

"Tell Aaron and his sons: This is how you are to bless the Israelites. Say to them:"

"May the LORD bless you and keep you; may the LORD cause His face to shine upon you and be gracious to you; may the LORD lift up His countenance toward you and give you peace."

"So they shall put My name on the Israelites, and I will bless them."

That blessing will be spoken over Israel's descendants for thousands of years, in rooms Aaron could not have pictured. In this camp it had a job: before the people moved a single mile, the priests stamped God's name on them with words about His face shining on them, and God took the actual blessing on Himself. The priests said it. He did it.

Chapter 7 is the longest chapter in the book, and it is one day told twelve times. On the day Moses finished setting up the tabernacle and anointed it, the leaders of Israel, the same twelve men who had assisted at the census, brought their dedication offerings. First came six covered wagons and twelve oxen, two leaders to a wagon, one ox each. Moses gave the wagons to the Levites who needed wheels: two wagons and four oxen to the Gershonites for the curtains, four wagons and eight oxen to the Merarites for the heavy frames and boards. The Kohathites got none, because their service was the holy things, and those were carried on shoulders.

Then, one leader on each of twelve days, came the identical gift, and the text records it in full twelve times over. A silver plate of 130 shekels and a silver bowl of 70 shekels, both filled with fine flour mixed with oil for a grain offering. A gold pan of 10 shekels, full of incense. A young bull, a ram, and a male lamb a year old for a burnt offering. A male goat for a sin offering. And for the peace offering, two oxen, five rams, five male goats, and five male lambs a year old. Nahshon of Judah brought his on the first day. Nethanel of Issachar on the second. Elizur of Reuben, who had led the census count, on the fourth. So it went, tribe by tribe, page by page, until Gamaliel and Abidan and Ahira had brought theirs and the dedication of the altar was complete, with the chapter's totals at the end: twelve of everything, counted like the people were counted. When Moses went into the Tent of Meeting to speak with the LORD, he heard the voice speaking to him from above the mercy seat, from between the two cherubim. The long list had been leading somewhere after all. The tent worked. God talked in it.

Chapter 8 set the Levites apart for their service in a ceremony the whole congregation watched. The Levites were sprinkled with the water of purification. They shaved their whole bodies and washed their clothes. Two bulls were brought, and the Israelites laid their hands on the Levites, the way a man laid his hand on his offering, because the Levites were being given as an offering themselves: Aaron offered them as a wave offering from the Israelites before the LORD.

"And I have given the Levites as a gift to Aaron and his sons from among the Israelites, to perform the service for the Israelites at the Tent of Meeting and to make atonement on their behalf, so that no plague will come against the Israelites when they approach the sanctuary."

Their working years were set: from twenty-five years old a Levite could come to serve at the tent, and at fifty he retired from the work itself, though he could still assist his brothers and keep watch. The men who would carry God's house through the wilderness had a start date and a stop date, like the offerings had measures and the camp had sides.

A year out of Egypt, the second Passover came, and the camp kept it on the fourteenth day of the first month, at twilight, exactly as commanded. But a group of men came to Moses with a problem the law had not yet answered out loud. They were unclean because of a dead body, and they wanted to know why they should be kept from presenting the LORD's offering at its appointed time with everyone else. Moses did not improvise. He told them to wait while he listened for what the LORD would say. The answer widened the feast: a man unclean by a corpse, or away on a journey, could keep the Passover a month later, on the fourteenth day of the second month, with unleavened bread and bitter herbs, nothing left till morning, no bone broken. A foreigner living among them who wanted to keep the Passover kept it by the same statute. One statute for the foreigner and the native of the land. The camp's God kept finding the man standing outside a rule and writing the door into the rule itself.

Above the whole arrangement sat the cloud. On the day the tabernacle was set up, the cloud had covered it, and from evening until morning it appeared like fire above the tent. Whether it stayed two days, a month, or a year, the Israelites camped as long as the cloud rested over the tabernacle, and when it lifted, they set out. Sometimes it rested only from evening till morning, and they set out in the morning. Sometimes it stayed, and they stayed, and kept the LORD's charge, and did not set out.

"They camped at the LORD's command, and they set out at the LORD's command; they carried out the LORD's charge according to His command through Moses."

Two trumpets of hammered silver were made for the last piece of machinery the march needed: a signal everyone could hear. Long blasts called the congregation together, or called only the leaders, or set the camps moving, east side first at one alarm, south side at the second. Short blasts in battle meant something no other signal in the camp meant: sound the trumpets when the enemy attacks, and you will be remembered before the LORD your God and saved. The trumpets were also blown over the sacrifices at the feasts and the new moons, a memorial before God. A camp this size could not be led by shouting. It would move, assemble, fight, and feast to the sound of silver.

On the twentieth day of the second month of the second year, the cloud lifted from above the tabernacle of the Testimony, and Israel set out from the Wilderness of Sinai in the order of their divisions, camp by camp, standard by standard, exactly the way chapter 2 had laid them out, the tent traveling in the middle with the Levites. They were bound for the wilderness of Paran.

Before the column moved, Moses made one personal request. Hobab, the son of his father-in-law Reuel the Midianite, was with the camp, and Moses asked him to come along. We are setting out for the place of which the LORD said, I will give it to you. Come with us, and we will treat you well, for the LORD has promised good things to Israel. Hobab said he would go back to his own land and his own people. Moses pressed him: please do not leave us, you know where we should camp in the wilderness, and you can be our eyes.

"If you come with us, we will share with you whatever good things the LORD gives us."

The text does not record Hobab's final answer, then or later. It records the march. Israel set out on a three-day journey from the mountain of the LORD, and the ark of the covenant went before them those three days, traveling ahead of the whole nation, to seek a resting place for them.

And Moses had a cry for the ark, at the start of every stage and at the end of it, two short sentences that turned a marching order into a battle and a homecoming.

Whenever the ark set out, Moses would say: "Rise up, O LORD! May Your enemies be scattered; may those who hate You flee before You."

And when it came to rest, he would say: "Return, O LORD, to the countless thousands of Israel."

The mountain of God was behind them. The camp that could not be blessed enough, counted enough, or organized enough was finally walking. It had taken two books of the Bible, a census, a law code, a priesthood, and a year at one mountain to get Israel moving toward the land it was promised in Genesis.

The road lasted three days before the complaining started.
