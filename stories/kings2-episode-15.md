2 Kings Episode 15: Jerusalem Falls
2 Kings 24-25, Berean Standard Bible

The last movement of the book opens with the cause stated before the events, in case the reader has forgotten Manasseh's oracle: Babylon came, Jehoiakim served Nebuchadnezzar three years and rebelled, and “the LORD sent Chaldean, Aramean, Moabite, and Ammonite raiders against Jehoiakim in order to destroy Judah, according to the word that the LORD had spoken through His servants the prophets.” “Surely this happened to Judah at the LORD’s command, to remove them from His presence because of the sins of Manasseh and all that he had done”, “and also for the innocent blood he had shed. For he had filled Jerusalem with innocent blood, and the LORD was unwilling to forgive.” Jehoiakim died with Egypt already finished as a power, Babylon holding everything from the Brook of Egypt to the Euphrates. His son Jehoiachin, eighteen years old, reigned three months, and when Nebuchadnezzar came up in person, the young king did the one sensible thing left to a king of Judah: he surrendered, walking out with his mother, his servants, his commanders, and his officials. It saved the city for nine more years and cost everything else. “As the LORD had declared, Nebuchadnezzar also carried off all the treasures from the house of the LORD and the royal palace, and he cut into pieces all the gold articles that Solomon king of Israel had made in the temple of the LORD.” Solomon's gold, cut into pieces: Hezekiah's envoys had once been shown it all. Then the people went: “He carried into exile all Jerusalem”, commanders, mighty men, craftsmen, metalsmiths, “ten thousand captives in all. Only the poorest people of the land remained.” Babylon installed the prisoner's uncle Mattaniah on the emptied throne and renamed him Zedekiah, and the book notes the reason the rest happened with a sentence that removes all suspense: “because of the anger of the LORD, all this happened in Jerusalem and Judah, until He finally banished them from His presence.” Zedekiah, the puppet, also rebelled.

The siege is told in the text's own dates, and this retelling keeps them, because the book wants the arithmetic felt. In Zedekiah's ninth year, on the tenth day of the tenth month, Nebuchadnezzar encamped against Jerusalem and built his siege wall. In the eleventh year, by the ninth day of the fourth month, “the famine in the city was so severe that the people of the land had no food.” That night the city was breached, and the king and his men of war fled through the gate between the two walls by the king's garden, toward the Arabah. The Chaldeans overtook him in the plains of Jericho, where Israel had first entered the land under Joshua, and his army deserted him there. At Riblah they pronounced judgment on the last king of David's line in Jerusalem: “they slaughtered the sons of Zedekiah before his eyes. Then they put out his eyes, bound him with bronze shackles, and took him to Babylon.” The last thing Zedekiah of Judah ever saw was the end of his house.

A month later Nebuzaradan, captain of the guard, came to finish the city. “He burned down the house of the LORD, the royal palace, and all the houses of Jerusalem”, every significant building. The walls were broken down all around. The people who remained were carried into exile, the poorest left behind again to tend vineyards and fields that no longer had owners. And the bronze went the way the gold had gone, in pieces: “the Chaldeans broke up the bronze pillars and stands and the bronze Sea in the house of the LORD, and they carried the bronze to Babylon.” The book lingers over the inventory of Solomon's work one last time, the two pillars, the Sea, the stands, and weighs it in a phrase: “the weight of the bronze from all these articles was beyond measure.” The chief priest Seraiah, the second priest Zephaniah, the doorkeepers, the officials and advisors and sixty men of the city were taken to Riblah and executed there. “So Judah was taken into exile, away from its own land.” The sentence the prophets had been pronouncing since Manasseh, the sentence Samaria had already served, is complete in one line.

What remained in the land got one brief chapter of almost-government. Gedaliah, son of Ahikam, of the family of Shaphan the scribe who had once carried the found Book to Josiah, was made governor at Mizpah, and he swore an oath to the army commanders who came in from the hills: “Do not be afraid of the servants of the Chaldeans. Live in the land and serve the king of Babylon, and it will be well with you.” In the seventh month, Ishmael of the royal family came with ten men and murdered him, along with the Judeans and Chaldeans with him, and the whole remnant, small and great, fled to Egypt for fear of Babylon's answer. The land of Judah was finally empty of everything, king, temple, priesthood, governor, and people, and the book could have ended there, in Egypt, with the flight.

It does not. The last scene is set thirty-seven years into Jehoiachin's exile, in Babylon, in the year a new king, Evil-merodach, took the throne, and “he released King Jehoiachin of Judah from prison.” “And he spoke kindly to Jehoiachin and set his throne above the thrones of the other kings who were with him in Babylon.” The captive who had surrendered at eighteen changed out of his prison clothes, “and he dined regularly at the king’s table for the rest of his life.” The book of Kings, which began with an old king dying in Jerusalem and a son securing a throne, ends with a dethroned king eating at a foreign table on a daily allowance: “the king provided Jehoiachin a daily portion for the rest of his life.” No explanation is attached. No return is promised in these pages, no Cyrus named, no future sketched. The line of David is alive, fed, and seated above the other captive kings in the capital of the empire that destroyed his city, and there the book stops, and this series will leave it exactly there until Chronicles takes up the story from the exile's side, looking back.
