2 Kings Episode 1: Fire from Heaven, and the Mantle Falls
2 Kings 1-2, Berean Standard Bible

The two books of Kings are one book in the Hebrew text, and this half opens without a pause, days after Ahab's death, with the kingdom already coming apart at its edges: “After the death of Ahab, Moab rebelled against Israel.” In Samaria, Ahab's son Ahaziah fell through the lattice of his upper room and lay badly hurt, and the injured king sent messengers to Ekron, to a Philistine god with a name the text gives as Baal-zebub, to ask whether he would recover. Elijah the Tishbite met the embassy on the road, sent by an angel, with a question that is the whole scene: “Is it because there is no God in Israel that you are on your way to inquire of Baal-zebub, the god of Ekron?” And with the question came the sentence: “You will not get up from the bed on which you are lying. You will surely die.” The messengers turned back without ever reaching Ekron, and when the king demanded a description of the man who had stopped them, they said: “He was a hairy man,” “with a leather belt around his waist.” Ahaziah knew the description the way all Israel knew it. “It was Elijah the Tishbite,” said the king.

So the king sent soldiers to fetch the prophet, a captain with fifty men, who found Elijah sitting on top of a hill and ordered him down in the king's name. Elijah answered: “If I am a man of God, may fire come down from heaven and consume you and your fifty men.” Fire fell, and it did. A second captain came with the same order and more haste in it, and the fire of God fell again. The third captain climbed the hill on his knees and begged for his life: “Man of God, may my life and the lives of these fifty servants of yours please be precious in your sight.” “But now may my life be precious in your sight.” The angel told Elijah to go down with him and not be afraid, and Elijah stood before the king and said the sentence to his face. Ahaziah died as the word had said, and because he had no son, his brother Jehoram took the throne. The fire scenes are told without comment, twice repeated and once refused, and the text lets the difference between the captains speak for itself.

Then the book turns to the old prophet's last journey, and it is walked at a companion's pace. Elijah and Elisha set out from Gilgal, and three times, at Bethel, at Jericho, and at the Jordan, Elijah tried to release his servant from following: “Please stay here, for the LORD has sent me on to Bethel.” Three times Elisha refused with the same oath: “As surely as the LORD lives and as you yourself live, I will not leave you.” At each stop the sons of the prophets came out to Elisha with the news everyone somehow knew: “Do you know that the LORD will take your master away from you today?” “Yes, I know,” he replied. “Do not speak of it.” At the Jordan, Elijah rolled up his cloak and struck the water, and it parted, and the two crossed on dry ground, fifty of the sons of the prophets watching from a distance. On the far side, Elijah offered one last gift: “Tell me, what can I do for you before I am taken away from you?” Elisha asked for the heir's share: “Please, let me inherit a double portion of your spirit”. Elijah answered: “You have requested a difficult thing,” said Elijah. “Nevertheless, if you see me as I am taken from you, it will be yours. But if not, then it will not be so.”

What Elisha saw was fire again, but fire of another kind: “suddenly a chariot of fire with horses of fire appeared and separated the two of them, and Elijah went up into heaven in a whirlwind.” Elisha cried out: “My father, my father, the chariots and horsemen of Israel!” and tore his clothes. Then he picked up the cloak that had fallen from Elijah and stood on the bank of the Jordan and struck the water with it, asking: “Where now is the LORD, the God of Elijah?” The water parted for him as it had for his master, and the watching prophets read the sign correctly: “The spirit of Elijah rests on Elisha.” They still begged to send fifty men searching the mountains and valleys in case the Spirit had set Elijah down somewhere, and when Elisha finally yielded to their embarrassment and let them go, they searched three days and found nothing. His answer when they returned was gentle by his standards: “Didn’t I tell you not to go?”

Elisha's first works as the heir were acts of repair. At Jericho the men of the city brought him their problem, good land poisoned by bad water, and he threw salt from a new bowl into the spring and spoke the word: “I have healed this water. No longer will it cause death or unfruitfulness.” The text adds that the water has been healthy to this day. Then comes the scene this episode will not soften. On the road up to Bethel, a group of boys came out of the city jeering at the prophet: “Go up, you baldhead! Go up, you baldhead!” Elisha turned, looked at them, “and called down a curse on them in the name of the LORD. Suddenly two female bears came out of the woods and mauled forty-two of the boys.” The book records it in three sentences and moves on to Mount Carmel and Samaria without explanation or apology, and this retelling leaves it where the book leaves it: the strange, severe first sign that the mantle had passed to a man through whom the word of the LORD acted, for healing and for judgment alike.
