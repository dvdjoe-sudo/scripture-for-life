Deuteronomy Episode 4: Judges, a King, and a Prophet to Come
Deuteronomy 17 to 20, Berean Standard Bible

The judges Moses had just appointed needed cases, courts, and limits, and the speech turned to all three. But chapter 17 began one step earlier, at the altar the judges would serve. "You shall not sacrifice to the LORD your God an ox or a sheep with any defect or serious flaw, for that is detestable to the LORD your God." A settled people would be tempted to bring God the animal they could spare. The law closed that door first.

Then came the hardest case a town court would ever hear: a man or woman found worshiping other gods, bowing to the sun or the moon or the host of heaven. Moses built the procedure before he named the penalty, and the procedure slowed everything down. A report was not a verdict. "And if it is reported and you hear about it, you must investigate it thoroughly. If the report is true and such an abomination has happened in Israel," then the person was brought out to the gates and stoned.

Even then, one voice could not kill anyone. "On the testimony of two or three witnesses a man shall be put to death, but he shall not be executed on the testimony of a lone witness." And the witnesses carried the weight of their own words into their hands: "The hands of the witnesses shall be the first in putting him to death, and after that, the hands of all the people." A man who testified to a death had to start it. The chapter's refrain followed, the one this section of the speech keeps sounding: "So you must purge the evil from among you."

Some cases would be too hard for a town gate. Bloodshed, lawsuits, assaults, disputes the local judges could not settle were to go up to the one place, to the Levitical priests and to the judge presiding at that time. "Inquire of them, and they will give you a verdict in the case." The verdict from the chosen place was final. The people were to do everything instructed there, not turning aside "to the right or to the left from the decision they declare to you".

The contempt that followed a refused verdict was itself a capital matter. "But the man who acts presumptuously, refusing to listen either to the priest who stands there to serve the LORD your God, or to the judge, must be put to death." Moses gave the reason the speech gives again and again for its hardest penalties: "Then all the people will hear and be afraid, and will no longer behave arrogantly."

Then Moses did something stranger than sentencing. He legislated for a request nobody had made yet. "When you enter the land that the LORD your God is giving you and have taken possession of it and settled in it, and you say," and here the future request stands in the people's own words: "Let us set a king over us like all the nations around us". No king was commanded. No king was forbidden. The office was fenced before it existed.

The fences were these. The king would be the man "whom the LORD your God shall choose", taken from among their brothers, never a foreigner. And three appetites were capped before the first crown was ever cast. "But the king must not acquire many horses for himself or send the people back to Egypt to acquire more horses". Behind the horse trade stood the old road, and God's own sentence on it: "You are never to go back that way again." He must not take many wives, "lest his heart go astray". He must not accumulate large amounts of silver and gold.

The last fence was not a cap. It was a desk. "When he is seated on his royal throne, he must write for himself a copy of this instruction on a scroll in the presence of the Levitical priests." His own hand, his own copy, kept with him, read "all the days of his life". The purpose was stated plainly: "so that he may learn to fear the LORD his God by carefully observing all the words of this instruction and these statutes". And the outcome Moses promised for a king under the book was not glory. "Then his heart will not be exalted above his countrymen". A king who read the law daily would reign as a brother among brothers, and he and his sons would reign many years in Israel.

Chapter 18 turned to the tribe with no desk and no land at all. The Levites held no portion or inheritance with Israel; the food offerings of the LORD were their inheritance, and Moses said it in the shortest form the speech uses for anything: "the LORD is their inheritance, as He promised them". So the people's tables carried the priests. From every sacrifice, the shoulder, the jowls, and the stomach went to the priests. Firstfruits of grain, new wine, and oil, and the first wool off the flock, followed. And a Levite who left his own town and came in earnestness to the chosen place was to serve alongside his brothers there and "eat equal portions", even if he had money of his own from selling his father's estate. The tribe's poverty was structural, so the law made its support structural too.

From the priests Moses turned to the other voices the land would offer, and the list came down like a roll call: no one who "sacrifices his son or daughter in the fire, practices divination or conjury, interprets omens, practices sorcery," no one who "casts spells, consults a medium or spiritist, or inquires of the dead." This was not a list of superstitions to smile at. "For whoever does these things is detestable to the LORD. And because of these detestable things, the LORD your God is driving out the nations before you." Israel was dispossessing peoples for these practices. To adopt them on arrival would be to move into a house and take up the crime it was emptied for. "You must be blameless before the LORD your God."

Against those voices Moses set the one voice God promised to keep sending. "The LORD your God will raise up for you a prophet like me from among your brothers. You must listen to him." And he grounded the promise in the people's own request, the one from the day of the assembly at Horeb, when they had begged not to hear God's voice directly again. Their words stood in the text: "Let us not hear the voice of the LORD our God or see this great fire anymore, so that we will not die!"

God's answer to that request was not rebuke. "They have spoken well." Then came the promise itself, in God's own words as Moses reported it: "I will raise up for them a prophet like you from among their brothers. I will put My words in his mouth, and he will tell them everything I command him." And a weight hung on the listening: "And I will hold accountable anyone who does not listen to My words that the prophet speaks in My name."

The office had its own death penalty. A prophet who dared to speak in God's name what God had not commanded, or who spoke in the name of other gods, was to die. And Moses answered the question his hearers had not yet asked out loud. They might ask in their hearts: "How can we recognize a message that the LORD has not spoken?" The test was plain and unsparing: "When a prophet speaks in the name of the LORD and the message does not come to pass or come true, that is a message the LORD has not spoken." The people owed such a man nothing, not even their fear. "Do not be afraid of him."

Chapter 19 went back to the ground, to cities and blood. When the nations were cut off and Israel settled in their cities and houses, three cities were to be set apart, with roads built to reach them and the land divided into three regions, so that a manslayer could flee and live. Moses told the case the cities existed for, and he told it as a work accident: two neighbors go into the forest to cut timber, the axe swings, "but the blade flies off the handle and strikes and kills his neighbor". No hatred, no intent, a tool failing in a man's hands. Without the city, the avenger of blood might pursue him in a rage and strike him dead on a long road, "though he did not deserve to die, since he did not intend any harm". If God enlarged the territory, three more cities were to be added, so that innocent blood would not be shed in the land.

The refuge had a wall the hater could not climb. "If, however, a man hates his neighbor and lies in wait, attacks him and kills him, and then flees to one of these cities," the elders of his own city were to send for him, bring him back, and hand him over to the avenger of blood to die. "You must show him no pity. You are to purge from Israel the guilt of shedding innocent blood, that it may go well with you." Mercy for the accidental, none for the ambush: the same chapter held both, and the difference was intent.

One sentence stood between the refuge cities and the witness laws, quiet enough to miss: "You must not move your neighbor's boundary marker, which was set up by your ancestors". In a land where the inheritance was the promise made visible, moving a stone in the night was theft against a man's grandchildren.

Then Moses returned to the courtroom, to the witness stand he had already built in chapter 17. A lone witness could never establish a charge, of any kind, against anyone. "A matter must be established by the testimony of two or three witnesses." But two witnesses could lie as well as one, so the law built a trap for the liar. Both parties were to stand before the LORD, before the priests and judges in office. "The judges shall investigate thoroughly, and if the witness is proven to be a liar who has falsely accused his brother," then the penalty the liar had aimed at his brother landed on the liar instead: "you must do to him as he intended to do to his brother".

Here, and only here in the speech, Moses laid down the measure in its famous form: "You must show no pity: life for life, eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, and foot for foot." It stands in the text not as a license for revenge but as the sentence on a false witness, the punishment fitted exactly to the harm he tried to do by lying. And the refrain sounded over it a third time: the rest of the people would hear and be afraid, and never again do anything so evil among them.

Chapter 20 took the army out. "When you go out to war against your enemies and see horses, chariots, and an army larger than yours, do not be afraid of them; for the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, is with you." Before a blow was struck, the priest stepped forward with an address of his own: "Hear, O Israel, today you are going into battle with your enemies. Do not be fainthearted or afraid; do not be alarmed or terrified because of them. For the LORD your God goes with you to fight for you against your enemies, to give you the victory."

Then the officers shrank the army on purpose. "Has any man built a new house and not dedicated it? Let him return home, or he may die in battle and another man dedicate it." The same question went to the man who had planted a vineyard and not yet enjoyed its fruit, and to the man pledged to a woman and not yet married. Last, the officers asked the question no recruiting speech asks: "Is any man afraid or fainthearted? Let him return home, so that the hearts of his brothers will not melt like his own." Only after the sendings-home did the officers appoint commanders and lead the army out. The ranks that marched were the ranks that remained after every man with an unfinished life, and every man whose fear might spread, had been sent back to his house.

When the army reached a city, the first weapon was an offer. "When you approach a city to fight against it, you are to make an offer of peace." A city far away that opened its gates lived, and served. A city that refused war was besieged, and when God delivered it, its men fell and its spoil was taken. But the cities of the land itself, the inheritance, were another matter: "you must not leave alive anything that breathes." Moses gave the reason in the same breath, and it was the reason running under this whole section of the speech: "so that they cannot teach you to do all the detestable things they do for their gods, and so cause you to sin against the LORD your God."

The chapter's last law was aimed at the soldiers' own axes. In a long siege, the fruit trees around a city were not to be cut down for siege works, "because you can eat their fruit". Only trees that bore no food could be felled for the ramparts. And Moses left the army with a question that sounds almost like mercy reasoning with a siege line: "Are the trees of the field human, that you should besiege them?"

Courts, a king under a book, a tribe fed by the altar, a prophet still to come, cities of refuge with roads running to them, and an army that sends its fearful home. The machinery of a nation was on the table. What remained was smaller and harder: the cases waiting inside ordinary life, a lost ox, a found cloak, a wage unpaid at sundown. Moses turned to those next.
