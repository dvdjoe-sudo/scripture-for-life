1 Kings Episode 7: Kings and Prophets
1 Kings 15-16, Berean Standard Bible

From here the book changes its gait. The long scenes give way to a rhythm, and the rhythm is a measuring stick. A king begins to reign, in a year counted by the other kingdom's king. He reigns so many years. He does right, or he does evil, in the eyes of the LORD. He is measured against one of two men: against David, the standard for Judah, or against Jeroboam son of Nebat and the sin he made Israel commit, the standard for the north. The rest of his acts are in the chronicles. He rests with his fathers. His son reigns in his place. Over and over the formula falls, like a bell struck for each reign, and inside the formula the two kingdoms slide in opposite directions at different speeds. Judah gets a lamp kept burning for David's sake. Israel gets coups.

Judah first, because Judah's thread is the slower one. Abijam, Rehoboam's son, reigned three years and walked in his father's sins, and the only reason the story continues past him is stated as a doctrine: “Nevertheless, for the sake of David, the LORD his God gave him a lamp in Jerusalem by raising up a son to succeed him and to make Jerusalem strong.” Then the book pauses over David himself, generations dead, and gives its final assessment of the man the whole story bends around: “For David had done what was right in the eyes of the LORD and had not turned aside from anything the LORD commanded all the days of his life, except in the matter of Uriah the Hittite.” One exception, named, and it is the Bathsheba matter, the wound at the center of 2 Samuel. Everything else about David, the book files as right. Abijam's son Asa reigned forty-one years, the long bright reign of this stretch, and he did what was right as David had done. He banished the male shrine prostitutes and tore down the idols his fathers had made, and when his own grandmother Maacah, the queen mother, made a detestable Asherah pole, Asa deposed her and chopped the pole down and burned it in the Kidron Valley. Reform reached his own house. And still the formula keeps its honesty about him in a single verse with the halves side by side: “The high places were not removed, but Asa's heart was fully devoted to the LORD all his days.”

Asa's reign also shows what the border war now cost. Baasha of Israel fortified Ramah, a few miles north of Jerusalem, to blockade Judah's roads and strangle the kingdom's movement. Asa's answer was to empty what was left of the treasuries, the silver and gold remaining in the house of the LORD and the palace, Shishak had taken the bulk a generation before, and send it all to Ben-hadad of Damascus with a request to break his treaty with Baasha and attack from the north. It worked exactly as purchased. Ben-hadad struck Ijon and Dan and Abel-beth-maacah and the whole region of Naphtali, Baasha abandoned Ramah and withdrew, and Asa conscripted every man in Judah without exception to haul Ramah's stones and timbers away and build Geba and Mizpah out of the enemy's abandoned fortress. Shrewd, effective, and bought with the temple's remaining wealth: the text reports the transaction and the triumph in the same flat voice and lets both stand. In his old age Asa's feet became diseased, and he rested with his fathers, and Jehoshaphat his son reigned. The refrain of the era runs under both kingdoms like a drumbeat: there was war between Asa and Baasha throughout their days.

Israel's thread runs faster and bloodier, and it runs on a sentence spoken at Shiloh. Nadab son of Jeroboam reigned two years, did evil, walked in his father's way, and was struck down by Baasha son of Ahijah while he and all Israel were besieging Gibbethon. Baasha then did to Jeroboam's house what Ahijah had promised from the day of the disguised wife: “He did not leave to Jeroboam anyone who breathed, but destroyed them all according to the word that the LORD had spoken through His servant Ahijah the Shilonite”. The dynasty God had offered to build for Jeroboam ended in extermination by the next usurper, two reigns in. Baasha reigned twenty-four years and walked in Jeroboam's way himself, and the word of the LORD came to Jehu son of Hanani against him with the trap inside the judgment spelled out. God had lifted Baasha out of the dust and made him ruler, and Baasha had become Jeroboam over again, so his house would become Jeroboam's house: “Anyone belonging to Baasha who dies in the city will be eaten by dogs, and anyone who dies in the field will be eaten by the birds of the air.” The identical sentence, word for word, that Ahijah had pronounced on the house Baasha himself had exterminated. The executioner of God's judgment on one dynasty heard the same judgment on his own, partly, the text notes, because of the very exterminating he had done. The wheel does not care whose hands are on it.

After Baasha, the wheel spins faster. His son Elah reigned two years and was murdered by Zimri, commander of half his chariots, while the king was in Tirzah getting drunk in his steward's house. Zimri exterminated Baasha's house to the last male, kinsman or friend, fulfilling Jehu's word, and then his own reign is measured by the book in a unit smaller than any king before him: “Zimri reigned in Tirzah for seven days.” The army at Gibbethon heard the news and proclaimed its own commander, Omri, king on the spot, marched on Tirzah, and took it, and Zimri went into the citadel of the palace and burned it down on top of himself, king for a week, dead in his own fire. Half of Israel then backed a rival, Tibni, and the kingdom fought a small civil war inside its rebellion until Tibni died and Omri stood alone. Omri reigned twelve years and did more wickedly than all before him, and made one purchase that outlasted every dynasty in the north: he bought a hill from a man named Shemer for two talents of silver and built a city on it and named it Samaria, after the man who had owned the hill. The capital he founded would give its name to the whole northern kingdom in the prophets' mouths, and outlive his house, and Omri's house itself barely outlived him.

Because Omri's son was Ahab, and with Ahab the parade stops being a parade. Ahab reigned twenty-two years in Samaria, and the formula's verdict on him breaks its own scale: “However, Ahab son of Omri did evil in the sight of the LORD, more than all who were before him.” Then the book explains, in a sentence whose contempt is barely contained: “And as if it were not enough for him to walk in the sins of Jeroboam son of Nebat, he even married Jezebel the daughter of Ethbaal king of the Sidonians, and he then proceeded to serve and worship Baal.” Jeroboam's calves had at least worn the name of Israel's God, however falsely. Ahab built Baal a temple in Samaria and set up his altar in it, and raised an Asherah pole besides, and the summary is the worst the book has given any king: “Thus Ahab did more to provoke the LORD, the God of Israel, to anger than all the kings of Israel before him.” The chapter's last line is a quiet horror set in a rebuilding project. In Ahab's days Hiel of Bethel rebuilt Jericho, the city Joshua had cursed in the days of the conquest, and the curse came due in his own family: he laid its foundation at the cost of his firstborn, Abiram, and set up its gates at the cost of his youngest, Segub, according to the word spoken through Joshua son of Nun five centuries before. The north had a Baal temple in its capital and a cursed city rising on bought ground with children's lives in its walls. The stage was fully built, and into it, in the next breath of the book, steps a prophet from Gilead with no introduction at all, to tell the king the rain is finished.
