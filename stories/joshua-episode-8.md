Joshua Episode 8: Choose This Day
Joshua 23 to 24, Berean Standard Bible

A long time passed. The land rested, the surveyors' map became addresses, and Joshua grew old in the country he had divided. Then he called Israel together one more time, its elders, leaders, judges, and officers, and the speech he gave them was a farewell in the shape of Moses' own, because Joshua had been standing nearby when Moses gave his.

He began with their eyes: "you have seen everything that the LORD your God has done to all these nations for your sake, because it was the LORD your God who fought for you." The remaining nations were already allotted on paper, and God would drive them out as promised. What remained for Israel was the charge Joshua had received first for himself: "Be very strong, then, so that you can keep and obey all that is written in the Book of the Law of Moses, not turning aside from it to the right or to the left." No association with the nations left among them, no calling on their gods' names, no serving or bowing: "Instead, you shall hold fast to the LORD your God, as you have done to this day."

He reminded them of the arithmetic of the conquest: "One of you can put a thousand to flight, because the LORD your God fights for you, just as He promised." And he set the whole duty inside a single tender sentence: "Therefore watch yourselves carefully, that you love the LORD your God."

Then the warning, as plain as Deuteronomy's curses and aimed at assimilation rather than invasion: if you turn away and cling to the nations that remain, if you intermarry and associate with them, "know for sure that the LORD your God will no longer drive out these nations before you. Instead, they will become for you a snare and a trap, a scourge in your sides and thorns in your eyes, until you perish from this good land that the LORD your God has given you."

Joshua named his own death without ceremony: "Now behold, today I am going the way of all the earth, and you know with all your heart and soul that not one of the good promises the LORD your God made to you has failed. Everything was fulfilled for you; not one promise has failed." And then he turned the verdict over, the way a coin has two faces: "But just as every good thing the LORD your God promised you has come to pass, likewise the LORD will bring upon you the calamity He has threatened, until He has destroyed you from this good land He has given you."

The last assembly met at Shechem, the valley where Abraham had built his first altar in the land and where Jacob had buried foreign gods under an oak. All the tribes presented themselves before God, and Joshua let God tell the story. It runs in the first person, I took, I gave, I sent, I brought, across the whole history the reader of this book now carries: "Long ago your fathers, including Terah the father of Abraham and Nahor, lived beyond the Euphrates and worshiped other gods. But I took your father Abraham from beyond the Euphrates and led him through all the land of Canaan, and I multiplied his descendants." Isaac, then Jacob and Esau, Seir given to one and Egypt swallowing the others. "Then I sent Moses and Aaron, and I afflicted the Egyptians by what I did there, and afterward I brought you out." The sea brought over the pursuers. The wilderness years, the Amorite kings, Balak hiring a curse and getting blessings instead: "but I would not listen to Balaam. So he blessed you again and again, and I delivered you from his hand." The Jordan crossed, Jericho and the seven nations delivered, the hornet sent ahead: "I sent the hornet ahead of you, and it drove out the two Amorite kings before you, but not by your own sword or bow." And the summary of the gift, the line the entire recital was building toward: "So I gave you a land on which you did not toil and cities that you did not build, and now you live in them and eat from vineyards and olive groves that you did not plant."

Then Joshua turned God's history into Israel's decision: "Now, therefore, fear the LORD and serve Him in sincerity and truth; cast aside the gods your fathers served beyond the Euphrates and in Egypt, and serve the LORD." And he forced the choice into the open with the most famous sentence he ever spoke: "But if it is unpleasing in your sight to serve the LORD, then choose for yourselves this day whom you will serve, whether the gods your fathers served beyond the Euphrates, or the gods of the Amorites in whose land you are living. As for me and my house, we will serve the LORD!"

The people answered like the congregation at the Jordan's stones had taught them to answer: "Far be it from us to forsake the LORD to serve other gods!" They rehearsed their reasons, Egypt, the signs, the protection on the journey, the nations driven out, and landed it: "We too will serve the LORD, because He is our God!"

Joshua's response startles everyone who expects a leader to accept a pledge and close the meeting. He argued with their vow: "You are not able to serve the LORD, for He is a holy God; He is a jealous God; He will not forgive your rebellion or your sins. If you forsake the LORD and serve foreign gods, He will turn and bring disaster on you and consume you, even after He has been good to you." He was not discouraging them. He was pricing the promise, making sure the yes they were about to give was weighed, not warm. They gave it anyway, louder: "No!" "We will serve the LORD!"

Then Joshua told them: "You are witnesses against yourselves that you have chosen to serve the LORD." "We are witnesses!" they said. He gave them the first act of the covenant, get rid of the foreign gods among you and incline your hearts to the LORD, and they answered with the final form of the pledge: "We will serve the LORD our God and obey His voice."

So Joshua made a covenant for the people that day at Shechem, set down a statute and ordinance, and recorded everything in the Book of the Law of God. And then, one last time in this book, somebody set up a stone. Joshua took a large stone and raised it under the oak near the sanctuary of the LORD, and he told the assembly what it was for: "You see this stone. It will be a witness against us, for it has heard all the words the LORD has spoken to us, and it will be a witness against you if you ever deny your God." Twelve stones at Gilgal, a pile in Achor, a pile at Ai's gate, a cave sealed at Makkedah, an altar named Witness by the Jordan, and now a stone under the oak at Shechem. Joshua's whole leadership can be read in rock: each one a place where God had acted and Israel had answered, set up so that forgetting would have to walk past evidence.

Then he sent the people away, each to his own inheritance, and the book closes with three burials. "Some time later, Joshua son of Nun, the servant of the LORD, died at the age of 110." He was buried at Timnath-serah, the town he had asked for, in the hill country of Ephraim. The assistant who had stood at Moses' elbow carried Moses' own title at the end, servant of the LORD, and the narrator's judgment on his era is one calm line: "Israel had served the LORD throughout the days of Joshua and of the elders who outlived him and who had experienced all the works that the LORD had done for Israel."

The second burial reached back furthest. "And the bones of Joseph, which the Israelites had brought up out of Egypt, were buried at Shechem in the plot of land that Jacob had purchased from the sons of Hamor," the field at the same Shechem where Israel had just renewed the covenant. Joseph had made his brothers swear to carry his bones up when God came to them, back in the last chapter of Genesis, and the promise had traveled through slavery, exodus, wilderness, and conquest, and it came to rest in the ground a few steps from the witness stone. The third burial was Eleazar son of Aaron, laid in the hill country at Gibeah, the town given to his son Phinehas, the priest who had carried the embassy to the altar by the Jordan.

The book ends with the land divided, the covenant renewed, the witnesses in place, and the generation that had seen the Jordan stop now under the ground it had inherited. Everything depended on whether the children who would ask about the stones would give the answer their fathers rehearsed. The next book opens with the words this one has been holding back since its first line, and they are almost the same words: after the death of Joshua.
