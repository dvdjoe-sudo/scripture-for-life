Judges Episode 4: Gideon: Fleece, and Three Hundred
Judges 6 to 7, Berean Standard Bible

The next oppression was not an army that came and fought. It was a system that came and ate. "Again the Israelites did evil in the sight of the LORD; so He delivered them into the hand of Midian for seven years." Midianites, Amalekites, and the people of the east timed their invasions to the harvest: "Whenever the Israelites planted their crops, the Midianites, Amalekites, and other people of the east would come up and invade them, encamping against them as far as Gaza and destroying the produce of the land. They left Israel with no sustenance, neither sheep nor oxen nor donkeys." They came "like a great swarm of locusts," with innumerable camels, and Israel lived in the holes of its own country, "shelters for themselves in the mountains, caves, and strongholds." Seven years of planting for someone else. "Israel was greatly impoverished by Midian, and the Israelites cried out to the LORD."

This time God answered the cry first with a prophet, not a deliverer, and the prophet brought a diagnosis instead of an army: "I brought you up out of Egypt, out of the house of slavery. I delivered you out of the hands of Egypt and all your oppressors. I drove them out before you and gave you their land." Then the charge, with the people's own gardens in view: "I am the LORD your God. You must not fear the gods of the Amorites, in whose land you dwell." And the verdict: "But you did not obey Me." No new information. Israel knew the story by heart. That was the problem the whole book keeps circling: knowing was not the same as obeying, and a generation that had been told everything was starving anyway.

The deliverer, when he came, was found hiding from the oppression he was meant to end. The angel of the LORD sat under the oak at Ophrah, "where his son Gideon was threshing wheat in a winepress to hide it from the Midianites." A winepress is a pit. You thresh wheat on an open floor where the wind takes the chaff; Gideon was doing it underground, in secret, a cupful at a time. Into that pit the angel spoke the most unlikely sentence in the book so far: "The LORD is with you, O mighty man of valor."

Gideon's reply is the honest argument of a man who knows the family stories and cannot square them with the winepress: "Please, my Lord," Gideon replied, "if the LORD is with us, why has all this happened to us? And where are all His wonders of which our fathers told us, saying," and here Gideon quotes the fathers the way the fathers were quoted at every table in Israel: "Has not the LORD brought us up out of Egypt?" Then his own conclusion, flat as a stone: "But now the LORD has forsaken us and delivered us into the hand of Midian."

God did not answer the argument. He answered the man. "Go in the strength you have and save Israel from the hand of Midian. Am I not sending you?" Gideon laid out his credentials, which were a list of smallness: "how can I save Israel? Indeed, my clan is the weakest in Manasseh, and I am the youngest in my father's house." And the promise that carries the whole Gideon story was given, the same one given to Moses at a bush and to Joshua at a river: "Surely I will be with you," the LORD replied, "and you will strike down all the Midianites as one man."

Gideon asked for a sign and brought an offering, a young goat, unleavened bread, a pot of broth, and the angel had him lay it on a rock and pour the broth over it. The staff touched the food, "and fire flared from the rock and consumed the meat and the unleavened bread. Then the angel of the LORD vanished from his sight." Gideon knew then what he had been talking to, and terror replaced argument: "Oh no, Lord GOD! I have seen the angel of the LORD face to face!" The answer was the name of the altar Gideon built on the spot: "Peace be with you. Do not be afraid, for you will not die." He called the altar The LORD Is Peace, and the text says it stood in Ophrah to the narrator's own day.

That night the commission stopped being theoretical. Gideon was ordered to tear down his own father's altar to Baal and cut down the Asherah pole beside it, and to build a proper altar to the LORD on the stronghold and offer his father's second bull on it, with the Asherah's wood for fuel. He did it, with ten servants, and the text states his fear as plainly as his obedience: "But because he was too afraid of his father's household and the men of the city, he did it by night rather than in the daytime." The first battle of the deliverer was against his father's gods, fought at midnight, and it counts in the text as done.

Morning brought the city to Joash's door demanding his son's death for the ruined altar. Joash, whose altar it had been, defended the son who destroyed it with the sharpest logic in the chapter: "Are you contending for Baal? Are you trying to save him? Whoever pleads his case will be put to death by morning! If Baal is a god, let him contend for himself with the one who has torn down his altar." If Baal needs a mob, he is no god; if he is a god, he needs no mob. Gideon walked out of that morning with a new name, Jerubbaal, let Baal contend with him, and the name stuck to him for the rest of the book, the recorder using both names as if the dare were his title.

Then the invasion came in force, Midian and Amalek and the east crossing the Jordan and camping in the Valley of Jezreel, and the pattern of the cycle finally turned: "So the Spirit of the LORD came upon Gideon, who blew the ram's horn and rallied the Abiezrites behind him." Messengers went through Manasseh and to Asher, Zebulun, and Naphtali, and this time the tribes came up. And Gideon, with an army finally gathering behind him, asked God for the sign everyone remembers, and he asked it in full knowledge of how it sounded: "If You are going to save Israel by my hand, as You have said, then behold, I will place a fleece of wool on the threshing floor. If there is dew only on the fleece and all the ground is dry, then I will know that You are going to save Israel by my hand, as You have said." Morning: wet fleece, dry ground, a bowlful of water wrung out. Then the request reversed, with its own apology built in: "Do not be angry with me; let me speak one more time. Please allow me one more test with the fleece. This time let it be dry, and the ground covered with dew." And that night God did so. The book does not rebuke the fleece. It records a frightened man asking twice and a God answering twice, and then it takes the frightened man's army away from him in stages, which is a harder test than the fleece ever was.

At the spring of Harod, with Midian camped north in the valley by the hill of Moreh, God announced the reduction and its reason: "You have too many men for Me to deliver Midian into their hands, lest Israel glorify themselves over Me, saying," and the sentence God would not let Israel say is the sentence every other victory in the ancient world ended with: "My own hand has saved me." So the fearful were invited to leave, using the law Moses had given for exactly this, and twenty-two thousand men turned back. Ten thousand remained. "There are still too many men." Down at the water God sifted them by how they drank: those who lapped water with their tongues like dogs, scooping with their hands to their mouths, against those who knelt down to drink. The lappers numbered three hundred. "With the three hundred men who lapped the water I will save you and deliver the Midianites into your hand. But all the others are to go home." Thirty-two thousand had become three hundred, and the three hundred took the provisions and the rams' horns of the men sent home, horns and food for an army that was about to fight with noise and light instead of numbers.

That night God offered the frightened commander one more kindness, unasked: "Get up and go down against the camp, for I have delivered it into your hand. But if you are afraid to do so, then go down to the camp with your servant Purah and listen to what they are saying." Gideon went down, because he was afraid, and at the outposts of a camp lying in the valley "like a swarm of locusts," with camels "as countless as the sand on the seashore," he heard an enemy soldier tell a dream: "Behold, I had a dream," he said, "and I saw a loaf of barley bread come tumbling into the Midianite camp. It struck the tent so hard that the tent overturned and collapsed." Barley bread, the poor man's loaf, a nobody's food, rolling into the camp of kings. And the friend interpreted it on the spot, by name: "This is nothing less than the sword of Gideon son of Joash, the Israelite. God has delivered Midian and the whole camp into his hand." Gideon, who had asked God for sign after sign, received the last one from the enemy's own mouth, and he bowed in worship in the dark outside the camp of the men who feared him.

The battle, when it came, was mostly sound and light. Three hundred men in three companies, each with a ram's horn in one hand and a large jar holding a torch in the other, took positions around the camp. "Watch me and do as I do," Gideon said. "When I and all who are with me blow our horns, then you are also to blow your horns from all around the camp and shout," and the shout he gave them put God's name first: "For the LORD and for Gideon!" At the beginning of the middle watch, just after the guard changed, three hundred horns blew at once and three hundred jars smashed, torches flaring out of the dark on every side, and the cry went up: "A sword for the LORD and for Gideon!" The text is careful about what the three hundred did next: "Each Israelite took his position around the camp, and the entire Midianite army fled, crying out as they ran." They stood still. The camp did the rest: "And when the three hundred rams' horns sounded, the LORD set all the men in the camp against one another with their swords." An army of locusts woke in darkness to horns and fire on every side, assumed a vast force had surrounded it, and killed itself.

The fugitives ran for the Jordan, and now the tribes Gideon had sent home entered the story he had been reduced to win without them: men of Naphtali, Asher, and Manasseh were called out in pursuit, and Ephraim seized the waters of the Jordan ahead of the fugitives as far as Beth-barah. Ephraim also captured Midian's two princes, Oreb and Zeeb, raven and wolf, and killed them, Oreb at the rock of Oreb and Zeeb at the winepress of Zeeb, places that took their names from the deaths. They brought the heads of the two princes to Gideon on the other side of the Jordan. The winepress Gideon hid in has become a place where a Midianite prince died, and the deliverer is across the river now, in pursuit, holding the heads of princes, and about to discover that victory has its own dangers: the men of Ephraim are coming to him angry, and what he does with the next few conversations will shape the rest of his life.
