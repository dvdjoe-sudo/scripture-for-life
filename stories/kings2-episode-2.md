2 Kings Episode 2: Water in the Desert
2 Kings 3, Berean Standard Bible

Elisha's first public work was done in a war he wanted no part of. Jehoram, Ahab's son, now reigned in Samaria. He was not his father and mother, the text is careful to say: he removed the sacred pillar of Baal Ahab had made. “Nevertheless, he clung to the sins that Jeroboam son of Nebat had caused Israel to commit; he did not turn away from them.” Moab had paid Israel a yearly tribute of a hundred thousand lambs and the wool of a hundred thousand rams, and with Ahab dead, Moab stopped paying. Jehoram mobilized Israel and called on Judah to join the campaign, and Jehoshaphat answered with the old alliance in full: “I am as you are, my people are your people, and my horses are your horses”. The road Jehoram chose went around by the Desert of Edom, seven days of marching with the king of Edom's forces added, and at the end of it there was no water, for the army or for the animals dying around them.

Jehoram read the disaster as a trap: he cried out, “Alas,” said the king of Israel, “for the LORD has summoned these three kings to deliver them into the hand of Moab!” Jehoshaphat asked the question he always asked, whether a prophet of the LORD was anywhere near, and one of Jehoram's own servants knew the answer: “Elisha son of Shaphat is here. He used to pour water on the hands of Elijah.” Jehoshaphat affirmed: “The word of the LORD is with him.” So three kings went down to a prophet's camp to ask for help, and Elisha greeted the king of Israel with open contempt: “What have we to do with each other? Go to the prophets of your father and of your mother!” Only Jehoshaphat's presence bought Jehoram an answer at all: “As surely as the LORD of Hosts lives, before whom I stand, were it not for my regard for the presence of Jehoshaphat king of Judah, I would not look at you or acknowledge you.” Then the prophet asked for music, a harpist, and while the harpist played, the hand of the LORD came on him.

The word he spoke was strange and practical: “Dig this valley full of ditches.” Rain would not do this the ordinary way. “You will not see wind or rain, but the valley will be filled with water, and you will drink”, and your cattle and your animals with you. “This is a simple matter in the sight of the LORD”, Elisha said, “and He will also deliver the Moabites into your hand.” The kings were told to press the victory hard: every fortified city attacked, every good tree cut down, every spring stopped, every good field ruined with stones. The next morning, at the hour of the morning sacrifice, water flowed into the valley from the direction of Edom and filled the land. Across the border, the Moabites had mustered everyone who could bear arms and were watching at dawn. The sun shone on the water and it looked red to them, red as blood, and they read it as their deliverance: “This is blood!” they exclaimed. “The kings have clashed swords and slaughtered one another. Now to the plunder, Moab!” They charged a camp that was waiting for them, and Israel rose up and struck them down and invaded Moab, doing everything the word had listed, city by city, field by field, spring by spring, until only Kir-haraseth stood, ringed by slingers.

Then the episode ends with the scene it will not explain. Mesha, king of Moab, tried to break through to the king of Edom with seven hundred swordsmen and failed, and from the wall of his last city, in sight of the armies below, “he took his firstborn son, who was to succeed him, and offered him as a burnt offering on the city wall.” Whatever the watchers below made of it, the text records the effect in one flat sentence: “there was great fury against the Israelites, so they withdrew and returned to their own land.” The victory the LORD had given stopped at that wall. The book does not say what the fury was, whose it was, or what the kings thought as they marched home, and this retelling leaves the silence exactly where the book leaves it: water in a dry valley, given freely, and a war that ended in a way nobody in it had planned.
