Ruth Episode 1: Where You Go, I Will Go
Ruth 1, Berean Standard Bible

The book of Judges closed with a nation doing what was right in its own eyes, and this small book opens inside the same years, in the same small country, with a famine: "In the days when the judges ruled, there was a famine in the land." The famine reached Bethlehem, whose name means house of bread, and a man named Elimelech did what families did in famines all through Genesis. He took his wife Naomi and his two sons, Mahlon and Chilion, and crossed east into Moab to wait it out. Moab was not a neutral address. It was the nation born in a cave in Genesis 19, the nation of Balak and Balaam, the nation whose king Eglon had held Israel for eighteen years in the days of Ehud. Elimelech's family were Ephrathites of Bethlehem, Israelites of Judah, settling as sojourners among a people with a long history against their own.

In Moab, Elimelech died. His sons married Moabite women, one named Orpah and the other named Ruth, and the family stayed about ten years, long enough for a sojourn to become a life. Then both sons died as well, "and Naomi was left without her two sons and without her husband." The text piles the losses in one line and moves on, because Naomi is the one left holding them. A widow in a foreign country, with no sons, no grandchildren, and no claim on anyone, was as close to nothing as the ancient world had a category for. Everything she had carried into Moab was buried there.

What turned her toward home was news framed as a visitation: "When Naomi heard in Moab that the LORD had attended to His people by providing them with food, she and her daughters-in-law prepared to leave the land of Moab." It is worth noticing, here at the start, how this book handles God. He acts directly only twice in its four chapters, once here, giving His people food, and once at the very end. Everything between those two acts is people: their speeches, their choices, their kindnesses, with heaven mostly listening. Naomi heard that the famine at home had broken, and she set out on the road back to Judah with both her daughters-in-law beside her.

Partway along the road, she stopped and tried to send them home, and her farewell is the first sounding of the word this whole book is built on: "Go back, each of you to your mother’s home. May the LORD show you loving devotion, as you have shown to your dead and to me. May the LORD enable each of you to find rest in the home of your new husband." Loving devotion is the translation's phrase for the Hebrew word hesed, covenant kindness, loyalty that acts. Naomi blesses the young widows with the very thing they have shown her, and then releases them from her with a kiss. Both of them wept and refused: "Surely we will return with you to your people."

Naomi answered with arithmetic, and the arithmetic was merciless. "Return home, my daughters. Why would you go with me? Are there still sons in my womb to become your husbands?" Even if she married that night and bore sons, would they wait, unmarried, for boys to grow up? The custom of a brother raising up heirs for the dead, which will drive the whole second half of this book, is here stated only to be ruled impossible. And under the arithmetic sat her verdict on herself: "it is much more bitter for me than for you, because the hand of the LORD has gone out against me." She was not protecting them from a hard trip. She was telling them there was nothing at the end of it but her.

Orpah kissed her and went back, and the text holds no grudge against her for it; she did the sensible thing her mother-in-law had twice commanded. Ruth did something else. The verse says she clung to Naomi, and the Hebrew word is the Bible's word for a husband holding fast to his wife in Genesis. What she did next she did out loud, in the most famous speech in the book, spoken by a Moabite widow to an Israelite one on an open road: "Do not urge me to leave you or to turn from following you. For wherever you go, I will go, and wherever you live, I will live; your people will be my people, and your God will be my God. Where you die, I will die, and there I will be buried. May the LORD punish me, and ever so severely, if anything but death separates you and me."

Every line of it is a door closing behind her. Not a visit, a relocation of existence: your road, your home, your people, your God, your grave. And she sealed it by swearing in the name of Naomi's God, the LORD, a God who was not hers by birth or by her people's worship, calling His judgment down on herself if she broke it. Naomi argued no further. "When Naomi saw that Ruth was determined to go with her, she stopped trying to persuade her." The two of them walked the rest of the way, down out of Moab's hills, across the Jordan valley, and up into Judah, mostly in silence, as far as the text is concerned. The book gives Ruth one great speech and then lets her be quiet for a long time.

Bethlehem noticed them come in. When they entered Bethlehem, the whole town was stirred because of them, and the women of the town exclaimed: "Can this be Naomi?" It had been more than ten years, and she had left with a husband and two sons and come back with a Moabite daughter-in-law and nothing else. Her answer to the town is the theological center of the chapter, and it is an accusation she makes against heaven with her chin up: "Do not call me Naomi," she replied. "Call me Mara, because the Almighty has dealt quite bitterly with me. I went away full, but the LORD has brought me back empty. Why call me Naomi? After all, the LORD has testified against me, and the Almighty has afflicted me." Naomi means pleasant. Mara means bitter. She was renaming herself after her losses and charging them, in public, to the account of the Almighty, and nobody in the town argued with her, and the narrator does not correct her either. The book lets her say it.

But the narrator does place one fact beside her speech, the way this book places all its hope, quietly and in passing. She had come back empty, she said, at the exact moment the harvest that feeds people was beginning, with Ruth standing next to her: "So Naomi returned from the land of Moab with her daughter-in-law Ruth the Moabitess. And they arrived in Bethlehem at the beginning of the barley harvest." Full and empty are Naomi's words. The barley was God's timing, unannounced. What a penniless foreign widow could do in a town at barley harvest was go out to the fields, and that is where Ruth goes next, in the morning, to work.
