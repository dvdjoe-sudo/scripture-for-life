1 Samuel Episode 3: Give Us a King
1 Samuel 8 to 10, Berean Standard Bible

The system that held Israel together for twenty years had the same flaw as the one before it, and it showed up in the same place: the judge's sons. Samuel grew old and appointed his boys, Joel and Abijah, as judges in Beersheba, and the text gives them the epitaph Eli's sons earned a generation earlier, in the vocabulary of the courthouse instead of the sanctuary: "But his sons did not walk in his ways; they turned aside toward dishonest gain, accepting bribes and perverting justice." Twice now a faithful man's household had rotted at the top, and the elders of Israel drew the conclusion that seemed obvious and reached, with it, for the most consequential demand in the nation's history. They came to Samuel at Ramah with a diagnosis and a prescription. The diagnosis was true: "you are old, and your sons do not walk in your ways." The prescription was the problem: "Now appoint a king to judge us like all the other nations."

Samuel took it as a personal rejection and prayed, and God's answer reframed the entire request in one sentence: "Listen to the voice of the people in all that they say to you. For it is not you they have rejected, but they have rejected Me as their king." Not a succession plan. A regime change. Since Egypt they had been doing this, God said, forsaking Him for other gods, and now the forsaking had found its final form: they wanted a king of flesh to do what their invisible King had been doing since the Exodus, judging them and fighting their battles. God's instruction was to grant it, but to price it first, solemnly, in public. So Samuel delivered the warning, and the warning is built on one verb, repeated like hammer blows. The king will take. He will take your sons for his chariots and his plowing and his weapons. He will take your daughters to be perfumers, cooks, and bakers. He will take the best of your fields and vineyards and olive groves and give them to his servants. He will take a tenth of your grain, a tenth of your flocks, your servants, your cattle, your donkeys, "and you yourselves will become his slaves". And then the line that makes the speech prophecy rather than politics: "When that day comes, you will beg for relief from the king you have chosen, but the LORD will not answer you on that day."

They heard the whole price list. They said no to the warning itself: "We must have a king over us. Then we will be like all the other nations, with a king to judge us, to go out before us, and to fight our battles." Like all the other nations. Go out before us and fight our battles. Within living memory the LORD had thundered the Philistines off a praying congregation at Mizpah without Israel lifting a sword, and the elders' request was still this: give us a man we can see, to do reliably what God does on His own schedule. God told Samuel to listen to their voice and appoint the king. Judgment and mercy arrive in the same act here, because the man God had already chosen to send was, in God's own words about him, a rescuer: He had looked on His people, and their cry had come to Him. Israel would get its king. Israel would also get exactly the king its request described.

He came from the smallest tribe, the one the other tribes had nearly exterminated in the last chapters of Judges. Kish the Benjamite had a son named Saul, "choice and handsome, without equal among the Israelites", and, in the detail the book will keep using like a measuring stick, "a head taller than any of the people". The future king enters the story herding lost livestock. His father's donkeys had wandered off, and Saul and a servant searched the hills for days and found nothing, and were about to give up when the servant suggested consulting the seer in the nearby town, a man of God whose word always came to pass. He meant Samuel. Saul worried about the gift etiquette, the servant produced a quarter shekel of silver from his own pocket, and Israel's first king walked into his destiny looking for donkeys, carrying somebody else's money, to ask a prophet for directions.

God had spent the previous day preparing the prophet. "At this time tomorrow I will send you a man from the land of Benjamin, and you are to anoint him ruler over My people Israel; he will save them from the hand of the Philistines. For I have looked upon My people, because their cry has come to Me." When the tall young stranger appeared in the gateway, God named him to Samuel on the spot: "Here is the man of whom I spoke; he shall rule over My people." Saul asked the seer where the seer's house was, and Samuel answered, I am he, and proceeded to unmake Saul's errand piece by piece. The donkeys were found; forget them. The place of honor at the feast was his. The cook brought out a leg of meat that had been set aside on orders before Saul ever left home, and Samuel said it had been kept for this occasion. And Saul, to his credit, pushed back with the truth about himself as he knew it: "Am I not a Benjamite from the smallest tribe of Israel, and is not my clan the least of all the clans of Benjamin? So why would you say such a thing to me?" He meant it. Remember that he meant it. The tragedy of Saul is not that his humility was fake at the start; it is what happened to it.

On the roof that night Samuel talked with him, and in the morning, at the edge of the city, he sent the servant ahead and took out a flask of oil and poured it on Saul's head and kissed him: "Has not the LORD anointed you ruler over His inheritance?" Then came the signs, three of them for one day's walk home: men at Rachel's tomb with news of the donkeys, three pilgrims at the Oak of Tabor who would hand him bread, and at Gibeah of God a band of prophets coming down from the high place, at which point the Spirit of the LORD would rush on him and he would prophesy and be turned into another man. It all happened, exactly, in order. The neighbors who saw the donkey-herder ranting with the prophets asked the question that became a proverb on the spot: "What has happened to the son of Kish? Is Saul also among the prophets?" And when his uncle quizzed him, Saul told him about the donkeys and said not one word about the kingship. Whether that was humility or the first instinct to hide, the text lets it sit there unnamed, the way it let the baggage sit later.

Samuel summoned the nation to Mizpah, the same ground where the thunder had rolled, and before he ran the lot he made them hear what the lot meant. He recited the Exodus, the rescues, the whole record, and then named their day: they had rejected the God who saves them from all their troubles, and had said to Him, "No, set a king over us." Then tribe by tribe, clan by clan, the choice narrowed: Benjamin, the smallest; the clan of Matri; and Saul son of Kish. And Saul could not be found. They inquired of the LORD again, and the answer has embarrassed kingship ever since: "Behold, he has hidden himself among the baggage." They ran and fetched him out of the luggage, and stood him up in the crowd, and he was a head taller than anyone there, and Samuel presented him with the people's own criteria satisfied in full view: "Do you see the one the LORD has chosen? There is no one like him among all the people." And all the people shouted, "Long live the king!" Samuel wrote the rights and duties of kingship on a scroll and laid it up before the LORD, a written constitution for a job they had just been warned about, and everyone went home. Saul went home to Gibeah with a band of men whose hearts God had touched. And some worthless men looked at the tall, silent, baggage-hiding new king and asked the question the Philistines were about to answer: "How can this man save us?" They brought him no gifts. Saul heard about it, and held his peace, and said nothing at all.
