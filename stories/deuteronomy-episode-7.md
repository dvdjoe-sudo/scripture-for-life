Deuteronomy Episode 7: Choose Life, a Song, and the Mountain
Deuteronomy 30 to 34, Berean Standard Bible

Moses' last address began where chapter 28 had ended, with the disaster already done. "When all these things come upon you" and Israel called them to mind among the nations where God had banished them, the road home stood open. Return, obey with all heart and soul, and the promise answered in kind: "Then He will restore you from captivity and have compassion on you and gather you from all the nations to which the LORD your God has scattered you." No exile was too far to be gathered from. "Even if you have been banished to the farthest horizon, He will gather you and return you from there."

And the return would go deeper than geography. Back in chapter 10, Moses had commanded the people to circumcise their own hearts. Here, at the end, he said God would do it: "The LORD your God will circumcise your hearts and the hearts of your descendants, and you will love Him with all your heart and with all your soul, so that you may live." The curses would fall instead on the enemies who had hated and persecuted them, and the LORD would delight in their prosperity again, as He had delighted in their fathers'.

Then Moses cut off the last excuse, that obedience might be out of reach. "For this commandment I give you today is not too difficult for you or beyond your reach." It was not stored in heaven, needing someone to climb for it. It was not beyond the sea, needing a sailor. The two imagined errands stood in the speech as questions no one had to ask: "Who will ascend into heaven to get it for us and proclaim it, that we may obey it?" "Who will cross the sea to get it for us and proclaim it, that we may obey it?" No errand was needed. "But the word is very near you; it is in your mouth and in your heart, so that you may obey it."

So the whole book came down to one setting-before. "See, I have set before you today life and prosperity, as well as death and disaster." Love the LORD, walk in His ways, keep His commands, and live and increase. Turn away, bow to other gods, and perish, the days in the land cut short. Then Moses called his witnesses, the same heaven and earth he had called at the start of the speeches, and gave the command the whole farewell had been building toward: "I call heaven and earth as witnesses against you today that I have set before you life and death, blessing and cursing. Therefore choose life, so that you and your descendants may live". And the reason under the choosing was a person, not a prize: "For He is your life, and He will prolong your life in the land that the LORD swore to give to your fathers, to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob."

Chapter 31 opened with a farewell of a different kind. Moses told the people his own news plainly: "I am now a hundred and twenty years old; I am no longer able to come and go, and the LORD has said to me," and here his own sentence from God stood bare: "You shall not cross the Jordan." He would not lead the crossing. But the crossing would not be leaderless. "The LORD your God Himself will cross over ahead of you. He will destroy these nations before you, and you will dispossess them. Joshua will cross ahead of you, as the LORD has said." Sihon and Og were named as the pattern of what God would do to the kings across the river.

To all Israel he gave the charge that would follow Joshua's name through the wars to come: "Be strong and courageous; do not be afraid or terrified of them, for it is the LORD your God who goes with you; He will never leave you nor forsake you." Then he called Joshua forward and said it again in front of everyone, so the people heard the promise made to their next leader with their own ears: "Be strong and courageous, for you will go with this people into the land that the LORD swore to their fathers to give them, and you shall give it to them as an inheritance." And again: "The LORD Himself goes before you; He will be with you. He will never leave you nor forsake you. Do not be afraid or discouraged."

Before Moses left them, he made sure the words would outstay both him and Joshua. He wrote this law down and gave it to the priests who carried the ark and to all the elders, with a standing order: at the end of every seven years, in the year of release, at the Feast of Tabernacles, the law was to be read aloud "in the hearing of all Israel." The assembly for the reading named everyone: men, women, children, and the foreigners within the gates, gathered "so that they may listen and learn to fear the LORD your God and to follow carefully all the words of this law". And the point of the seven-year reading was the audience that had not been born yet: "Then their children who do not know the law will listen and learn to fear the LORD your God, as long as you live in the land that you are crossing the Jordan to possess."

Then the LORD called Moses and Joshua together to the Tent of Meeting, and appeared in the pillar of cloud over the entrance, and told Moses the future without softening a word of it. "You will soon rest with your fathers, and these people will rise up and prostitute themselves with the foreign gods of the land they are entering. They will forsake Me and break the covenant I have made with them." God's anger would burn; He would hide His face; troubles would come; and the people, in that day, would ask the question God quoted back before they ever asked it: "Have not these disasters come upon us because our God is no longer with us?"

The remedy God ordered was not another speech. It was a song. "Now therefore, write down for yourselves this song and teach it to the Israelites; have them recite it, so that it may be a witness for Me against them." A speech can be left behind; a song is carried in the mouth. When the good land had made them full and they had turned to other gods, the song would still be there: "This song will testify against them, because it will not be forgotten from the lips of their descendants. For I know their inclination, even before I bring them into the land that I swore to give them." That very day Moses wrote it and taught it.

At the tent, the LORD commissioned Joshua Himself: "Be strong and courageous, for you will bring the Israelites into the land that I swore to give them, and I will be with you." And when Moses had finished writing the words of this law in a book, from beginning to end, he ordered the Levites who carried the ark to put the book not inside it but beside it: "Take this Book of the Law and place it beside the ark of the covenant of the LORD your God, so that it may remain there as a witness against you." The reason he gave them was not tender: "For I know how rebellious and stiff-necked you are. If you are already rebelling against the LORD while I am still alive, how much more will you rebel after my death!" Then he gathered the elders and officers, called heaven and earth to witness against them one more time, and recited the song to the whole assembly, from beginning to end.

The song opens by calling the same witnesses. "Give ear, O heavens, and I will speak; hear, O earth, the words of my mouth." Its teaching was to fall like rain and settle like dew, and its first doctrine was a name: "He is the Rock, His work is perfect; all His ways are just. A God of faithfulness without injustice, righteous and upright is He." Against that Rock the song set the people: a perverse and crooked generation, repaying their Father and Creator with corruption. "Is He not your Father and Creator? Has He not made you and established you?" Remember the days of old, the song said. Ask your father. Ask your elders.

Then the song sang the rescue the way a poet sings it. God found Jacob in a desert land, in a barren, howling wilderness, guarded him "as the apple of His eye", and carried him the way an eagle carries its young: "As an eagle stirs up its nest and hovers over its young, He spread His wings to catch them and carried them on His pinions. The LORD alone led him, and no foreign god was with him." He fed him honey from the rock and oil from the flinty crag, curds and milk and the fat of lambs, the choicest wheat, wine from the finest grapes.

"But Jeshurun grew fat and kicked". Jeshurun, the song's fond name for Israel, grown fat and bloated and gorged on the gifts, "abandoned the God who made him and scorned the Rock of his salvation." They provoked Him with foreign gods, sacrificed to demons and to new gods their fathers had never feared, and the song's saddest line is a forgetting, not a rebellion: "You ignored the Rock who brought you forth; you forgot the God who gave you birth."

God's answer in the song is a hidden face and a measured fire, disasters heaped, arrows spent, hunger and pestilence and the sword outside and terror inside. And then, astonishingly, a restraint, and the reason for it is not Israel's merit. God said He would have cut them to pieces and blotted out their memory from mankind, had He not dreaded the enemy's taunt, the conqueror's misreading of his own victory: "Our own hand has prevailed; it was not the LORD who did all this." Even in judgment, the song says, God guarded His name among the nations. The foolish nation that provoked Him would be answered in kind, made jealous by those who were not a people. But the vault was sealed and the day was coming: "Vengeance is Mine; I will repay. In due time their foot will slip; for their day of disaster is near, and their doom is coming quickly." The LORD would vindicate His people and have compassion on His servants when their strength was gone, and the gods they had run to would be asked where they were, the rock that ate their sacrifices, summoned to rise and shelter them now. Over the silence stands the declaration the song was built to carry: "See now that I am He; there is no God besides Me. I bring death and I give life; I wound and I heal, and there is no one who can deliver from My hand."

Moses finished the song and spoke his own last charge over it, and it was about children again. "Take to heart all the words I have solemnly declared to you this day, so that you may command your children to carefully follow all the words of this law." These words, he told them, were the opposite of decoration: "For they are not idle words to you, because they are your life, and by them you will live long in the land that you are crossing the Jordan to possess."

That same day, the LORD told Moses to climb. "Go up into the Abarim Range to Mount Nebo, in the land of Moab across from Jericho, and view the land of Canaan, which I am giving to the Israelites as their own possession." There he would die and be gathered to his people, as Aaron had died on Mount Hor, and God gave the reason one final time, stated to Moses' face: "For at the waters of Meribah-kadesh in the Wilderness of Zin, both of you broke faith with Me among the Israelites by failing to treat Me as holy in their presence." And the sentence in its gentlest form: "Although you shall see from a distance the land that I am giving the Israelites, you shall not enter it."

Before the climb, Moses blessed the tribes, and the text introduces him for the last time by the title that outranks prophet: "This is the blessing that Moses the man of God pronounced upon the Israelites before his death." The blessing opened at Sinai, where the whole story of the law began: "The LORD came from Sinai and dawned upon us from Seir; He shone forth from Mount Paran and came with myriads of holy ones, with flaming fire at His right hand." Then tribe by tribe. Reuben: let him live and not die, nor his men be few. Judah: hear his cry and bring him to his people, and be his help against his foes. Levi, the tribe that had said of father and mother "I do not consider them", keeper of the Urim and the Thummim, tested at Massah and Meribah: "He will teach Your ordinances to Jacob and Your law to Israel". Benjamin, the beloved of the LORD, resting secure, shielded all day long, resting on God's shoulders. Joseph longest of all, his land blessed with the dew of heaven and the deep waters below, the best of the ancient mountains, "and with the favor of Him who dwelt in the burning bush", his horns the horns of a wild ox goring the nations to the ends of the earth, the myriads of Ephraim and the thousands of Manasseh. Zebulun rejoicing in his journeys and Issachar in his tents, feasting on the abundance of the seas. Gad, the lion who chose the best land. Dan, "a lion's cub, leaping out of Bashan." Naphtali, abounding with favor. Asher, most blessed of sons, his foot dipped in oil, his gate bolts iron and bronze: "and may your strength match your days." Simeon's name is not spoken in the chapter. The blessing covered the tribes and closed not on any tribe but on God: "There is none like the God of Jeshurun, who rides the heavens to your aid, and the clouds in His majesty. The eternal God is your dwelling place, and underneath are the everlasting arms." And its last word to Israel was joy: "Blessed are you, O Israel! Who is like you, a people saved by the LORD? He is the shield that protects you, the sword in which you boast."

"Then Moses went up from the plains of Moab to Mount Nebo, to the top of Pisgah, which faces Jericho. And the LORD showed him the whole land". The view ran the full map of the promise: Gilead as far as Dan, all of Naphtali, the land of Ephraim and Manasseh, all the land of Judah as far as the Western Sea, the Negev, and the region from the Valley of Jericho, the City of Palms, all the way to Zoar. And God spoke the last words Moses would hear: "This is the land that I swore to give Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob when I said," and the old promise stood inside the sentence: "I will give it to your descendants." Then the boundary, kept to the end: "I have let you see it with your own eyes, but you will not cross into it."

"So Moses the servant of the LORD died there in the land of Moab, as the LORD had said. And He buried him in a valley in the land of Moab facing Beth-peor, and no one to this day knows the location of his grave." No monument, no shrine, no place to make a pilgrimage of. The man who spoke with God face to face was buried by God, in a grave no one can find. "Moses was a hundred and twenty years old when he died, yet his eyes were not weak, and his vitality had not diminished." He did not fade. He finished. Israel grieved for him in the plains of Moab thirty days, until the time of mourning came to an end.

And the story was already in other hands. "Now Joshua son of Nun was filled with the spirit of wisdom because Moses had laid his hands on him. So the Israelites obeyed him and did as the LORD had commanded Moses." Then the five books close with the epitaph, the narrator's own last word on the man: "Since that time, no prophet has risen in Israel like Moses, whom the LORD knew face to face", no prophet who did all the signs and wonders in Egypt, "and no prophet who performed all the mighty acts of power and awesome deeds that Moses did in the sight of all Israel."

The Torah ends there, east of the river, with the promise seen and unentered and the leader buried out of sight. Genesis began with God speaking a world into being; Deuteronomy ends with a grave no one can find and a people standing at the border with a book, a song, and a new leader. The next voice in the story belongs to Joshua.
