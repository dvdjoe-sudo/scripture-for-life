Leviticus Episode 1: Offerings at the Tent
Leviticus 1 to 7, Berean Standard Bible

The tabernacle was finished, and God had filled it. That was the problem no one in the camp had faced before.

At the end of Exodus, the cloud covered the Tent of Meeting and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle. Moses was unable to enter. The God who had brought Israel out of Egypt now lived in the middle of their camp, in a tent surrounded by their tents, and even Moses stood outside.

Leviticus opens from inside that tent.

"Then the LORD called to Moses and spoke to him from the Tent of Meeting, saying,"

God called Moses toward Him and gave him a way for the whole people to come near. The way was offerings. Again and again in these seven chapters, a man or woman who had something to bring, or something to confess, or something to repay, would walk to the entrance of the Tent of Meeting leading an animal, and what happened there followed a pattern a first-time reader can learn once and then watch repeat.

The animal had to be without blemish, a male from the herd or the flock for the larger offerings. The person bringing it laid his hand on its head. For the burnt offering, God said why.

"He is to lay his hand on the head of the burnt offering, so it can be accepted on his behalf to make atonement for him."

Then the offerer slaughtered it before the LORD. Aaron's sons the priests took the blood and splattered it on all sides of the altar at the entrance to the Tent of Meeting. The animal was skinned and cut into pieces. Its entrails and legs were washed with water. The priests arranged the pieces on the wood burning on the altar.

That pattern, hand on the head, slaughter, blood on the altar, burning on the wood, runs under almost everything that follows. The offerings differ in what is burned, who eats, and why a person came.

The burnt offering was given whole. A young bull from the herd, or a male sheep or goat from the flock, was burned on the altar, all of it, as a food offering, a pleasing aroma to the LORD. Nothing was held back for a meal. A man who had no herd animal could bring a bird instead, a turtledove or a young pigeon. The priest twisted off its head at the altar, drained its blood on the side of the altar, removed the crop and threw it on the east side, in the place for ashes, and burned the bird on the wood, torn open by its wings but not divided in two.

The grain offering came from the field and the kitchen rather than the herd. Fine flour with olive oil poured on it and frankincense put on it. Or cakes baked in an oven, unleavened, mixed with oil. Or bread made on a griddle, or cooked in a pan, crumbled with oil poured over it. Or, at firstfruits, crushed heads of new grain roasted on the fire. The priest took a handful of the flour and oil, with all the frankincense, and burned that handful on the altar as a memorial portion. The rest belonged to Aaron and his sons. The text calls it a most holy part of the food offerings to the LORD, and Aaron's family would eat it in a holy place.

Two rules governed every grain offering. No leaven and no honey were to be burned on the altar. And no grain offering went without salt.

"You must not leave the salt of the covenant of your God out of your grain offering."

The peace offering was built for a shared table. A man or woman could bring an animal from the herd or the flock, male or female, without blemish. The hand went on its head. It was slaughtered at the entrance to the Tent of Meeting, and its blood was splattered on all sides of the altar. Then only certain parts were burned: the fat that covered the entrails, both kidneys with their fat, and the lobe of the liver, burned on top of the burnt offering already on the wood.

"All the fat is the LORD's."

What remained became food. Later in the instructions the peace offering is named by its reasons: a sacrifice of thanksgiving, a vow, or a freewill offering. The meat of a thanksgiving offering had to be eaten the day it was offered, none of it left until morning. A vow or freewill offering could be eaten that day and the next. Anything left on the third day had to be burned, and eating it then would not be accepted. A person who was ceremonially unclean and ate the meat anyway was to be cut off from his people. From every peace offering, the breast was waved before the LORD and given to Aaron and his sons, and the right thigh was given to the priest who offered the blood and the fat. God called that their permanent portion from the people of Israel.

Then the offerings turned from gifts a person chose to bring to failures a person had to face.

The sin offering covered sins done unintentionally, when someone broke one of the LORD's commandments without meaning to and then became aware of what he had done. The offering changed with the person. If the anointed priest sinned, bringing guilt on the people, he brought a young bull without blemish. He laid his hand on its head at the entrance to the tent and slaughtered it before the LORD. This time the blood went further in. The priest dipped his finger in the blood and sprinkled some of it seven times before the LORD, in front of the veil of the sanctuary. He put blood on the horns of the altar of fragrant incense inside the tent, and poured out the rest at the base of the altar of burnt offering outside. The fat, kidneys, and lobe of the liver were burned on the altar. The rest of that bull, hide, flesh, head, legs, entrails, and dung, was carried outside the camp to a clean place where the ashes were poured out, and burned there on a wood fire.

If the whole congregation strayed without knowing it, the elders of the congregation laid their hands on a young bull and the same rite followed, and the text gives the result in a sentence that becomes a refrain in these chapters.

"In this way the priest will make atonement on their behalf, and they will be forgiven."

A leader who sinned unintentionally brought an unblemished male goat. One of the common people brought an unblemished female goat, or a lamb. In those cases the blood was put on the horns of the altar of burnt offering and the rest poured out at its base, the fat was burned as a pleasing aroma, and the refrain came again, shortened to one man's scale: the priest will make atonement for him, and he will be forgiven.

Some sins named in chapter 5 are easy to picture. A witness who heard a public charge and failed to testify. A person who touched something unclean, a dead animal or another unclean thing, and only later realized it. A person who swore thoughtlessly with his lips, to do something good or evil, and forgot the oath until it came back to him. Guilt did not wait for intent. When he realized it, he was guilty.

"If someone incurs guilt in one of these ways, he must confess the sin he has committed,"

His offering was a female lamb or goat from the flock. But the law had already thought about the man counting his animals and finding he had none to spare.

If he could not afford a lamb, he could bring two turtledoves or two young pigeons, one as a sin offering and one as a burnt offering. If he could not afford two birds, he could bring a tenth of an ephah of fine flour, with no oil and no frankincense on it, because it was a sin offering. The priest burned a handful of it on the altar, made atonement for him, and he was forgiven. The poorest man in the camp stood at the same altar as the priest with his bull, and the same sentence was spoken over his handful of flour.

The guilt offering dealt with wrongs that could be measured. A man who acted unfaithfully against the LORD's holy things without intending to brought an unblemished ram of proper value in silver shekels. He also had to make restitution for what he had harmed, adding a fifth of its value to it, and give it to the priest.

That fifth ran further. Chapter 6 opens with sins against a neighbor: a deposit entrusted to a man and then denied, something stolen, a neighbor extorted, lost property found and lied about, a false oath sworn over any of it. Once the man had sinned and became guilty, wishing it away was not an option written into the law.

"He must make restitution in full, add a fifth of the value, and pay it to the owner on the day he acknowledges his guilt."

Then he brought his guilt offering, the unblemished ram, to the priest, and the priest made atonement for him before the LORD. Forgiveness in these chapters did not float free of the neighbor who had been wronged. The money went back first, with more added to it, and then the ram came to the altar. And a man who sinned without knowing it was still guilty and bore his punishment until the ram was brought and atonement was made for the wrong he had committed in ignorance.

After five chapters spoken to the whole people, the last two chapters turn and speak to Aaron and his sons about their own work at the altar they would tend.

The burnt offering was to remain on the hearth of the altar all night, until morning, and the fire on the altar was to be kept burning. In the morning the priest, dressed in his linen robe and linen undergarments, removed the ashes the fire had consumed and placed them beside the altar. Then he changed his clothes and carried the ashes outside the camp to a clean place. Every morning he added wood to the fire, arranged the burnt offering on it, and burned the fat portions of the peace offerings on it.

"The fire shall be kept burning on the altar continually; it must not be extinguished."

The priests ate some offerings and never touched others, and the difference mattered. They ate the rest of the grain offering, without leaven, in the courtyard of the Tent of Meeting. Any male among the sons of Aaron could eat it. The sin offering was most holy. The priest who offered it ate it in a holy place, and anything that touched its flesh became holy. A clay pot it had been boiled in had to be broken; a bronze pot had to be scoured and rinsed with water. But a sin offering whose blood had been brought into the Tent of Meeting to make atonement in the Holy Place was not to be eaten at all. It had to be burned. The guilt offering followed the same law as the sin offering, and it belonged to the priest who made atonement with it. The hide of a burnt offering belonged to the priest who presented it. A priest's own grain offering was burned completely. It was not to be eaten.

The fat and the blood were barred to everyone, in every dwelling, as a permanent statute. A man who ate blood was to be cut off from his people.

That is where the instructions land, with their own table of contents spoken at the end.

"This is the law of the burnt offering, the grain offering, the sin offering, the guilt offering, the ordination offering, and the peace offering," which the LORD gave Moses on Mount Sinai, on the day He commanded the Israelites to present their offerings to the LORD in the Wilderness of Sinai.

The way in was now written down in full. A bull or a handful of flour. Blood on the altar and blood before the veil. A debt repaid with a fifth added before the ram was led in. A fire that was never to go out.

The laws kept naming Aaron and his sons, over and over, chapter after chapter, as the men who would take the blood, arrange the wood, eat in the holy place, and keep the fire. No one had washed them yet. No one had robed them, anointed them, or set them apart at the entrance of the tent in front of the people.

That came next, and the whole assembly was to be gathered to watch it.
