Ruth Episode 3: Under His Wings
Ruth 3 to 4, Berean Standard Bible

The harvests ended, and Naomi, who had spent the road home telling her daughters-in-law to find their future somewhere else, began to build one herself. "My daughter, should I not seek a resting place for you, that it may be well with you?" Resting place is the very thing she had prayed God would give Ruth in a new husband's house back in chapter 1. The blessing had not been answered in Moab or on the road. Naomi had decided, apparently, to become part of its answer, and her instrument was the fact she had filed away the night Ruth came home with an ephah of barley: Boaz was a kinsman-redeemer, and tonight he would be winnowing barley on the threshing floor.

Her instructions were precise and they were daring. "Therefore wash yourself, put on perfume, and wear your best clothes. Go down to the threshing floor, but do not let the man know you are there until he has finished eating and drinking. When he lies down, note the place where he lies. Then go in and uncover his feet, and lie down, and he will explain to you what you should do." Read it plainly: a young foreign widow was to go alone at night to where a wealthy older man slept after his harvest meal, and lie down at his feet, and trust everything to his character. By every custom of that world, Naomi was staking Ruth's reputation, the only asset Ruth had built in Bethlehem, on the proposition that Boaz was exactly what his introduction claimed, a man of noble character. Ruth's answer accepted the whole risk in six words' worth of obedience: "I will do everything you say," she answered.

She did it exactly. Boaz ate and drank and was in good spirits, the text says, using the same word for his heart's gladness that Naomi had used for Ruth's future welfare, and lay down at the end of the heap of grain to sleep beside his harvest, as owners did in threshing season to guard it. Ruth came secretly, uncovered his feet, and lay down. At midnight the cold on his feet or the presence beside them startled him awake, and he turned and found a woman lying there, and asked who she was. Her answer was not an apology and it was not small talk. "I am your servant Ruth," she replied. "Spread the corner of your garment over me, for you are a kinsman-redeemer."

In Hebrew, the corner of a garment is a kanaf, a wing. It is the same word Boaz himself had used in his prayer over her in the field, that she had come to take refuge under the wings of the God of Israel. Ruth had taken his blessing and turned it into a proposal, in the language of the law: spread your wing over me, marry me, redeem this family, because that office is yours. Everything Naomi's plan risked was now in the open, and everything depended on how this man answered a vulnerable woman in the dark, with no witnesses, when no one would have known.

He answered with a blessing, and with the book's great word for the third and last time: "May the LORD bless you, my daughter. You have shown more kindness now than before, because you have not run after the younger men, whether rich or poor." Kindness, hesed. Her first kindness, Boaz said, was the loyalty she had shown Naomi. This second one was greater: she had not gone shopping for a young husband, rich or poor, but had come to the family's redeemer to raise up the dead man's house. And then he gave her the compliment the narrator had been saving since his own introduction: "And now do not be afraid, my daughter. I will do for you whatever you request, since all my fellow townspeople know that you are a woman of noble character." A man of noble character, a woman of noble character. The book's two leads carry the same description in the same Hebrew words, and each of them applies it to the other.

Then Boaz did the thing that separates noble character from a romantic gesture. There was a redeemer nearer than he was, and the right was that man's first, and Boaz would not step around the law in the dark even though the law might cost him Ruth. He swore it with an oath: "if he wants to redeem you, good. Let him redeem you. But if he does not want to redeem you, as surely as the LORD lives, I will." She lay at his feet until morning and left before anyone could recognize her, to protect the name she had just been praised for, and he filled her shawl with six measures of barley and sent her home with a sentence Ruth carried back like a report: "Do not go back to your mother-in-law empty-handed." Empty. It was the word Naomi had hurled at heaven in the town square, I went away full and the LORD has brought me back empty, and whether or not Boaz had ever heard that speech, the book sets his sentence against hers and lets them rhyme. Naomi heard the barley hit the floor and the report of the night and delivered her verdict on Boaz, which is also the book's: "Wait, my daughter," said Naomi, "until you find out how things go, for he will not rest unless he has resolved the matter today."

He did not rest. That morning Boaz went up to the town gate, where Israel did its legal business, and sat down, and the nearer redeemer happened along, in this book's familiar way of happening, and Boaz seated him and ten elders of the city as witnesses. Then he conducted the negotiation like the careful man he was: land first. Naomi was selling the piece of land that had belonged to Elimelech, and the right to buy it back into the family was the nearer man's before it was Boaz's. "I will redeem it," he replied. Then Boaz laid the rest of it on the table, in public, where it belonged: "On the day you buy the land from Naomi and also from Ruth the Moabitess, you must also acquire the widow of the deceased in order to raise up the name of the deceased on his inheritance." The land came with a Moabite widow, and any son she bore would be counted the dead man's heir, inheriting the very land the redeemer had just paid for. The nearer man did his arithmetic in front of the elders and declined: "I cannot redeem it myself, or I would jeopardize my own inheritance. Take my right of redemption, because I cannot redeem it." He would not risk his own name to raise up another man's, and the book, which names shepherds and gleaners and towns-women, never gives him a name at all.

An old custom sealed it. The narrator pauses to explain it for readers of a later day, because it was already going out of use: "Now in former times in Israel, concerning the redemption or exchange of property, to make any matter legally binding a man would remove his sandal and give it to the other party, and this was a confirmation in Israel." The sandal came off and passed over, and then Boaz stood and made his declaration to the elders and everyone at the gate: "You are witnesses today that I am buying from Naomi all that belonged to Elimelech, Chilion, and Mahlon. Moreover, I have acquired Ruth the Moabitess, Mahlon’s widow, as my wife, to raise up the name of the deceased through his inheritance, so that his name will not disappear from among his brothers or from the gate of his home. You are witnesses today."

The town answered with a blessing, and listen to whose names Bethlehem reached for to bless a Moabite bride: "May the LORD make the woman entering your home like Rachel and Leah, who together built up the house of Israel. May you be prosperous in Ephrathah and famous in Bethlehem. And may your house become like the house of Perez, whom Tamar bore to Judah, because of the offspring the LORD will give you by this young woman." Rachel and Leah, the mothers of the nation. And Perez, the son of Tamar, the other widow in this family's history who had to secure the line of Judah by bold action when the men would not. The elders at the gate were blessing Ruth with the two most honored comparisons their family story owned, and one of them was a woman every bit as much an outsider to the line as Ruth herself.

"So Boaz took Ruth, and she became his wife. And when he had relations with her, the LORD enabled her to conceive, and she gave birth to a son." There is the book's second direct act of God, set like a clasp opposite the first. In chapter 1 He attended to His people by providing them with food. Here He enables a widow who had been ten years married without a child to conceive. Between those two acts, everything was hesed, people being kind at cost to themselves: a daughter-in-law who would not leave, a landowner who dropped grain on purpose, an old woman who schemed for someone else's future, a redeemer who would not cut a legal corner. The book seems determined to show heaven working almost entirely through that.

And the frame closes exactly where it opened, on Naomi. The women of Bethlehem, the same women who had asked can this be Naomi, now sang over her: "Blessed be the LORD, who has not left you this day without a kinsman-redeemer. May his name become famous in Israel. He will renew your life and sustain you in your old age. For your daughter-in-law, who loves you and is better to you than seven sons, has given him birth." Better than seven sons, the perfect family's number, and it is said of the Moabite. Naomi took the child onto her lap and became his nurse, and the neighbors named him, and what they said is the book in one sentence: "A son has been born to Naomi," they said, and they named him Obed, which means servant. The woman who came home empty holds the future in her lap, and the whole town assigns the baby to her account.

Then the book ends with ten names, and they are not filler, they are the destination. Perez, whom Tamar bore to Judah, fathered Hezron. Hezron fathered Ram, and Ram fathered Amminadab, and Amminadab fathered Nahshon, the same Nahshon who led the tribe of Judah in the wilderness generation, and Nahshon fathered Salmon, and Salmon fathered Boaz, and Boaz fathered Obed, and Obed fathered Jesse, and Jesse fathered David. David. The genealogy carries the family straight out of Egypt's generation into Bethlehem's greatest house, and it runs, openly and on the record, through a Moabite widow who clung to her mother-in-law on a road. Israel's law in Deuteronomy had said that no Moabite was to enter the assembly of the LORD, even to the tenth generation. Ruth does not argue with that text and does not explain it. It simply lets the names stand where they fall, three generations from Ruth to Israel's king, and closes.

So the judges era, which this series has watched spiral from Gideon to Gibeah, contains this too, in the same years and the same hills: a famine survived, a vow kept, a field gleaned, a gate honored, a baby on an old woman's lap. Everyone in the book does what is right, and almost none of it is in their own eyes. The next story in the order picks up at the tabernacle in Shiloh, where another woman is about to pour out a grief like Naomi's before a priest who does not understand her, and her name is Hannah, and she is praying for a son.
