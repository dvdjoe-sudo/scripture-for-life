Amos Episode 3: Seek Me and Live
Amos 5-6, Berean Standard Bible

Chapter five opens with something a prophet does only when the verdict is already written: Amos sings Israel's funeral lament while Israel is still alive to hear it. “Hear this word, O house of Israel, this lamentation I take up against you”, and the song itself is three lines long: “Fallen is Virgin Israel, never to rise again. She lies abandoned on her land, with no one to raise her up.” The nation in the song is a young woman dead in a field with no one to lift her, and the tense is past, a death reported, not predicted. The arithmetic of it follows: “The city that marches out a thousand strong will have but a hundred left”, a ninety percent loss stated as calmly as a census. That the lament is sung in advance is itself part of the mercy of the book: a nation hearing its own funeral dirge still has ears, and ears are what the next words are aimed at.

Because immediately after the dirge, the alternative is offered, and offered as a refrain. “Seek Me and live!” The command is then spelled out by negation, because Israel's whole religious map ran the other way: “Do not seek Bethel or go to Gilgal; do not journey to Beersheba, for Gilgal will surely go into exile, and Bethel will come to nothing.” The shrines were the seeking. Pilgrimage, sacrifice, and feast at the king's sanctuary felt like seeking God, and the prophet strips the equation bare: the places are under sentence themselves, and a man cannot seek shelter in a condemned building. “Seek the LORD and live, or He will sweep like fire through the house of Joseph”, fire with no one at Bethel to put it out. Between the commands stands the book's second doxology, because the God who must be sought is not the god of the shrines: “There are those who turn justice into wormwood and cast righteousness to the ground.” “He who made the Pleiades and Orion, who turns darkness into dawn and darkens day into night, who summons the waters of the sea and pours them over the face of the earth”, “the LORD is His name”, “He flashes destruction on the strong, so that fury comes upon the stronghold.” The maker of constellations and tides is the one being turned into wormwood justice by men who imagined Him housed.

The middle of the chapter names the mechanism, and it is the city gate, where Israel's courts sat and where the poor man's case went to die: “There are those who hate the one who reproves in the gate and despise him who speaks with integrity.” “For I know that your transgressions are many and your sins are numerous. You oppress the righteous by taking bribes; you deprive the poor of justice in the gate.” The consequence is measured out in the currency of the builders: because you trample the poor and tax his grain, “you will never live in the stone houses you have built; you will never drink the wine from the lush vineyards you have planted”. And the honest men have already learned the system's lesson: “the prudent keep silent in such times, for the days are evil”. Through it all the offer stands unrescinded, the refrain again in a new key: “Seek good, not evil, so that you may live. And the LORD, the God of Hosts, will be with you, as you have claimed.” “Hate evil and love good; establish justice in the gate. Perhaps the LORD, the God of Hosts, will be gracious to the remnant of Joseph.” Perhaps. The book of Amos does not deal in guaranteed outcomes; it deals in a door still open, with judgment on the threshold and grace stated as a possibility no one in the gate had earned.

Then the chapter turns on the nation's proudest expectation. Israel longed for the Day of the LORD, the day God would finally settle accounts with Israel's enemies, and Amos asks the book's most dangerous question: “Woe to you who long for the Day of the LORD! What will the Day of the LORD be for you? It will be darkness and not light.” The picture of it is a man escaping one death into the next: “It will be like a man who flees from a lion, only to encounter a bear, or who enters his house and rests his hand against the wall, only to be bitten by a snake.” Lion outside, bear behind, snake inside the house itself; there is no room in the sequence that is safe. And before the picture, the feasts come under the book's plainest rejection: “I hate, I despise your feasts! I cannot stand the stench of your solemn assemblies.” Offerings unaccepted, fattened cattle unregarded, and then worship's music itself dismissed: “Take away from Me the noise of your songs! I will not listen to the music of your harps.” In place of all of it, one sentence, the most quoted line Amos ever carried: “But let justice roll on like a river, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream.” Not a seasonal wadi that runs after rain and dries by summer, but a stream that never stops. God reaches back past the shrines to the wilderness to ask whether the sacrifices were ever the point: “Did you bring Me sacrifices and offerings forty years in the wilderness, O house of Israel?” What Israel had actually carried through those years, He says, were Sakkuth their king and Kaiwan their star god, idols of their own making, and so the sentence closes the chapter: “I will send you into exile beyond Damascus”, farther than the enemy capital the book began by judging.

Chapter six moves the camera from the gate to the couch. “Woe to those at ease in Zion and those secure on Mount Samaria”, the distinguished men of the foremost nation, so secure they “dismiss the day of calamity and bring near a reign of violence”. The portrait is drawn from the furniture out: “You lie on beds inlaid with ivory, and lounge upon your couches. You dine on lambs from the flock and calves from the stall.” They improvise songs on the harp like David, inventing instruments, anointing themselves with the finest oils, “You drink wine by the bowlful”, not by the cup. And in the center of the inventory, the one thing missing is named in a single line that carries the whole chapter: “you fail to grieve over the ruin of Joseph”. Joseph, the tribes, their own house, was ruining, and the men on the ivory beds had oil for their skin and wine by the bowl and not one unit of grief. The sentence matches the portrait: “you will now go into exile as the first of the captives, and your feasting and lounging will come to an end”, first in the column they refused to imagine. God swears by Himself against the pride of it: “I abhor Jacob’s pride and detest his citadels, so I will deliver up the city and everything in it.” What remains afterward is described in a whisper: in a house of ten, all die; the relative burning bodies asks if anyone is left inside, and the answer comes back with a warning attached, “Silence”, “for the name of the LORD must not be invoked”. Even prayer is hushed, for fear of what invoking the name might draw down. The book ends the movement with God's own rhetorical questions to a people who had inverted the world: “Do horses gallop on the cliffs? Does one plow the sea with oxen? But you have turned justice into poison and the fruit of righteousness into wormwood”. And against their boasting over two captured border towns, the last word is a nation raised from Lebo-hamath to the Brook of the Arabah, the whole length of the land Amos was sent to warn.
