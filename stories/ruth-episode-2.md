Ruth Episode 2: As It Happened
Ruth 2, Berean Standard Bible

Before Ruth takes a single step, the narrator introduces a man she has not met: "Now Naomi had a relative on her husband’s side, a prominent man of noble character from the clan of Elimelech, whose name was Boaz." Hold on to that description, a man of noble character. The book hands out that exact compliment twice, and the second time it will be Boaz saying it about Ruth. For now he is just a name in Elimelech's clan, a wealthy landowner in Bethlehem, and Ruth does not know he exists.

What Ruth knows is that two widows cannot eat a reputation, so the initiative in this chapter is entirely hers. "Please let me go into the fields and glean heads of grain after someone in whose sight I may find favor." Gleaning was Israel's built-in mercy: the law Moses gave commanded harvesters to leave the edges of their fields unharvested and the dropped stalks where they fell, for the poor, for the foreigner, and for the widow. Ruth was all three at once. But the law gave gleaners a claim, not a welcome, and a lone foreign woman still needed a field owner in whose sight she found favor. Naomi's whole answer was permission and a blessing in four words: "Go ahead, my daughter," she replied.

"So Ruth departed and went out into the field and gleaned after the harvesters. And she happened to come to the part of the field belonging to Boaz, who was from the clan of Elimelech." She happened to come. The Hebrew piles chance on chance, her chance chanced upon his field, and the narrator lets the coincidence sit there smiling, in a book where God has acted directly exactly once and the characters pray constantly. Ruth chose a field at random, or thought she did, and it belonged to the one man in Bethlehem who was kin to her dead husband, wealthy enough to help, and, the day was about to show, the kind of man who noticed a stranger's daughter-in-law and asked questions about her.

Boaz came down from Bethlehem to his harvest, and the first words out of his mouth introduce him better than the narrator did: he greeted his harvesters: "The LORD be with you." And they answered in kind: "The LORD bless you," they replied. Then he saw the new gleaner and asked his foreman: "Whose young woman is this?" The foreman's answer identified her not by her name but by her story: "She is the Moabitess who returned with Naomi from the land of Moab." And he repeated her request from the morning, because it had stayed with him: "Please let me glean and gather among the sheaves after the harvesters." She had come at morning and worked straight through, he said, resting only a short time in the shelter. In a harvest field, in front of the owner, the foreman of Bethlehem testified to two things about the foreign widow: what she had done for Naomi, and how hard she worked.

Boaz went to her himself, and what he gave her was safety first and bread second. "Listen, my daughter. Do not go and glean in another field, and do not go away from this place, but stay here close to my servant girls." Stay with the women, he meant, and stay in my field, and the reason stands inside his next sentence: "Indeed, I have ordered the young men not to touch you. And when you are thirsty, go and drink from the jars the young men have filled." A foreign widow gleaning alone in strange fields was exposed in ways the chapter does not need to spell out, because Naomi will spell them out tonight. Boaz removed the danger by command, gave her the run of the field, and put her on his own water jars like one of his workers.

Ruth went down on her face to the ground, and her question is the honest one underneath this whole book: "Why have I found such favor in your eyes that you should take notice of me, even though I am a foreigner?" Boaz's answer tells her that her story arrived in Bethlehem before she did, and he tells it back to her in vocabulary any Israelite would recognize: "I have been made fully aware of all you have done for your mother-in-law since the death of your husband, how you left your father and mother and the land of your birth, and how you came to a people you did not know before." Left your father and your mother and the land of your birth. That is the shape of Abraham's call, Genesis 12, and Boaz uses it for a Moabite gleaner without blinking. Then he prayed over her, and his prayer is the seed of everything still coming: "May the LORD repay your work, and may you receive a rich reward from the LORD, the God of Israel, under whose wings you have taken refuge."

Ruth answered a woman who knew her place and planned to keep her dignity inside it: "My lord," she said, "may I continue to find favor in your eyes, for you have comforted and spoken kindly to your maidservant, though I am not like one of your servant girls." At mealtime he called her over, not to the gleaners' corner but beside the harvesters, bread and vinegar sauce and roasted grain pressed on her by the owner himself, and "she ate and was satisfied and had some left over." Satisfied is a word Bethlehem had not heard from this household since the famine. She saved the leftovers for Naomi without being told.

Then Boaz did the thing that separates generosity from manners. As she rose to glean again, he ordered his young men, out of her hearing: "Even if she gathers among the sheaves, do not insult her. Rather, pull out for her some stalks from the bundles and leave them for her to gather. Do not rebuke her." Gleaning was supposed to be scraps and leftovers, the economics of crumbs. Boaz quietly rewired it so his own harvesters were harvesting for her, dropping deliberate handfuls into her path and pretending it was accident. She gleaned until evening, beat out the grain, and carried home about an ephah of barley, a load no gleaner gathers in a day without help, plus the saved roasted grain from lunch.

Naomi saw the heap and did the math instantly. "Where did you glean today, and where did you work? Blessed be the man who noticed you." Ruth told her the name, and the older woman who had renamed herself Bitter three days before erupted in the book's second sounding of its great word: "May he be blessed by the LORD, who has not withdrawn His kindness from the living or the dead." Kindness is hesed again, the loving devotion Naomi had prayed over her daughters-in-law on the road, now appearing in a field owner's conduct. And then Naomi said the sentence that turns this chapter from a good day into a plot: "The man is a close relative. He is one of our kinsman-redeemers." A kinsman-redeemer, a goel, was the family guardian in Israel's law, the relative charged with buying back a family's land before it was lost and rescuing kin who had fallen into ruin. Naomi had buried a husband and two sons and renamed herself for the bitterness of it, and she had just heard that one of the men obligated to redeem her family had fed her daughter-in-law from his own table.

Ruth stayed in Boaz's fields through the rest of the barley harvest and the wheat harvest after it, weeks of steady provision, working alongside his servant girls as Naomi advised: "My daughter, it is good for you to work with his young women, so that nothing will happen to you in another field." Nothing dramatic happened in those weeks, and the book does not rush past them. A widow was fed, a foreigner was safe, and an old woman at home was doing arithmetic again, but a different kind than on the road to Bethlehem. Naomi had a redeemer in view now, and a daughter-in-law of noble character, and the harvest was ending.
