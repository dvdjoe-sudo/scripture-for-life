Judges Episode 3: Deborah and the Song of Victory
Judges 4 to 5, Berean Standard Bible

"After Ehud died, the Israelites again did evil in the sight of the LORD. So the LORD sold them into the hand of Jabin king of Canaan, who reigned in Hazor." Hazor. Joshua had burned that city to the ground and beheaded its king, and a generation later another Jabin reigned there over Israel, because a burned city can be rebuilt by the enemy while the victors are busy forgetting. Jabin's commander was Sisera, based at Harosheth-hagoyim, and the instrument of the oppression was the one Israel had never solved: "Jabin had nine hundred chariots of iron, and he had harshly oppressed the Israelites for twenty years." Twenty years of iron. Then the cry.

The deliverer this time was already at work before there was a war to fight. "Now Deborah, a prophetess, the wife of Lappidoth, was judging Israel at that time. And she would sit under the Palm of Deborah between Ramah and Bethel in the hill country of Ephraim, where the Israelites would go up to her for judgment." Of all the judges in this book, Deborah is the only one shown doing what the title says, settling the nation's disputes in the open air, day after day, before she ever calls an army. She is prophetess and judge at once, and the war begins with her summons.

She sent for Barak son of Abinoam, from Kedesh in Naphtali, up in the north where the chariots were, and delivered God's order in God's voice: "Surely the LORD, the God of Israel, is commanding you:" "Go and march to Mount Tabor, taking with you ten thousand men of Naphtali and Zebulun. And I will draw out Sisera the commander of Jabin's army, his chariots, and his troops to the River Kishon, and I will deliver him into your hand."

Barak's answer has been debated ever since, and the text gives it without comment: "If you will go with me, I will go; but if you will not go with me, I will not go." Read it as faith that wants God's prophet present, or as courage that needs borrowing; the text lets both stand and only records the price. "I will certainly go with you," Deborah replied, "but the road you are taking will bring you no honor, because the LORD will be selling Sisera into the hand of a woman." Selling, the cycle's own word for what God does to Israel, turned around on the oppressor. Barak went. So did Deborah. Ten thousand men of Zebulun and Naphtali gathered at Kedesh and went up Mount Tabor, a round mountain standing alone over the Jezreel plain, with Deborah among them.

The narrator plants one tent in the ground before the battle and expects the reader to remember it: "Now Heber the Kenite had moved away from the Kenites, the descendants of Hobab the father-in-law of Moses, and had pitched his tent by the great tree of Zaanannim, which was near Kedesh." A Kenite, kin to Moses by marriage, camped apart, at peace with Jabin. Remember the tent.

Sisera heard Barak was on Tabor and summoned everything: "he summoned all nine hundred of his iron chariots and all the men with him, from Harosheth-hagoyim to the River Kishon." Nine hundred chariots need flat, dry ground, and the Kishon valley in the dry season was a parade ground. Then Deborah gave the word that started the battle, and its confidence is total: "Arise, for this is the day that the LORD has delivered Sisera into your hand. Has not the LORD gone before you?" Barak charged downhill with ten thousand men at his back, and the prose gives the rout one agent: "And in front of him the LORD routed with the sword Sisera, all his charioteers, and all his army. Sisera abandoned his chariot and fled on foot." The commander of nine hundred chariots left his chariot in the mud and ran. Barak pursued the army all the way back to Harosheth-hagoyim, "and the whole army of Sisera fell by the sword; not a single man was left."

All but one. Sisera ran on foot to the one address in the north where he expected safety, the tent of Jael, wife of Heber the Kenite, "because there was peace between Jabin king of Hazor and the house of Heber the Kenite." Jael came out to meet him with the words of hospitality: "Come in, my lord. Come in with me. Do not be afraid." She covered him with a blanket. He asked for water; "she opened a container of milk, gave him a drink, and covered him again," milk to make a tired man sleep. He set his own guard instructions before he slept: "Stand at the entrance to the tent," he said, and if anyone came asking whether a man was there, she was to say no.

The text tells what she did instead with the plainest verbs it owns. "But as he lay sleeping from exhaustion, Heber's wife Jael took a tent peg, grabbed a hammer, and went silently to Sisera. She drove the peg through his temple and into the ground, and he died." A tent peg and a workman's hammer were a tent-dwelling woman's everyday tools; pitching and striking tents was her trade. The general who had oppressed Israel for twenty years died in his sleep, in a woman's tent, killed with the tools of the house that sheltered him, outside the battle entirely, exactly where Deborah's sentence had put the honor: in the hand of a woman, and not the woman anyone expected.

Barak arrived in pursuit, and Jael went out to greet him with the day's second invitation: "Come, and I will show you the man you are seeking." He went in, "and there lay Sisera dead, with a tent peg through his temple." The chapter closes on the king, not the commander: "On that day God subdued Jabin king of Canaan before the Israelites. And the hand of the Israelites grew stronger and stronger against Jabin king of Canaan until they destroyed him."

Then Israel sang about it, and chapter 5 is the song, Deborah and Barak together, the book's own commentary on its battle. The song reaches further back than the prose does, to God marching from Seir and Sinai while "the earth trembled, the heavens poured out rain, and the clouds poured down water." That rain is the song's explanation of the rout: the heavens opened over the Kishon, and iron chariots drown in mud. It remembers the bad years before Deborah in lines that measure what oppression did to ordinary life: "In the days of Shamgar son of Anath, in the days of Jael, the highways were deserted and the travelers took the byways. Life in the villages ceased; it ended in Israel, until I, Deborah, arose, a mother in Israel." And it names the cause the prose only implied: "When they chose new gods, then war came to their gates. Not a shield or spear was found among forty thousand in Israel."

The song does the muster roll the prose skipped, tribe by tribe, and it is not gentle with everyone. Ephraim came down, Benjamin, the commanders of Machir, the staff-bearers of Zebulun, and Issachar "rushing into the valley at his heels." But Reuben gets the same line twice, an indictment in refrain: "In the clans of Reuben there was great indecision," with its men sitting among the sheepfolds listening to the whistling for the flocks. "Gilead remained beyond the Jordan. Dan, why did you linger by the ships? Asher stayed at the coast and remained in his harbors." And one town is cursed by the angel of the LORD outright: "Curse Meroz," says the angel of the LORD. "Bitterly curse her inhabitants; for they did not come to help the LORD, to help the LORD against the mighty." Against them all the song sets the two tribes that bled: "Zebulun was a people who risked their lives; Naphtali, too, on the heights of the battlefield."

The battle itself the song hands to heaven: "From the heavens the stars fought; from their courses they fought against Sisera. The River Kishon swept them away, the ancient river, the River Kishon." Then it turns to Jael and gives her the song's highest title: "Most blessed among women is Jael, the wife of Heber the Kenite, most blessed of tent-dwelling women." "He asked for water, and she gave him milk." And it sings the killing three times over in falling lines, ending flat: "where he collapsed, there he fell dead."

The song's last scene is its coldest. It sets a window in Harosheth or Hazor and a mother looking through the lattice, asking the question the whole song has already answered: "Why is his chariot so long in coming? What has delayed the clatter of his chariots?" Her ladies comfort her with the arithmetic of plunder, imagining the army dividing spoil, "a girl or two for each warrior," dyed and embroidered cloth for Sisera's neck. The reader knows what the window does not: the chariot is empty in the mud, the general is dead in a tent, and the woman who killed him has been pronounced most blessed among women by the nation he oppressed. The song does not pity the mother or mock her. It lets her keep looking out the window, and ends: "So may all Your enemies perish, O LORD! But may those who love You shine like the sun at its brightest." And the land had rest for forty years.

Prose told what happened; the song told what it meant, and the two accounts keep their own details, Meroz and the muster roll on one side, the blanket and the milk on the other. Neither is corrected by the other. That is how this book remembers: a battle and a song about the battle, standing side by side, both true in the way they were given. The rest will last forty years. When it ends, the oppressor will not be a king with chariots but a swarm out of the desert that eats Israel's harvest year after year, and the deliverer will be found hiding from it, threshing wheat in a winepress.
