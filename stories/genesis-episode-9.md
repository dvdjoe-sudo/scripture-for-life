Genesis Episode 9: A Family Breaking Apart
Genesis 34 to 38, Berean Standard Bible

Dinah went out, and what happened to her split her brothers open.

Dinah was the daughter Leah had borne to Jacob. She went out to see the women of the land. Shechem, the son of Hamor the Hivite and a prince of the land, saw her. He took her and lay with her by force. His soul was drawn to Dinah, and he loved her and spoke tenderly to her after he had violated her. Shechem told his father Hamor to get the girl for him as a wife.

When Jacob heard that Shechem had defiled his daughter Dinah, his sons were in the field with his livestock. Jacob kept silent until they came home.

Hamor went out to speak with Jacob. At the same time Jacob's sons came in from the field. When they heard what had happened, the men were grieved and furious, "because Shechem had committed an outrage in Israel by lying with Jacob's daughter, a thing that should not be done."

Hamor asked for Dinah as a wife for Shechem and offered open doors between their peoples. Their daughters could marry each other's sons. They could live in the land together, trade in it, and acquire property. Shechem added his own plea. He would give whatever bride price and gift they asked, if only they would give him the girl to be his wife.

Jacob's sons answered Shechem and Hamor with deceit because Shechem had defiled Dinah. They said they could not give their sister to a man who was uncircumcised. The men of the city could become one people with them if every male among them was circumcised as Jacob's household was. Hamor and Shechem carried the proposal to the men at the city gate. They framed it as peace and profit: the people's land, livestock, and property could belong to Shechem's city if the men agreed. Every male was circumcised.

On the third day, while the men were still in pain, Dinah's brothers Simeon and Levi took their swords, came against the unsuspecting city, and killed every male. They killed Hamor and Shechem with the sword, took Dinah from Shechem's house, and departed. The other sons of Jacob came upon the dead and plundered the city because their sister had been defiled. They took flocks, herds, donkeys, wealth, children, wives, and everything in the houses and fields.

Jacob said to Simeon and Levi that they had brought trouble on him by making him a stench to the inhabitants of the land, the Canaanites and the Perizzites. Jacob's household was small in number. If the peoples gathered against him, they could destroy him and his household. Simeon and Levi answered with the wound the story would not put down. Should Shechem have treated their sister like a prostitute?

Then God told Jacob to arise, go up to Bethel, and live there. Jacob was to make an altar to the God who had appeared to him when he fled from Esau. Jacob ordered his household and all who were with him to put away the foreign gods among them, purify themselves, and change their garments. They gave Jacob all the foreign gods in their possession and the rings in their ears, and Jacob buried them under the oak near Shechem.

As they journeyed, the terror of God fell on the cities around them, and no one pursued Jacob's sons. Jacob came to Luz, which is Bethel, in the land of Canaan. He built an altar and called the place El Bethel, because there God had revealed Himself to Jacob when he fled from his brother. Deborah, Rebekah's nurse, died and was buried under the oak below Bethel. It was named Allon-bachuth, Oak of Weeping.

God appeared to Jacob again after he came from Paddan-aram and blessed him. God repeated the name change: Jacob would no longer be called Jacob, but Israel would be his name. God said, "I am God Almighty. Be fruitful and multiply." He promised that nations and kings would come from Jacob, and He gave Jacob the land He had given to Abraham and Isaac. Jacob set up a stone pillar where God had spoken with him and poured out a drink offering and oil on it. He called the place Bethel.

They journeyed from Bethel, and while they were still some distance from Ephrath, Rachel went into labor. Her labor was hard. The midwife told her not to fear, because she would have another son. As Rachel's soul was departing, for she was dying, she named the boy Ben-oni, Son of My Sorrow. His father called him Benjamin. Rachel died and was buried on the way to Ephrath, which is Bethlehem. Jacob set a pillar over her tomb.

While Israel lived in that region, Reuben went and lay with Bilhah, his father's concubine, and Israel heard of it.

Genesis then names Jacob's twelve sons. Leah bore Reuben, Simeon, Levi, Judah, Issachar, and Zebulun. Rachel bore Joseph and Benjamin. Bilhah, Rachel's servant, bore Dan and Naphtali. Zilpah, Leah's servant, bore Gad and Asher. Jacob came to Isaac at Mamre, at Kiriath-arba, which is Hebron, where Abraham and Isaac had lived as foreigners. Isaac lived 180 years. He breathed his last and died, old and full of days, and Esau and Jacob buried him.

Esau's family receives its own record. Esau had taken wives from Canaan, and he had sons who became chiefs in Edom. He moved from the land of Canaan because the land could not support both his household and Jacob's household with all their livestock. Esau settled in the hill country of Seir. Genesis names his descendants, the chiefs of the sons of Esau, and kings who reigned in Edom before any king reigned over Israel. Jacob's twin had become a nation with its own rulers.

Joseph enters the next movement at seventeen years old.

Joseph was tending the flock with the sons of Bilhah and Zilpah, his father's wives, and he brought a bad report about them to their father. Israel loved Joseph more than any of his other sons because he had been born to him in his old age. Israel made Joseph a robe of many colors. "When Joseph's brothers saw that their father loved him more than any of them, they hated him and could not speak a kind word to him."

Joseph had a dream. In the dream, he and his brothers were binding sheaves in the field. Joseph's sheaf rose and stood upright, and his brothers' sheaves gathered around it and bowed down. His brothers asked whether he intended to reign over them or rule over them, and they hated him even more. Then Joseph dreamed another dream. The sun, the moon, and eleven stars were bowing down to him. He told his father and his brothers. Israel rebuked him and asked whether he, Joseph's mother, and Joseph's brothers would actually come and bow to the ground before Joseph. His brothers were jealous of him, "but his father kept in mind what he had said."

Israel sent Joseph from the Valley of Hebron to see if all was well with his brothers and the flock at Shechem. Joseph found a man wandering in the field near Shechem, and the man told him the brothers had moved on to Dothan. Joseph went after them.

His brothers saw him in the distance, and before he reached them they plotted to kill him. They spoke of throwing him into a pit and blaming a fierce animal. Reuben heard the plan and tried to rescue Joseph. He told them not to shed blood, but to throw Joseph into a pit in the wilderness and not lay a hand on him. Reuben meant to return later, pull Joseph out, and bring him back to their father.

When Joseph came to his brothers, they stripped him of the robe Israel had made for him and threw him into the pit. The pit was empty. There was no water in it. As they sat down to eat, they saw a caravan of Ishmaelites coming from Gilead with spices, balm, and myrrh, on their way to Egypt. Judah asked what would be gained by killing their brother and concealing his blood. He proposed selling Joseph to the Ishmaelites instead of laying a hand on him, because Joseph was their brother and their own flesh.

"So when the Midianite traders passed by, his brothers pulled Joseph out of the pit and sold him for twenty shekels of silver to the Ishmaelites, who took him to Egypt."

Reuben returned to the pit and found Joseph gone. He tore his clothes and demanded where he could go now. The brothers took Joseph's robe, slaughtered a goat, and dipped the robe in the blood. They sent it to their father and asked him to identify whether it was his son's robe or not. Jacob recognized it. He said a fierce animal had devoured Joseph, that Joseph had surely been torn to pieces. Jacob tore his garments, put sackcloth around his waist, and mourned for his son many days.

"All his sons and daughters tried to comfort him, but he refused to be comforted. 'No,' he said. 'I will go down to Sheol mourning for my son.' So his father wept for him."

Meanwhile the Midianites sold Joseph in Egypt to Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh and the captain of the guard.

Around that time, Judah went down from his brothers and stayed with a man named Hirah the Adullamite. There Judah married the daughter of a Canaanite man named Shua. She bore three sons: Er, Onan, and Shelah. Judah obtained a wife for Er, his firstborn. Her name was Tamar. Er was wicked in the sight of the LORD, and the LORD put him to death.

Judah told Onan to go in to his brother's wife and raise up offspring for his brother. Onan knew the offspring would not be his, so whenever he went in to Tamar he wasted his seed on the ground so he would not give offspring to his brother. What he did was wicked in the sight of the LORD, and the LORD put him to death too. Judah told Tamar to live as a widow in her father's house until Shelah grew up. He said that in words, but he feared Shelah might die like his brothers. He did not give Shelah to Tamar.

After a long time, Judah's wife died. When Judah had finished mourning, he went up to Timnah with Hirah to his sheepshearers. Tamar was told that her father-in-law was going up to Timnah. She took off her widow's garments, covered herself with a veil, wrapped herself, and sat at the entrance to Enaim, on the road to Timnah. She had seen that Shelah had grown up, and she had not been given to him as a wife.

Judah saw her and thought she was a prostitute because she had covered her face. He turned aside to her by the road and asked to come in to her. He did not know she was his daughter-in-law. Tamar asked what he would give her. Judah promised a young goat from his flock. Tamar asked for a pledge until he sent it: his signet, his cord, and the staff in his hand. Judah gave them to her and went in to her, and she conceived by him. Then Tamar arose and went away. She laid aside her veil and put her widow's garments back on.

Judah sent the young goat by Hirah to recover the pledge, but Hirah could not find the woman. Men of the place said there had been no prostitute there. Judah let the matter rest so he would not become a laughingstock. About three months later, Judah was told that Tamar had prostituted herself and was pregnant. Judah said she should be brought out and burned.

As she was being brought out, Tamar sent a message to her father-in-law. She said she was pregnant by the man to whom the signet, cord, and staff belonged, and asked Judah to identify them. Judah recognized the items and said, "She is more righteous than I, since I did not give her to my son Shelah." And he did not have relations with her again.

When the time came for Tamar to give birth, there were twins in her womb. During labor one child put out a hand. The midwife tied a scarlet thread around his hand, saying this one came out first. But the hand drew back, and his brother came out. She said a breach had been made, and he was named Perez. Afterward his brother came out with the scarlet thread on his hand, and he was named Zerah.

The household of Israel was in pieces. Dinah had been violated and avenged with a sword. Rachel was buried on the road. Reuben had shamed his father. Esau's nation stood apart. Jacob believed Joseph was dead. Judah's own house carried twins born from the pledge he had left beside a road.

And Joseph, the son Jacob mourned as dead, was alive in Egypt in the house of Potiphar.
