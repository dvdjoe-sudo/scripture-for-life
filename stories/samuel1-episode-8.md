1 Samuel Episode 8: The Fugitive
1 Samuel 21 to 24, Berean Standard Bible

David's first stop as a fugitive was a sanctuary, and he arrived lying. At Nob, the priest Ahimelech met him trembling, because royal servants did not travel alone, and David explained that he was on a secret mission for the king, that his men were waiting at an appointed place, and that he needed bread. There was no mission. There were no men. The story got him what he needed anyway: the only bread in the house, the consecrated loaves, the Bread of the Presence, given to a hungry fugitive by a priest who bent the rules of holiness toward mercy. It also got him a sword, and the sword was an old friend. Behind the ephod, wrapped in a cloth, Nob kept the trophy of the Valley of Elah, and the priest offered it and David said the obvious: "There is none like it; give it to me." One man watched the whole transaction and remembered it, a herdsman detained at the sanctuary that day: Doeg the Edomite, Saul's chief shepherd. David saw him there. File it the way David did, with a weight that will matter: he noticed Doeg, and went on eating.

From Nob he ran to the last address anyone would choose, Gath, Goliath's hometown, carrying Goliath's sword, and Achish's courtiers recognized him by the song: was this not David, the king of the land, the tens of thousands man? David, who had faced the giant without blinking, came apart at being recognized, and saved himself by an act. He feigned madness before the court, scratching at the gate doors, letting spittle run down his beard, until Achish drove him out with contempt: "Am I in need of madmen, that you have brought this man to rave in my presence? Must this man come into my house?" Israel sings two psalms out of that humiliation to this day: Psalm 56, whose heading names David's seizure by the Philistines in Gath, and Psalm 34, whose heading ties his feigned madness and escape to the song of a man who blessed the LORD at all times for it. The book does not quote them; it just leaves the drooling fugitive at the gate, and lets the Psalter tell you what he was praying.

At the cave of Adullam the fugitive became a captain. His brothers and his father's whole household came down to him, and then came everybody with nowhere else to go: "And all who were distressed or indebted or discontented rallied around him, and he became their leader. About four hundred men were with him." The cave filled with the casualties of Saul's kingdom, and David's first royal act was elder care. He took his parents across to Moab, to the king of his great-grandmother Ruth's people, and asked shelter for them "until I learn what God will do for me". Then the prophet Gad told him not to stay in the stronghold but to go back into Judah, into the danger, and David obeyed. Note what the wilderness court was accumulating while the palace emptied: a prophet to guide him, and presently a priest with the ephod to inquire for him. Saul, who had lost Samuel, was about to ensure he had no priests either.

At Gibeah, the king sat under his tamarisk tree with his spear in his hand and harangued his own tribe like a man reading his fears aloud. "Is the son of Jesse giving all of you fields and vineyards and making you commanders of thousands or hundreds?" It was Samuel's warning turned inside out: the king who took everyone's fields accused the fugitive of promising fields. You have all conspired against me, Saul said; not one of you told me my own son made a covenant with him. And into the silence of the Benjaminites stepped the one man at court with no Israelite loyalties to lose. Doeg the Edomite reported Nob: "I saw the son of Jesse come to Ahimelech son of Ahitub at Nob." He inquired of God for him, gave him provisions, gave him Goliath's sword. Ahimelech was summoned with his whole father's house, and his defense, delivered to a king holding a spear, is a model of a clear conscience: "Who among all your servants is as faithful as David, the king’s son-in-law, the captain of your bodyguard and honored in your house?" I inquired for him as I had always done; I knew nothing of any affair. Saul sentenced them anyway: "You will surely die, Ahimelech, you and all your father’s house!" And here the court split along the line of the whole book. The king's Israelite guards, ordered to kill the priests of the LORD, refused outright; they would not lift a hand. So Saul turned to the Edomite: "You turn and strike down the priests!" Doeg did. Eighty-five men who wore the linen ephod died that day, and Doeg went on to Nob and put the city to the sword, men and women, children and infants, oxen, donkeys, and sheep: the full catalogue of destruction, word for word the ban Saul had refused to complete against Amalek, executed instead against a town of priests inside Israel. Psalm 52 carries Doeg's name in its heading forever, David's verdict on the informer set to music.

One priest survived. Abiathar, Ahimelech's son, escaped and ran to David, and David took the guilt on himself in words nobody made him say: "I knew that Doeg the Edomite was there that day, and that he was sure to tell Saul. I myself am responsible for the lives of everyone in your father’s house." Stay with me, he said; the man seeking your life is seeking mine; "You will be safe with me". It is the covenant instinct of David in one scene, absorbing blame and offering shelter, and from that day the fugitive's camp had what Saul's palace lacked, a priest of the LORD with the ephod of inquiry, because Saul had murdered the rest.

The inquiries saved more than David. When the Philistines raided Keilah, a walled town of Judah, David asked the LORD twice, over his own men's fear, and went and saved the city whose king-protector should have saved it. Saul heard David was shut inside walls and read the trap as God's gift: "God has delivered him into my hand, for he has trapped himself by entering a town with gates and bars." David asked the ephod two questions, and the answers describe his whole fugitive life in advance. Will Saul come down? He will. Will the citizens of Keilah, the people David had just rescued, surrender him to Saul? They will. Gratitude had a shelf life of days; David slipped out before the siege, six hundred men now, and lived place to place in the wilderness of Ziph, where the text posts its quiet verdict across the whole era: "Day after day Saul searched for him, but God would not deliver David into his hand."

In that wilderness the covenant came to him one more time in person. Jonathan slipped out of his father's court to Horesh and strengthened David's hand in God, and the prince spoke the succession aloud, from the far side of surrender: "Do not be afraid, for my father Saul will never lay a hand on you. And you will be king over Israel, and I will be your second-in-command. Even my father Saul knows this is true." They renewed the covenant, and Jonathan went home. The Ziphites, David's own Judeans, then did what Keilah had only been predicted to do: they went up to Gibeah and sold his position to Saul, and Saul blessed them for it in the LORD's name: "May you be blessed by the LORD," replied Saul, "for you have had compassion on me." Psalm 54's heading remembers the Ziphites' errand. Twice Saul's net closed on the mountain in Maon, his men on one slope, David's on the other, the trap an hour from springing, and twice or once was enough: a messenger galloped in with news of a Philistine raid, and Saul had to break off and go defend the kingdom he was neglecting to chase the man he feared. The place got a name out of it, the Rock of Escape.

Then, at En-gedi, the hunt delivered Saul himself into David's hand, helpless and alone. The king came into a cave to relieve himself, the very cave in whose recesses David and his men were hiding, and David's men read it as heaven's signature on regicide, quoting what they took for a prophecy: "Behold, I will deliver your enemy into your hand, that you may do with him as you wish." David crept forward through the dark with a knife, close enough to kill the king of Israel unhurried, and cut off a corner of his robe instead. Even that much violence against the anointed man's clothing stricken his conscience afterward, and he held his men back with both hands and a doctrine: "The LORD forbid that I should do such a thing to my master, the LORD’s anointed. May I never lift my hand against him, since he is the LORD’s anointed." Saul had been anointed by Samuel; the office was God's, and David would not take the kingdom out of God's timing with a blade, not even in self-defense, not even with opportunity served up in a cave.

From the cave mouth he called after the departing king, bowed facedown, and made his case with the evidence in his hand: "See, my father, look at the corner of your robe in my hand. For I cut it off, but I did not kill you." And he put the feud in the court where he had decided it belonged: "May the LORD judge between you and me, and may the LORD take vengeance on you, but my hand will never be against you." Against whom has the king of Israel come out, he asked; you are pursuing a dead dog, a flea. And Saul, confronted with mercy at point-blank range, broke, and wept aloud, and said the truest things he ever said about the two of them: "You are more righteous than I, for you have rewarded me with good, though I have rewarded you with evil." And then the hunter prophesied against his own hunt: "Now I know for sure that you will be king and that the kingdom of Israel will be established in your hands." He asked one thing, the thing Jonathan had asked, that David swear not to cut off his descendants or wipe out his name when he came to power, and David swore it freely. Then Saul went home. And David, who had just heard his enemy confess his destiny and weep over him, did not go home with him, and did not trust the tears past the horizon. He went back up to the stronghold. Both men, for once, understood each other perfectly.
