1 Samuel Episode 2: The Ark Taken, and Ebenezer
1 Samuel 4 to 7, Berean Standard Bible

The sign God gave Eli's house had a date waiting for it, and the Philistines set it. Israel marched out to fight them and camped at a place called, of all things, Ebenezer, and lost four thousand men in the first battle. The elders' diagnosis of the defeat was theological, and it was wrong in the way that religious confidence can be wrong. "Why has the LORD brought defeat on us before the Philistines today? Let us bring the ark of the covenant of the LORD from Shiloh, so that it may go with us to save us from the hand of our enemies." The ark was the throne-footstool of God, the place between the cherubim where He had promised to meet Moses. The elders treated it as a weapon to be carried onto the field, a guarantee that could be hauled out of the sanctuary and pointed at the enemy, and they sent for it, and Hophni and Phinehas, the two priests whose corruption had brought the sentence down, came with it into the camp. Israel shouted so loudly the ground shook.

The shout frightened the Philistines half to death. They knew the old stories, Egypt and the plagues, and they said the gods had come into the Hebrew camp. And then, in one of the most honest moments an enemy gets in this book, fear turned into resolve: "Take courage and be men, O Philistines! Otherwise, you will serve the Hebrews just as they served you. Now be men and fight!" They fought like men who believed surrender was slavery, and Israel, with the ark on the field and the shout still in the air, was routed. Thirty thousand foot soldiers fell. "The ark of God was captured, and Eli’s two sons, Hophni and Phinehas, died." Both brothers, on the same day, exactly as the man of God had said. The ark had not saved Israel. The ark had been taken, because the God of the ark was not a talisman to be carried by corrupt priests into a battle He had not called.

A runner from Benjamin tore through to Shiloh with his clothes ripped and dirt on his head, and found Eli sitting by the road, watching, blind, ninety-eight years old, his heart trembling, the text says, not for his sons but for the ark of God. The report came in stages and each stage was worse, and Eli bore the first ones. Israel had fled. There had been a great slaughter. "Your two sons, Hophni and Phinehas, are both dead, and the ark of God has been captured." At the mention of the ark, not at the mention of his sons, the old man fell backward off his chair by the gate and broke his neck and died, having judged Israel forty years. Down in the town, Phinehas' wife, pregnant, heard the news and went into labor and died giving birth to a son, and with her last strength she named the boy for the whole catastrophe: Ichabod, no glory, saying, "The glory has departed from Israel," because the ark of God had been captured. A dying woman gave the era its epitaph.

What the Philistines did with their trophy tells you what they thought they had won. They carried the ark into the temple of their god Dagon in Ashdod and set it beside his statue, a prisoner of war presented to the victor. In the morning Dagon was on his face on the floor before the ark. They stood him back up. The next morning he was down again, and this time his head and his hands were broken off and lying on the threshold: "Only the torso remained." The text reports this with a perfectly straight face, and notes, almost as an aside, that Dagon's priests in Ashdod do not step on the threshold to this day. Then the hand of the LORD, which Israel had tried to borrow for a battle, fell on Ashdod on its own errand: tumors, panic, a plague with rats in it, and the ark began a tour no one had planned, from Ashdod to Gath to Ekron, and at Ekron the people screamed when it arrived: "They have brought us the ark of the God of Israel in order to kill us and our people!" The prisoner was conquering the empire city by city, without one Israelite soldier in the room.

After seven months the Philistines consulted their own priests about how to send it back, and their priests, to their credit, read the situation more clearly than Israel's elders had. Do not send it back empty, they said. Send a guilt offering: five gold tumors and five gold rats, one of each for every Philistine ruler. "Give glory to the God of Israel, and perhaps He will lift His hand from you and your gods and your land." And they reached for the precedent their whole region knew: "Why harden your hearts as the Egyptians and Pharaoh hardened theirs?" Then they designed a test to settle whether any of this had been God at all. Two milk cows, never yoked, calves taken away and penned, hitched to a new cart with the ark on it and no driver. If the cows walked away from their own crying calves and took the road to Israelite Beth-shemesh, it was the LORD. The cows went straight up the highway, "lowing as they went, never straying to the right or to the left", with the five rulers of the Philistines walking behind to watch their god's conqueror leave. At Beth-shemesh men were harvesting wheat, and they looked up and saw the ark coming driverless into the valley, and the cart was chopped for firewood and the cows offered on a great rock, and the day was joy. But the chapter does not end in joy, because the holiness that had flattened Dagon was not tame for Israel either. Seventy men of Beth-shemesh were struck down for looking inside the ark, and the town asked the question Shiloh should have asked years before: "Who can stand in the presence of the LORD, this holy God?"

So the ark went to rest at Kiriath-jearim, in the house of Abinadab on the hill, guarded by his consecrated son Eleazar, and there it stayed. And there the story does something quiet and enormous: "And from that day a long time passed, twenty years in all, as the ark remained at Kiriath-jearim. And all the house of Israel mournfully sought the LORD." Twenty years compressed into a verse, twenty years with no ark in any sanctuary, no great prophet's campaign, just a nation slowly mourning its way back toward God. When Samuel finally speaks in this book as its leader, it is at the end of those twenty years, and his first address is not strategy, it is conditions: "If you are returning to the LORD with all your hearts, then put away the foreign gods and Ashtoreths among you, prepare your hearts for the LORD, and serve Him only. And He will deliver you from the hand of the Philistines." Israel obeyed. The Baals and Ashtoreths went out, and the nation gathered at Mizpah and poured water out before the LORD and fasted and said the sentence revival always comes down to: "We have sinned against the LORD."

The Philistines heard about the gathering and did what armies do with unarmed revival meetings: they marched on it. Israel panicked, and turned, at last, to the right address: "Do not stop crying out to the LORD our God for us, that He may save us from the hand of the Philistines." Samuel offered a lamb and cried out, and Hannah's song walked off the page: she had sung that God would thunder from heaven against His enemies, and now, as the Philistine line advanced on a praying congregation, "the LORD thundered loudly against the Philistines and threw them into such confusion that they fled before Israel". Israel charged out of Mizpah and pursued them, and afterward Samuel set up a single stone between Mizpah and Shen and gave it the name the first battle's campsite had carried in irony. Ebenezer. "Thus far the LORD has helped us." The first Ebenezer was where Israel tried to use God and lost thirty thousand men and the ark. The second was where Israel gave God back His conditions and won without a battle plan. The stone was the lesson in geography.

The Philistines were subdued, the text says, and did not invade again all the days of Samuel, and the stolen cities came back, and Samuel settled into the work the book opened its adult story with: he judged Israel all his life, riding a yearly circuit, Bethel to Gilgal to Mizpah, and home to Ramah, where he built an altar. The boy who had answered a voice in the dark had become the nation's judge, priest, and prophet in one person, and for twenty years and more the system held together on the strength of one faithful man. It was a good system. It had one flaw, and Samuel was mortal, and his sons were already grown.
