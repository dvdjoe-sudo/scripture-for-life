1 Samuel Episode 6: The Shepherd Anointed, and the Giant
1 Samuel 16 to 17, Berean Standard Bible

God let Samuel grieve for Saul, and then God ended the grieving. "How long are you going to mourn for Saul, since I have rejected him as king over Israel? Fill your horn with oil and go. I am sending you to Jesse of Bethlehem, for I have selected from his sons a king for Myself." A king for Myself. Israel had demanded a king for themselves, and gotten one built to their specifications; the replacement would be God's own selection, made by God's own criteria, and almost nobody would know it had happened. Samuel's first response shows what Saul's Israel had already become: the prophet was afraid the king would kill him for going. He went under the cover of a sacrifice, and the elders of Bethlehem came out trembling to meet him, because in Saul's kingdom a visit from Samuel might be an arrest: "Do you come in peace?"

At Jesse's house the sons were paraded, and the first of them walked in tall and broad and king-shaped, and Samuel, the man who had watched God reject a king chosen for his height, made Israel's mistake all over again on sight: "Surely here before the LORD is His anointed." God's correction of His own prophet is the interpretive key to both books of Samuel, and it names the disqualifier directly: "Do not consider his appearance or height, for I have rejected him; the LORD does not see as man does. For man sees the outward appearance, but the LORD sees the heart." Height. Saul had been chosen by a nation measuring from the head up, a head taller than everyone, and the new king would be measured from the heart out. Six more sons passed. Seven rejections. "The LORD has not chosen any of these." Samuel asked the question that exposed how the youngest son ranked in his own father's house: "Are these all the sons you have?" And Jesse answered almost as an afterthought: "There is still the youngest," he said, "but he is tending the sheep." Not summoned. Not named by his father in the text, just the youngest, out with the flock, an errand boy's absence at a sacrifice where kings were being chosen. Samuel made the whole feast stand waiting: we will not sit down until he arrives. They sent for him, and the shepherd came in from the fields, "ruddy, with beautiful eyes and a handsome appearance", and the LORD said the words the whole secret turned on: "Rise and anoint him, for he is the one." Samuel took the horn of oil and anointed David in front of the brothers who had all been passed over, "and the Spirit of the LORD rushed upon David from that day forward".

The next verse performs the transfer in a single breath: "Now the Spirit of the LORD departed from Saul, and a spirit of distress from the LORD began to torment him." The book states the exchange flatly, Spirit for spirit, and does not soften its own attribution; the torment that now stalked Saul is laid at heaven's door by the same narrator who credits heaven with David's rise. Saul's servants prescribed music, and the search for a harpist produced a courtier's description of the new servant that reads, in hindsight, like a dossier: Jesse's son "knows how to play the harp. He is a mighty man of valor, a warrior, eloquent and handsome, and the LORD is with him." Mighty man of valor, warrior, the LORD with him: all of it already true of the shepherd before Goliath, filed away in small talk. Saul sent for David, and Saul loved him, the text says, very much, and made him his armor-bearer, and whenever the distress came on the king, the boy from Bethlehem played, "and the spirit of distress would depart from him". The rejected king's only relief was the anointed king's hands.

Then the Philistines came back, and the story doubles around to introduce David a second time, from the sheep side, as though the court musician were another person, and both portraits are left standing. In the Valley of Elah the armies camped on facing hills, and out of the Philistine lines came a champion whose measurements the text gives like a quartermaster: Goliath of Gath, six cubits and a span tall, nearer ten feet than nine, a bronze helmet, a coat of mail weighing five thousand shekels, a spear shaft like a weaver's beam with a six-hundred-shekel iron head. Twice a day, for forty days, he stood in the valley and offered single combat with national stakes, winner's nation enslaved to the loser's, and ended with a line aimed past the soldiers at their God: "I defy the ranks of Israel this day! Give me a man to fight!" And the king who was a head taller than any man in Israel, and his whole army, "were dismayed and greatly afraid".

David arrived on an errand. Jesse sent him with roasted grain and loaves for his three oldest brothers and ten cheeses for their commander, and the boy left the sheep with a keeper, reached the camp as the army marched out shouting, dumped his supplies with the quartermaster, and ran to the battle line, and arrived just in time to hear the morning taunt delivered again. What he asked the soldiers around him shows where his mind went that theirs had not. They talked about the king's reward, riches and the princess and tax exemption. David asked about the insult: "What will be done for the man who kills this Philistine and removes this disgrace from Israel? Just who is this uncircumcised Philistine, that he should defy the armies of the living God?" His oldest brother heard him and lit into him in front of the men, the rejected Eliab accusing the anointed David of the exact charge God had cleared him of: "I know your pride and wickedness of heart", he said, you came down to watch the battle. David's answer was the sound of a younger brother who had been absorbing this for years: "What have I done now?" But other ears carried his words to Saul, and Saul sent for him, and the shepherd told the king of Israel what no soldier in the valley had dared to say: "Let no man’s heart fail on account of this Philistine. Your servant will go and fight him!"

Saul objected with the resume gap, a boy against a warrior from his youth, and David answered with the only credentials he had, from the years nobody had watched: the lion and the bear that came for the flock, chased down and killed by hand, lambs rescued out of their mouths. "Your servant has killed lions and bears; this uncircumcised Philistine will be like one of them, for he has defied the armies of the living God." And then the creed underneath the credentials: "The LORD, who delivered me from the claws of the lion and the bear, will deliver me from the hand of this Philistine." Past deliverance read as future promise. Saul gave him permission and his own armor, and the armor did not fit the boy's habits, and David took it off and went down to the brook and chose five smooth stones and walked toward the Philistine with a staff, a sling, and a shepherd's bag. Goliath saw him coming and was insulted to the bottom of his giant heart: "Am I a dog," he said, "that you come at me with sticks?" and he cursed David by his gods and promised his flesh to the birds.

David answered the curse with the longest speech anyone gives in the chapter, and it is not about David. "You come against me with sword and spear and javelin, but I come against you in the name of the LORD of Hosts, the God of the armies of Israel, whom you have defied." This day, he said, the LORD will deliver you into my hand, and the Philistine army's carcasses to the birds, "Then the whole world will know that there is a God in Israel. And all those assembled here will know that it is not by sword or spear that the LORD saves; for the battle is the LORD’s, and He will give all of you into our hands." It is Jonathan's theology with a sling in it, the same confession his father's son made on the cliff at Michmash: the LORD saves, by many or by few, and the instruments are interchangeable. Then David did the thing that separates him from the king on the hill. Goliath moved forward to attack, and David ran. Toward him. He ran quickly to the battle line, hand in the bag, stone in the sling, and the stone sank into the giant's forehead, "and he fell facedown on the ground", face down in the dirt of the valley like Dagon on his temple floor before the ark. David had brought no sword, so he finished it with the Philistine's own, taking the head with the giant's blade, and the Philistine army ran, and Israel and Judah charged shouting and cut them down along the road to the very gates of Gath and Ekron. David carried the head to Jerusalem and kept the armor in his tent, a shepherd rearranging the furniture of the future.

And on the hill, the king who had loved the harpist, who had made him armor-bearer, who had sent him down in his own tunic, watched the boy come back holding the giant's head, and asked his general the question the rest of the book exists to answer: "Abner, whose son is this young man?" Abner did not know. So the king had him brought in, head still in hand, and asked him directly, and David answered with the plain address of the house God had chosen over Saul's: "I am the son of your servant Jesse of Bethlehem".
