1 Samuel Episode 9: Abigail, and the Night at Endor
1 Samuel 25 to 28, Berean Standard Bible

The book pauses on a death before it resumes the chase. "When Samuel died, all Israel gathered to mourn for him; and they buried him at his home in Ramah." The prophet who had anointed both kings was gone, and with him went the last voice Saul had ever obeyed, even badly. David withdrew to the wilderness of Paran, where his six hundred were doing for local shepherds what an army does in a country the king has abandoned: guarding them. In Carmel lived the richest beneficiary of that protection, a Calebite named Nabal, three thousand sheep, a thousand goats, and a wife named Abigail, whom the text introduces in a single balanced sentence against her husband: she was intelligent and beautiful; he was harsh and evil in his dealings. At shearing time, the feast of the pastoral year, David sent ten men with a courteous request for whatever Nabal could spare, pointing out, accurately, that his shepherds had lost nothing while David's men were near. Nabal answered the courtesy with a sneer that hit every target at once, David's legitimacy, David's family, and David's men: "Who is David? Who is this son of Jesse? Many servants these days are breaking away from their masters. Why should I take my bread and water and the meat I have slaughtered for my shearers, and give them to these men whose origin I do not know?"

David's response shows how thin the fugitive's righteousness could wear under insult. He did not appeal to God or weigh the provocation. He swore an oath of extermination: "May God punish David, and ever so severely, if I let one male belonging to Nabal survive until morning." Four hundred men strapped on swords to massacre a household over an unpaid catering bill and a rude answer. What saved David from the worst act of his life was a servant's report and a wife's speed. One of Nabal's own young men told Abigail what her husband had done and what was coming, and his testimony about David's men is in the record: "They were a wall around us, both day and night, the whole time we were herding our sheep near them." Abigail loaded donkeys with a small harvest of food, two hundred loaves, wine, dressed sheep, roasted grain, raisins, fig cakes, and rode out into the ravine to meet four hundred armed men, without telling her husband. She came off the donkey and fell facedown and delivered the greatest speech of the fugitive years, beginning by absorbing the guilt herself: "My lord, may the blame be on me alone". Then she separated her husband from his household with the text's own dictionary: "My lord should pay no attention to this scoundrel Nabal, for he lives up to his name: His name means Fool, and folly accompanies him."

Then she did the thing no one else in this book manages with David. She prophesied at him, accurately, and used his future as the argument against his present. The LORD, she said, has held you back from bloodshed and from avenging yourself with your own hand. Your enemies will end like Nabal. "Please forgive your servant’s offense, for the LORD will surely make a lasting dynasty for my lord, because he fights the LORD’s battles." And the lives of your enemies, she said, God "shall fling away the lives of your enemies like stones from a sling", a weapon David of all men could picture. Her closing argument was aimed at his conscience years ahead of schedule: when the LORD has made you ruler, "then my lord will have no remorse or guilt of conscience over needless bloodshed and revenge. And when the LORD has dealt well with my lord, may you remember your servant." She was asking a man marching to a massacre to imagine himself a king looking back at this morning, and to choose the memory he could live with.

David stood down, completely, in words that show why he and Saul are different men. Saul, corrected, negotiated for appearances; David, corrected by the wife of the man he came to kill, blessed the correction itself: "Blessed be the LORD, the God of Israel, who sent you to meet me this day! Blessed is your discernment, and blessed are you, because today you kept me from bloodshed and from avenging myself by my own hand." Had you not hurried, he told her, not one male of Nabal's house would have survived the night. He took the food, sent her home in peace, and let the vengeance go to the address Abigail had assigned it. That night Nabal held a feast fit for a king in his house, the king's courtesy he had refused the future king's men, and got very drunk, and in the morning his wife told him everything, and his heart failed within him and he became like a stone, and about ten days later the LORD struck him dead. David heard, and read the whole affair as God's own court ruling: "Blessed be the LORD, who has upheld my cause against the reproach of Nabal and has restrained His servant from evil." Then he sent for Abigail and married the woman who had saved him from himself. The chapter's final verse is Saul's counterstroke from far away: he had given his daughter Michal, David's wife, to another man, erasing the marriage as a king erases a contract.

The Ziphites sold David's position again, and Saul came down with three thousand men again, and the pattern of En-gedi repeated itself one valley over, sharpened. David and Abishai walked into the sleeping camp at night and stood over the king, his spear stuck in the ground by his head, his general and army asleep around him in a sleep the text credits to the LORD. Abishai asked for the kill with a professional's economy: "Today God has delivered your enemy into your hand. Now, therefore, please let me thrust the spear through him into the ground with one stroke. I will not need to strike him twice!" David refused, and this time the refusal comes out as settled doctrine with a timetable attached: "Do not destroy him, for who can extend a hand against the LORD’s anointed and be guiltless?" And then, over the sleeping king, David pronounced the manner of Saul's death without knowing he was doing it: "As surely as the LORD lives, the LORD Himself will strike him down; either his day will come and he will die, or he will go into battle and perish." He took the spear and the water jug and crossed the gulf, and shouted the guard awake and shamed Abner for sleeping on duty, and Saul, hearing the voice, said what he always said in these encounters: "Is that your voice, David my son?"

David's answer this time carries the deepest complaint of his exile. Why does my lord pursue his servant? What evil is in my hand? If the LORD has stirred you up, let Him accept an offering; but if men have done it, may they be cursed: "For today they have driven me away from sharing in the inheritance of the LORD", telling me, in effect, "Go, serve other gods". The king of Israel, he said, "has come out to look for a flea, like one who hunts a partridge in the mountains". And Saul confessed more honestly than he ever had: "I have sinned. Come back, David my son. I will never harm you again, because today you considered my life precious. I have played the fool and have committed a grave error!" It is, in content, an abdication of the quarrel, and Saul sealed it with a blessing that reads like a last will: "May you be blessed, David my son. You will accomplish great things and will surely prevail." They are the last words the two men ever exchanged. David went on his way. He did not come back. The reconciliation was real at the level of Saul's feeling and worthless at the level of Saul's behavior, and David had learned to price it accordingly.

Because even David's faith had a fatigue limit, and the next chapter opens inside his head at its lowest ebb: "One of these days now I will be swept away by the hand of Saul." No inquiry of the LORD is recorded this time, no ephod, just a tired man's arithmetic: the best move left was out, to Philistia, to the protection of Israel's enemy. Achish of Gath, who had once expelled him as a madman, now received him as a defector with six hundred families, and gave him a town of his own, Ziklag, which belonged to Judah's kings from that day forward. For a year and four months David lived the double life, raiding Israel's desert enemies, the Geshurites and Amalekites of the south, and reporting to Achish that he had raided Judah, leaving no survivors behind him to correct the story. The text records the arrangement without comment, the anointed of God surviving inside the enemy's patronage on managed truth, and Achish, entirely deceived, concluded that David had made himself an utter stench to his own people and would be his servant forever.

Then the Philistines mustered for the great campaign, and Achish summoned his vassal with an honor that was a trap in itself: David and his men would march with him against Israel, and David would be his bodyguard for life. David's answer deserves to be heard with both its meanings at once, because the text declines to choose between them: "Then you will come to know what your servant can do." The Philistine war machine was assembling at Shunem, and Israel camped opposite on Gilboa, and Saul saw the size of the army and his heart trembled violently, and he inquired of the LORD, and the heavens he had spent years disobeying went completely silent. "He inquired of the LORD, but the LORD did not answer him by dreams or Urim or prophets." Dreams, the priestly lot, the prophets: all three channels, closed in one verse, and each of them closed by Saul's own history. He had slaughtered the priests of the Urim. He had driven out and lost the prophets. Samuel was dead.

So the king who had purged the land of mediums went looking for one. His servants knew of a woman at Endor who still practiced, and Saul disguised himself and came to her by night with two men, and asked her to consult a spirit and bring up the one he would name. The woman, no fool, quoted the law at the stranger: you know what Saul has done, how he has cut off the mediums; why are you setting a trap for my life? And the king swore to her, by the LORD, the very God whose law he was breaking, that no punishment would come on her for this. Then he named the name: "Bring up Samuel," he said. What happened next frightened even the professional. When the woman saw Samuel, she screamed, because she understood instantly who her client was: "Why have you deceived me? You are Saul!" The book tells the scene as it stands in the text, without explaining the machinery. She described what she saw: "I see a god coming up out of the earth," an old man wrapped in a robe, and Saul knew it was Samuel and bowed with his face to the ground. And the voice that spoke was the voice that had pronounced every sentence of his reign, annoyed at being summoned: "Why have you disturbed me by bringing me up?" Saul poured out the whole of it: "I am deeply distressed," he said. "The Philistines are fighting against me, and God has turned away from me. He no longer answers me, either by prophets or by dreams. So I have called on you to tell me what to do."

The answer was the old sentence, unchanged, plus an appointment. "Why do you consult me," asked Samuel, "since the LORD has turned away from you and become your enemy?" The LORD has done what He said through me: "The LORD has torn the kingdom out of your hand and given it to your neighbor David. Because you did not obey the LORD or carry out His burning anger against Amalek, the LORD has done this to you today." Amalek. From beyond death, the prophet traced the fall to the same altar at Gilgal where it had begun. And then the final word, the one the night had been purchased for: "Moreover, the LORD will deliver Israel with you into the hand of the Philistines, and tomorrow you and your sons will be with me." Tomorrow. Saul fell full length on the ground, emptied of strength; he had eaten nothing all day and night. And the last kindness Saul received in this world came from the woman of Endor, who had risked her life at his word and now insisted the condemned king eat: she killed her fattened calf, baked unleavened bread with her own hands, and set a meal before him and his servants, and they ate, and rose, and went out into the night, back toward Gilboa, where tomorrow was waiting.
