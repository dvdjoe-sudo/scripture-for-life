Leviticus Episode 5: Feasts, Jubilee, and the Choice
Leviticus 23 to 27, Berean Standard Bible

The law had ordered the camp, the altar, the table, and the workshop. What it had not ordered yet was the calendar. These last five chapters mark Israel's time, year by year and generation by generation, and they end with a choice laid out in full.

God called the days His own.

"These are My appointed feasts, the feasts of the LORD that you are to proclaim as sacred assemblies."

The first appointed time was not a feast at all. It was the weekly Sabbath: six days of work, and the seventh day a Sabbath of complete rest, a sacred assembly, no work done, wherever Israel lived. It came first in the list because it came first in the week, and every feast after it was built on the same pattern of stopping.

The year opened in the first month. The Passover to the LORD began at twilight on the fourteenth day. On the fifteenth day began the Feast of Unleavened Bread: seven days of eating unleavened bread, with sacred assemblies and rest from regular work on the first day and the seventh. When Israel entered the land and reaped its harvest, they were to bring a sheaf of the firstfruits to the priest, who would wave it before the LORD on the day after the Sabbath. Until that day, no bread, no roasted grain, no fresh grain from the new crop was to be eaten.

From the day the sheaf was waved, Israel counted: seven full weeks, fifty days, to the day after the seventh Sabbath. Then came an offering of new grain, two loaves of bread with leaven baked from the new harvest, waved before the LORD with lambs and a bull and rams, a sacred assembly with no regular work. And right after the instructions for that feast, the chapter repeats a law from two sections back, as if to make sure the harvest festival did not crowd it out: when you reap the harvest of your land, do not reap to the edges of your field or gather the gleanings. Leave them for the poor and the foreign resident.

The seventh month carried the rest of the year on its back. On the first day of the month, a day of rest, a sacred assembly announced by trumpet blasts, with a food offering to the LORD. On the tenth day, the Day of Atonement again, now written into the calendar the whole people kept: a sacred assembly, souls humbled, no work at all, a Sabbath of complete rest from the evening of the ninth day to the following evening, Israelite and foreigner alike, and anyone who did not humble himself on that day was to be cut off from his people.

Five days later the year closed in shelters. On the fifteenth day of the seventh month, after the produce of the land had been gathered in, the Feast of Tabernacles ran seven days, with complete rest on the first day and on the eighth day after it. Israel was to take the fruit of majestic trees, palm branches, boughs of leafy trees, and willows of the brook, and rejoice before the LORD for seven days. And all the native-born of Israel were to live in booths for those seven days. The reason for this feast is stated as plainly as any in the book, and it points backward to tents like the ones Israel was still living in when these words were spoken.

"So that your descendants may know that I made the Israelites dwell in booths when I brought them out of the land of Egypt. I am the LORD your God."

Children not yet born would sleep one week a year in a shelter of branches, after the harvest was safely in, in a land with houses standing on it, so they would know where the family had started.

Chapter 24 steps back inside the tent before it steps into a courtroom. Clear oil of pressed olives was to be brought for the lamps, and Aaron was to tend the lamps on the pure gold lampstand outside the veil, continually, from evening until morning. Twelve loaves of fine flour were to be baked and set in two rows of six on the table of pure gold before the LORD, with pure frankincense by each row. Every Sabbath the bread was set out fresh on behalf of the Israelites as a permanent covenant, and the old bread belonged to Aaron and his sons, to eat in a holy place.

Then the chapter tells its one story, and it is a grim one.

A man whose mother was an Israelite and whose father was an Egyptian went out among the Israelites, and a fight broke out in the camp between him and an Israelite. In the fight, the son of the Israelite woman blasphemed the Name with a curse. They brought him to Moses. His mother's name is the only new name in the chapter: Shelomith, daughter of Dibri, of the tribe of Dan.

The camp did not decide the case on the spot.

"They placed him in custody until the will of the LORD should be made clear to them."

The LORD's answer came to Moses. The blasphemer was to be taken outside the camp. All who had heard him were to lay their hands on his head, and the whole assembly was to stone him. And the word for the camp was general: whoever blasphemes the name of the LORD must be put to death, foreign resident or native. Around that ruling the chapter sets the rest of its measure: a man who takes a life must be put to death; an animal killed must be paid for, animal for animal; and injury is measured exactly.

"Fracture for fracture, eye for eye, tooth for tooth. Just as he injured the other person, the same must be inflicted on him."

One ruling matters most for the man in custody, and it is the ruling his own case had raised. His father was an Egyptian. His mother was Israelite, of Dan. It made no difference to the law that fell on him, in either direction.

"You are to have the same standard of law for the foreign resident and the native; for I am the LORD your God."

Then Moses spoke to the Israelites, and they took the blasphemer outside the camp and stoned him. So the Israelites did as the LORD had commanded Moses.

Chapter 25 gives the land a Sabbath to match the people's. When Israel entered the land God was giving them, the land itself was to observe a Sabbath to the LORD. Six years they could sow their fields, prune their vineyards, and gather what grew. In the seventh year there was to be a Sabbath of complete rest for the land: no sowing, no pruning, no reaping the aftergrowth or gathering grapes from untended vines. What the land produced on its own in its rest year was food for everyone on it: the owner, his servants, his hired hand, the resident foreigner, his livestock, and the wild animals in his land.

The chapter hears the obvious question out loud and answers it.

"What will we eat in the seventh year if we do not sow or gather our produce?"

"I will send My blessing upon you in the sixth year, so that the land will yield a crop sufficient for three years." While Israel sowed in the eighth year, they would still be eating from the old harvest, until the ninth year's crop came in.

Then the count goes further out. Seven Sabbaths of years: seven times seven years, forty-nine years. On the Day of Atonement of that count, the horn was to be sounded far and wide throughout the land. The fiftieth year was consecrated.

"And proclaim liberty in the land for all its inhabitants. It shall be your Jubilee, when each of you is to return to his property and to his clan."

In the Year of Jubilee, everything that had been sold under pressure went home. A man who had sold his family's land could redeem it when he was able, and a close relative could redeem it for him; if no one did, it stayed with the buyer only until the Jubilee, and then it was released, and the man returned to his property. Land was priced by the years left until the Jubilee, more years, a higher price, fewer years, a lower one, because what was really being sold was a number of harvests, not the soil itself.

"The land must not be sold permanently, because it is Mine, and you are but foreigners and residents with Me."

Houses stood under a smaller version of the same clock: a house in a walled town could be redeemed for one full year, and after that it passed to the buyer for good, while houses in villages with no walls counted as open fields and were released in the Jubilee. The Levites' houses in their towns could always be redeemed, and the fields around Levitical towns could not be sold at all.

People went home in the Jubilee too. A countryman who fell into poverty was to be helped to keep living among his people, lent to without interest or profit. If he became destitute and sold himself to a fellow Israelite, he was not to be forced into slave labor. He was to stay as a hired worker or a temporary resident until the Year of Jubilee, and then he and his children were released, and he returned to his clan and to his fathers' property. If he sold himself to a wealthy foreigner living among them, the right of redemption stayed with him: an uncle, a cousin, any close relative could buy him back, or he could redeem himself if he prospered, priced by the years remaining to the Jubilee. The reason underneath every one of these releases is the same, and the chapter states it twice.

"For the Israelites are My servants. They are My servants, whom I brought out of the land of Egypt. I am the LORD your God."

A people God had carried out of slavery could be hired, could fall into debt, could serve years in another man's house. They could not be owned outright. Someone had already claimed them.

Chapter 26 lays the whole covenant out as a choice, and it is the longest sustained speech in the book. It opens with a promise of weight.

If Israel followed God's statutes and carefully kept His commandments, the rains would come in their season. The land would yield its produce and the trees their fruit, and the harvests would run into each other: threshing lasting until the grape harvest, the grape harvest until sowing time, food in plenty, and safety in the land. God would give peace to the land. Israel would lie down with nothing to fear. Dangerous animals would be removed, no sword would pass through the land, and Israel's enemies would fall before them. God would look on the people with favor, make them fruitful, and keep His covenant. Then the promise narrows to the sentence the whole book has been building toward since the glory filled the tent.

"I will make My dwelling place among you, and My soul will not despise you. I will walk among you and be your God, and you will be My people."

Then the chapter turns, and most of its length is the turning. If Israel failed to obey and carried out none of this, the judgments come in waves, and each wave ends with the same hinge: if after all this you will not obey Me. Terror and disease that waste the eyes. Sowing seed in vain while enemies eat the crop. Defeat, and rule by those who hate them, and fleeing when no one pursues. Then punishment sevenfold for their sins: skies like iron and land like bronze, strength spent for nothing, wild beasts robbing them of children and cattle and leaving the roads empty. Then hostility answered with hostility: plague, the sword avenging the covenant, cities under siege, bread baked ten women to an oven and weighed out and eaten without filling them, and the eating of the flesh of sons and daughters, stated without flinching. Then the high places destroyed, the dead piled on the carcasses of idols, cities in ruins, sanctuaries desolate, the people scattered among the nations and chased by the sound of a driven leaf, the land emptied and lying desolate, finally getting the rest it never got during their Sabbaths, while Israel wasted away in the lands of its enemies.

The chapter does not end there. The last movement of it is the one sentence a reader in exile, generations later, would have needed most.

"But if they will confess their iniquity and that of their fathers in the unfaithfulness that they practiced against Me," and their uncircumcised hearts are humbled, and they make amends for their iniquity, "then I will remember My covenant with Jacob and My covenant with Isaac and My covenant with Abraham, and I will remember the land."

"Yet in spite of this, when they are in the land of their enemies, I will not reject or despise them so as to destroy them and break My covenant with them; for I am the LORD their God."

For their sake He would remember the covenant with their fathers, whom He brought out of the land of Egypt in the sight of the nations, that He might be their God.

And then the book gets quiet. Chapter 27 has no feasts, no stories, and no threats. It is a price list for promises. When a person made a special vow to the LORD involving the value of persons, the priest valued them in silver shekels by age and sex: fifty shekels for a man from twenty to sixty, thirty for a woman, lower for the young and the old, and if the person was too poor for the valuation, the priest set a value the man could afford. An animal fit for the altar, once vowed, could not be exchanged, good for bad or bad for good; if a man swapped anyway, both animals became holy. A consecrated house or a parcel of inherited land was valued by the priest, land by the seed it took and the years left to the Jubilee, and could be redeemed with a fifth added, except for what a man had devoted wholly to the LORD, which could never be sold or redeemed at all. The firstborn of animals already belonged to the LORD by birth and could not be doubled up as a vow. The tithe closed the list: a tenth of the seed of the land and the fruit of the trees, holy to the LORD, and every tenth animal from the herd or flock that passed under the shepherd's rod, the herdsman taking no look at whether it was good or bad, and no swapping, because a swap made both animals holy.

That is the last law in Leviticus. The book's own final line names the mountain it never left.

"These are the commandments that the LORD gave to Moses for the Israelites on Mount Sinai."

Leviticus began with God's voice calling Moses from a tent Moses could not enter, and it ends with Israel still camped at the foot of the same mountain, in the same wilderness, not one mile closer to Canaan than when the book opened. Everything in between happened standing still. And yet the camp at the end of it is not the camp Exodus left behind. It has priests who have served and buried their own. It has an altar whose fire has never gone out. It has a year on its calendar when all its sins are carried away at once, a calendar that makes children sleep in branch shelters to remember Egypt, and a fiftieth year coming on its horizon when trumpets will send every family back to its own ground.

The next sound in the story is a count. God is about to number this taught, cleansed, warned, and promised people by tribe and by family, because a camp that has learned how to live around His tent is about to be told to march.
