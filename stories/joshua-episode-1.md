Joshua Episode 1: The Commission and the Crossing
Joshua 1 to 4, Berean Standard Bible

The book opens with a death in its first line and a promotion in its second breath. "Now after the death of His servant Moses, the LORD spoke to Joshua son of Nun, Moses' assistant". Forty years of story had filed past this man at Moses' elbow, at the mountain, against Amalek, up the slopes of the spies' mission and down under its sentence, and now the voice that had spoken to Moses spoke to him. Its first sentence was a fact and a starting gun together: "Moses My servant is dead. Now therefore arise, you and all these people, and cross over the Jordan into the land that I am giving to the children of Israel."

The promise came with the old borders attached: "I have given you every place where the sole of your foot will tread, just as I promised to Moses." The territory would run from the wilderness and Lebanon to the great River Euphrates, all the land of the Hittites, and west as far as the Great Sea. And over the borders God laid the promise He had made to Moses at another river and a mountain: "No one shall stand against you all the days of your life. As I was with Moses, so will I be with you; I will never leave you nor forsake you."

Then came the charge Joshua would hear three times in one speech, the sentence this book is built around. "Be strong and courageous, for you shall give these people the inheritance of the land that I swore to their fathers I would give them." Again, with the law attached: "Above all, be strong and very courageous. Be careful to observe all the law that My servant Moses commanded you. Do not turn from it to the right or to the left, so that you may prosper wherever you go." And then the discipline that would carry the courage: "This Book of the Law must not depart from your mouth; meditate on it day and night, so that you may be careful to do everything written in it. For then you will prosper and succeed in all you do." The third time, God made it a question too plain to dodge: "Have I not commanded you to be strong and courageous? Do not be afraid; do not be discouraged, for the LORD your God is with you wherever you go."

Joshua's first orders were logistics, not battle. He sent the officers through the camp with a message for every tent: "Prepare your provisions, for within three days you will cross the Jordan to go in and take possession of the land that the LORD your God is giving you to possess." Three days. After forty years, the deadline was three days.

Then Joshua turned to the tribes already settled, the Reubenites, Gadites, and half-tribe of Manasseh, whose families and herds were staying east of the river in the land Moses had given them. Their bargain from the plains of Moab still stood, and Joshua read it back to them in Moses' own words: "The LORD your God will give you rest, and He will give you this land." Their wives, children, and livestock could remain. But every mighty man of valor was to cross armed ahead of his brothers and fight beside them until the LORD gave the western tribes rest too; only then could the eastern men return to their own inheritance.

Their answer is worth hearing in full, because it is the first time in forty years of story that the people, unprompted and undivided, hand a leader his orders back as obedience. "Everything you have commanded us we will do, and everywhere you send us we will go. Just as we obeyed Moses in all things, so we will obey you. And may the LORD your God be with you, as He was with Moses." And they closed by throwing God's own charge back at the man who had just received it, a blessing shaped like an order: "Above all, be strong and courageous!"

Before the three days ran out, Joshua sent two men secretly from Shittim to inspect the land, especially Jericho. They entered the house of a prostitute named Rahab and stayed there, and the city knew by nightfall. The king of Jericho sent word to her door: "Bring out the men who came to you and entered your house, for they have come to spy out the whole land."

Rahab had already hidden the two men under stalks of flax laid out on her roof. What she told the king's messengers was a lie, and the text reports it without praise and without rebuke: the men had come, she said, she had not known where they were from, and at dusk, as the gate was closing, they had gone out; pursue them quickly and you may catch them. The king's men took the bait and rode for the fords of the Jordan, and the gate of Jericho shut behind them.

Then Rahab climbed to the roof, and the woman inside the enemy's wall made the fullest confession of faith anyone in the book has made yet. "I know that the LORD has given you this land and that the fear of you has fallen on us, so that all who dwell in the land are melting in fear of you. For we have heard how the LORD dried up the waters of the Red Sea before you when you came out of Egypt, and what you did to Sihon and Og, the two kings of the Amorites across the Jordan, whom you devoted to destruction. When we heard this, our hearts melted and everyone's courage failed because of you, for the LORD your God is God in the heavens above and on the earth below."

Hear what she had done: from inside Jericho, with nothing but forty-year-old news and recent word from across the river, she had arrived at the sentence Moses spent Deuteronomy teaching Israel to take to heart, that the LORD is God in heaven above and on the earth below. And she built a bargain on it. She had shown the spies kindness; now let them swear by the LORD to show kindness to her father's house, a sure sign, her parents and brothers and sisters and all who belonged to them delivered from death.

"Our lives for your lives!" the men agreed, on conditions, and the conditions were as plain as hers. She was to tie a scarlet cord in the window she would lower them through, gather her whole family inside her house, and keep the mission secret. Anyone who went out the door would carry his own blood on his head; anyone harmed inside the house, theirs on the spies'. Then she let them down by a rope through the window, for her house was built into the city wall, and sent them to the hill country to hide three days while the pursuit searched the road and returned empty.

The report the two men brought Joshua was eleven words of news and one of theology, and it inverted the report their fathers' generation had died for. "The LORD has surely delivered the entire land into our hands". And then the sentence underneath the news: "Indeed, all who dwell in the land are melting in fear of us." Forty years before, ten spies had come home saying the people's hearts melted at the giants in the land. These two came home saying the melting was on the other side of the wall.

Early the next morning Joshua left Shittim with all Israel and camped at the Jordan. After three days the officers went through the camp again, and this time the orders were about a piece of furniture: "When you see the ark of the covenant of the LORD your God being carried by the Levitical priests, you are to set out from your positions and follow it. But keep a distance of about two thousand cubits between yourselves and the ark. Do not go near it, so that you can see the way to go, since you have never traveled this way before."

The distance was the point. The ark would go first, half a mile of empty ground behind it, so that the whole nation could watch where the road into the land actually began. Joshua told the people: "Consecrate yourselves, for tomorrow the LORD will do wonders among you." And to Joshua himself, God said the quiet thing underneath the ceremony: "Today I will begin to exalt you in the sight of all Israel, so they may know that I am with you just as I was with Moses."

Joshua announced the sign before it happened, the way his master had taught the people to listen. "This is how you will know that the living God is among you and that He will surely drive out before you the Canaanites, Hittites, Hivites, Perizzites, Girgashites, Amorites, and Jebusites. Behold, the ark of the covenant of the Lord of all the earth will go ahead of you into the Jordan." Twelve men were chosen, one from each tribe, for work not yet explained. Joshua said that the moment the priests' feet touched the water, "its flowing waters will be cut off and will stand up in a heap."

The timing was the worst of the year, and the text makes sure we know it: "Now the Jordan overflows its banks throughout the harvest season." Not a low summer ford but a river in flood. The priests reached the edge, and "as soon as the priests carrying the ark reached the Jordan and their feet touched the water's edge," the flow stopped. The water coming down "stood still. It backed up as far upstream as Adam, a city in the area of Zarethan, while the water flowing toward the Sea of the Arabah (the Salt Sea) was completely cut off. So the people crossed over opposite Jericho."

The priests did not hurry across. "The priests carrying the ark of the covenant of the LORD stood firm on dry ground in the middle of the Jordan, while all Israel crossed over the dry ground, until the entire nation had crossed the Jordan." A nation walked through a stopped river in sight of Jericho's walls, and the men holding the ark stood in the middle of it until the last Israelite was through.

Before the priests came up, there was work for the twelve men. Each was to carry a stone from the middle of the Jordan, from the very place the priests stood, one stone per tribe on a man's shoulder, and set them down where the nation would spend the night. Joshua gave them the reason in advance, and it was a reason aimed at children not yet born: the stones were "to serve as a sign among you. In the future, when your children ask," and here the future question stands in the text: "What do these stones mean to you?" The answer was prescribed just as carefully: "The waters of the Jordan were cut off before the ark of the covenant of the LORD. When it crossed the Jordan, the waters were cut off." And then the verdict on the pile: "Therefore these stones will be a memorial to the Israelites forever."

Joshua did something more, and it was not in the orders. "Joshua also set up twelve stones in the middle of the Jordan, in the place where the priests who carried the ark of the covenant stood. And the stones are there to this day." Two memorials for one crossing: one at the camp, where children would ask about it, and one in the riverbed, where the returning water would cover it the moment the priests stepped out. The second pile was for no audience the text names.

The eastern tribes crossed too, armed before Israel as Moses had instructed, about forty thousand troops ready for battle crossing "before the LORD into the plains of Jericho." Then the priests were called up, and the day's two promises closed at once: "When the priests carrying the ark of the covenant of the LORD came up out of the Jordan and their feet touched the dry land, the waters of the Jordan returned to their course and overflowed all the banks as before." And the narrator records the other fulfillment in the same breath as the first: "On that day the LORD exalted Joshua in the sight of all Israel, and they revered him all the days of his life, just as they had revered Moses."

It was the tenth day of the first month, the very date on which, a generation earlier, Israel had chosen its Passover lambs in Egypt. The nation camped at Gilgal, on the eastern border of Jericho, and there Joshua set up the twelve stones. His teaching about them reached further than the camp that heard it: when children ask their fathers, "What is the meaning of these stones?" the answer is to begin with a dry riverbed: "Israel crossed the Jordan on dry ground." And then the comparison the answer itself builds in: "For the LORD your God dried up the waters of the Jordan before you until you had crossed over, just as He did to the Red Sea, which He dried up before us until we had crossed over." And the purpose of the pile outlasted the crossing itself: "He did this so that all the peoples of the earth may know that the hand of the LORD is mighty, and so that you may always fear the LORD your God."

Behind Israel, the Jordan ran at flood again, closed like a door. In front of Israel, a few miles west, stood Jericho, its gate shut since the spies came. Between the river and the wall lay the camp at Gilgal, and the first Passover in the land was four days away.
