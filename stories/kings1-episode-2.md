1 Kings Episode 2: A Wise and Discerning Heart
1 Kings 3-4, Berean Standard Bible

The reign began with a marriage and a reservation. Solomon made an alliance with Pharaoh of Egypt and took his daughter, bringing her to the City of David to wait while he finished his palace, the house of the LORD, and the wall around Jerusalem. And the text gives its young king a sentence with a hinge in the middle: “Solomon loved the LORD and walked in the statutes of his father David, except that he sacrificed and burned incense on the high places”. The high places were the old local shrines, still in use because no house for the Name of the LORD had been built yet, and the whole nation used them, not Solomon alone. But the word except is planted here, in the third chapter of the book, like a surveyor's mark. Solomon went up to the greatest of them, at Gibeon, and offered a thousand burnt offerings on its altar. And it was there, in the place the text has just marked, that God came to him in the night with the most open offer ever made to a king: “Ask, and I will give it to you!”

Solomon's answer is a small masterpiece of knowing where he stood. He recited what God had done for David, faithfulness answered with faithfulness, and then he set his own measure beside the size of the job: “But I am only a little child, not knowing how to go out or come in.” The people were too numerous to count. So he did not ask for years, or money, or dead enemies. He asked for capacity: “Therefore give Your servant an understanding heart to judge Your people and to discern between good and evil.” The text says it pleased the Lord that Solomon had asked for this, and the answer gave him the ask and then overflowed it. “I will give you a wise and discerning heart, so that there will never have been another like you, nor will there ever be.” Riches and honor came with it, unasked, and after the gifts came the one condition, the same condition David had charged him with on his deathbed: “So if you walk in My ways and keep My statutes and commandments, just as your father David did, I will prolong your days.” Solomon woke, and it had been a dream, and he went back to Jerusalem and stood before the ark and offered sacrifices and held a feast for all his servants. The wisdom had been given in the dark. The book now shows it working in the light, on the first hard case that walked through the door.

Two prostitutes came to the king, and the case had no evidence at all. They lived alone in one house. One had given birth, and three days later the other had given birth, and in the night the second woman's son died because she rolled over on him. What happened next was the whole dispute. The first woman said the mother of the dead child rose in the middle of the night, while she slept, and swapped the babies, laying the dead son at her breast and taking the living one. She woke to nurse and found a dead child, and in the morning light saw it was not the son she had borne. The other woman denied it flat: the living one was hers, the dead one was the other's. There were no witnesses. There was nothing to examine. It was one woman's word against another's, and both of them were nobodies, the lowest witnesses a court could hear. Solomon listened to the argument, restated it in one level sentence, and then he called for a sword. “Cut the living child in two and give half to one and half to the other.” The ruling was never a ruling. It was a blade held over the one thing both women had said and only one of them meant, and it drew the truth out of them in their next breath. The real mother spoke because she yearned with compassion for her son, and she surrendered her entire case to save the boy's life: “give her the living baby. Do not kill him!” The other woman said the opposite with equal speed: “He will be neither mine nor yours. Cut him in two!” Then the king gave his actual ruling, and it is worth hearing whole: “Give the living baby to the first woman. By no means should you kill him; she is his mother.” When all Israel heard of the judgment, the text says, they stood in awe of the king, for “they saw that the wisdom of God was in him to administer justice”. The dream at Gibeon had produced its first fruit, and it had fallen in the lowest court in the land, between two women nobody else would have believed.

Chapter 4 is the kingdom seen from above, and it reads like the answer to the prayer laid out in ledgers. The officials are listed: Benaiah over the army, Zadok the priest, secretaries and a recorder, and a name worth filing away, Adoniram son of Abda, in charge of the forced labor. The officials list still names Abiathar among the priests, as it stands in the text, though Solomon had sent him home to Anathoth in the last chapter. Twelve governors divided the land into twelve districts, each feeding the king and his household for one month of the year, a system that spread the cost of the court across the whole nation in rotation. And the cost was enormous. A single day at Solomon's table took thirty cors of fine flour and sixty of meal, ten fat oxen and twenty range oxen and a hundred sheep, besides deer and gazelles and fattened birds. The borders ran from the Euphrates to the border of Egypt, and the kingdoms inside them brought tribute. The people themselves are described in a sentence that reaches back to the promises made to Abraham: “The people of Judah and Israel were as numerous as the sand on the seashore, and they were eating and drinking and rejoicing.” And the peace is drawn in one domestic picture: “Judah and Israel dwelt securely from Dan to Beersheba, each man under his own vine and his own fig tree”. Every man under his own vine. No one under Solomon's table yet, though the table was being fed by all of them, one month at a time.

The chapter closes where the dream began, with the wisdom, now famous beyond Israel's borders. God gave Solomon “wisdom, exceedingly deep insight, and understanding beyond measure, like the sand on the seashore”. He was measured against the legendary sages of the East and of Egypt and found greater than all of them. He composed three thousand proverbs and a thousand and five songs, and his knowledge ran the whole created order, from the cedar in Lebanon to the hyssop growing out of a wall, animals and birds and reptiles and fish. Two of the psalms in Israel's hymnbook carry his name in their headings, Psalm 72 and Psalm 127, and the book will have more to say about the man who prayed for his kingdom to last. Men of all nations came to hear this wisdom, sent by kings who had heard of it. Israel had asked God for a king generations ago, in Samuel's day, and had been warned what a king would take. For now, under this king, the taking was organized, the borders were quiet, and the judgment was just. It is the highest point of the whole story of the monarchy, and the book lets it stand in full sunlight, ledgers, table, horses and all. The except of chapter 3 is still on the page. But it has not ripened yet.
