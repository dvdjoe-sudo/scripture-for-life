Judges Episode 8: Samson and Delilah
Judges 16, Berean Standard Bible

One day Samson went down to Gaza, the southernmost of the Philistines' five cities, and saw a prostitute and went in to her. Word reached the Gazites, and they surrounded the place and lay in wait at the city gate all night, keeping quiet, planning to kill him at dawn. Samson did not stay the night. "But Samson lay there only until midnight, when he got up, took hold of the doors of the city gate and both gateposts, and pulled them out, bar and all. Then he put them on his shoulders and took them to the top of the mountain overlooking Hebron." He carried a city's gate, doors, posts, and locking bar, on his shoulders, out of Gaza and up into Israel's hill country, and left it on a mountaintop like a signature. The ambush at dawn found an open hole where the gate had been. That is Samson at his ceiling: careless about why he was in Gaza, untouchable in what he could do there.

"Some time later, Samson fell in love with a woman in the Valley of Sorek, whose name was Delilah." The text says he loved her. It is the only woman in Samson's life the book names, the wife at Timnah went unnamed and so did the woman at Gaza, and it is the only time the book says Samson loved anyone. The lords of the Philistines came to her with a contract priced like a king's ransom: "Entice him and find out the source of his great strength and how we can overpower him to tie him up and subdue him. Then each one of us will give you eleven hundred shekels of silver." Five lords, eleven hundred shekels each, fifty-five hundred shekels of silver for one secret, more than three times the weight of gold Gideon took for his ephod, offered to a woman in a valley on Israel's border. She took the contract, and she asked him the question straight out, in nearly the lords' own words: "Please tell me the source of your great strength and how you can be tied up and subdued."

Samson lied to her three times, and the lies are told here in their pattern, because the pattern is the diagnosis. First: "If they tie me up with seven fresh bowstrings that have not been dried, I will become as weak as any other man." She tied him with seven fresh bowstrings while Philistines hid in her room, and called out the ambush phrase: "Samson, the Philistines are here!" He snapped the bowstrings "like a strand of yarn seared by a flame." Second, after her reproach, "You have mocked me and lied to me! Now please tell me how you can be tied up," he upgraded the lie slightly: "If they tie me up with new ropes that have never been used, I will become as weak as any other man." New ropes, hidden men, the same shout, and he snapped the ropes "like they were threads." Third time, the lie moved to his head: "If you weave the seven braids of my head into the web of a loom and tighten it with a pin, I will become as weak as any other man." She wove his hair into a loom while he slept, pin and web and all, and shouted again, and he woke and walked off with the loom. Each lie brought the secret closer to the surface. The third one was about his hair and his sleep, one step from the truth, told to the woman who had already betrayed the first two answers to men hiding in her room. Samson could count. He stayed anyway. That is the riddle of Samson that he never posed at a feast: not how he could be so strong, but how a man who tore a lion apart could not walk out of a room he knew was a trap.

Her fourth approach changed weapons, from curiosity to love itself. She threw his own claim back at him, how can you say you love me "when your heart is not with me? This is the third time you have mocked me and failed to reveal to me the source of your great strength!" And she kept at him: "Finally, after she had pressed him daily with her words and pleaded until he was sick to death, Samson told her all that was in his heart." Sick to death is the text's phrase for what seven days of tears had done at Timnah and daily pleading did at Sorek. His tongue was the weakest muscle he had. "My hair has never been cut, because I have been a Nazirite to God from my mother's womb. If I am shaved, my strength will leave me, and I will become as weak as any other man."

Delilah knew the sound of the truth when it finally came. When she realized he had revealed to her all that was in his heart, she sent this message to the lords of the Philistines: "Come up once more, for he has revealed to me all that is in his heart." The lords came up with the silver in their hands. And she lulled him to sleep on her lap, the safest place he knew, and called a man in to shave the seven braids off his sleeping head. "In this way she began to subdue him, and his strength left him." Then she called out the phrase for the fourth time, the phrase that had always been a false alarm: "Samson, the Philistines are here!" He woke and thought the thought that had always been true before: "I will escape as I did before and shake myself free." And the narrator sets the saddest line in the book beside it, level and quiet: "But he did not know that the LORD had departed from him." His strength had never been his hair, or his arms. It had been a presence, and the presence could leave a man while he slept, and he could wake reaching for it and find it gone.

The Philistines seized him and gouged out his eyes, the eyes that had done all his choosing, by his own rule that a woman was pleasing to his eyes, now answered by darkness. They brought him down to Gaza, the city whose gates he had carried off, and bound him with bronze shackles, real metal for the man ropes could not hold, "and forced to grind grain in the prison." Grinding was the work of women and slaves, pushing a millstone in circles. The deliverer of Israel spent his days walking in a circle in the dark, doing the labor of the household he might have had, in the stronghold of the enemy he was born to break. And underneath the shackles, one quiet verse turns the whole story: "However, the hair of his head began to grow back after it had been shaved."

The lords of the Philistines read the capture as theology and threw a festival for it, a great sacrifice to Dagon their god, with the verdict sung out loud: "Our god has delivered Samson our enemy into our hands." When the crowd saw the blind prisoner, they praised their god in a fuller chorus: "Our god has delivered into our hands our enemy who destroyed our land and multiplied our dead." And when the drinking had made their hearts merry, they had the idea that pride always eventually has: "Call for Samson to entertain us." Bring out the blind strongman and let him perform. They set him between the pillars of the temple to be laughed at, and the temple was full, every lord of the Philistines in attendance, and about three thousand men and women on the roof watching the show.

Samson asked the servant boy who led him by the hand for one small thing: "Lead me where I can feel the pillars supporting the temple, so I can lean against them." A blind man wanting to rest against a pillar. Nobody in the building understood what he was measuring. Then the prisoner prayed, the second prayer of his life in this book. His first, at Lehi, had asked God for water after a victory and complained while he asked. This one asks for his vengeance by name and calls it that: "O Lord GOD, please remember me. Strengthen me, O God, just once more, so that with one vengeful blow I may pay back the Philistines for my two eyes." The text does not upgrade his motive. He does not pray for Israel, or for the forty years, or for the deliverance he was born only to begin. He prays for his eyes, once more, and God answers the prayer as prayed.

He braced himself against the two central pillars, "with his right hand on one pillar and his left hand on the other," and said his last words, which are a request to be included in the death he was about to deal: "Let me die with the Philistines." Then he pushed with all his might, and the temple came down on the lords of the Philistines and everyone in it, roof, gallery, and congregation together. The narrator weighs the life in one sentence and closes the account: "So in his death he killed more than he had killed in his life."

His brothers and his father's whole family came down to Gaza to claim the body, the family that had traveled down to Timnah to arrange his marriage and watched his whole arc from a distance. They carried him up and buried him between Zorah and Eshtaol, where the Spirit had first begun to stir him, in the tomb of his father Manoah. "And he had judged Israel twenty years." The deliverance he was commissioned to begin, he began: five lords of the Philistines died in one afternoon, more of the enemy's leadership than any judge before him had killed at one stroke, and he did it blind, shackled, and praying for his own revenge. The book refuses to tidy him. He is the strongest deliverer and the least disciplined man in its pages, chosen from the womb and careless with everything he was chosen for, and God used him anyway, beginning a deliverance he never finished and would not live to see finished.

Samson is the last judge the book will follow. What comes next is not a deliverer story at all. The final five chapters step back from the cycle and show Israel from the inside, in its houses and shrines and roads, and they are set early in the period, in the days when Moses' own grandson was still alive to be hired as a priest. The refrain that will sound over them is the bleakest diagnosis the narrator owns: there was no king in Israel, and everyone did what was right in his own eyes.
