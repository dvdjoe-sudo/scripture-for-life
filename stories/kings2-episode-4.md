2 Kings Episode 4: Naaman and Gehazi
2 Kings 5, Berean Standard Bible

The healing of Naaman begins with the book's most unlikely herald: a captured Israelite girl, serving in the house of Aram's great commander. Naaman was a mighty man of valor, honored by his king because through him the LORD had given victory to Aram, “but he was a leper.” The girl, taken in a raid, said to her mistress: “If only my master would go to the prophet who is in Samaria, he would cure him of his leprosy.” The word climbed from a servant's mouth to a king's ear, and Naaman traveled south with a royal letter and a fortune: ten talents of silver, six thousand shekels of gold, ten sets of clothing. But the letter was addressed to the wrong man. It asked the king of Israel to cure the leprosy, and Jehoram read it as a trap and tore his clothes: “Am I God, killing and giving life, that this man expects me to cure a leper?” “Surely you can see that he is seeking a quarrel with me!” Elisha heard of the torn clothes and sent the correction: “Why have you torn your clothes? Please let the man come to me, and he will know that there is a prophet in Israel.”

So Naaman's cavalcade stopped at the door of Elisha's house, and the prophet did not come out. He sent a messenger with the whole prescription: “Go and wash yourself seven times in the Jordan, and your flesh will be restored, and you will be clean.” Naaman went away angry, and his complaint is the sound of a great man insulted by a small instruction: “I thought that he would surely come out, stand and call on the name of the LORD his God, and wave his hand over the spot to cure my leprosy.” The rivers of home were better water anyway: “Are not the Abanah and Pharpar, the rivers of Damascus, better than all the waters of Israel? Could I not have washed in them and been cleansed?” He turned for home in a rage, and his own servants talked him down with the logic of the humble: “My father, if the prophet had told you to do some great thing, would you not have done it?” How much more, then, when the word was only, “Wash and be cleansed”. Naaman went down into the Jordan and dipped seven times, “and his flesh was restored and became like that of a little child, and he was clean.”

He returned with all his attendants to stand before the prophet he had not been deemed worth coming out for, and made the confession the whole episode was built to hold: “Now I know for sure that there is no God in all the earth except in Israel.” Then he pressed his fortune on Elisha as a gift, and Elisha refused it with an oath: “As surely as the LORD lives, before whom I stand, I will not accept it.” The healing of God was not for sale, not even to a grateful general. Naaman asked instead for something stranger than gold: “please let me, your servant, be given as much soil as a pair of mules can carry.” Israelite earth to worship on in Damascus, because, he said, “your servant will never again make a burnt offering or a sacrifice to any other god but the LORD.” And he made one honest confession of the compromise waiting for him at home, that when his king leaned on his arm in the temple of Rimmon, he would have to bow too, and he asked pardon for it in advance: “may the LORD forgive your servant this one thing”. Elisha let him go in peace.

Then the episode turns, and the servant who carried the prophet's errands ran after the chariot for the prophet's price. Gehazi had watched the fortune refused and could not bear it: “my master has spared this Aramean, Naaman, by not accepting what he brought.” He caught up with Naaman and told a lie built entirely out of the prophet's world: “Everything is all right,” Gehazi replied, the very words the Shunammite had used in truth, now borrowed for a fraud. His master had sent him, he said: “Look, two young men from the sons of the prophets have just now come to me from the hill country of Ephraim. Please give them a talent of silver and two sets of clothing.” Naaman, generous as the healed are, insisted: “Please, take two talents.” Gehazi took them, hid them in the house, dismissed the servants, and stood before Elisha with a clear face and a small answer: “Your servant did not go anywhere,” he replied. Elisha's questions stripped the lie in two strokes: “Did not my spirit go with you when the man got down from his chariot to meet you?” “Is this the time to accept money and clothing, olive groves and vineyards, sheep and oxen, menservants and maidservants?” The sentence matched the theft to its source: “the leprosy of Naaman will cling to you and your descendants forever!” Gehazi went out from his master's presence leprous, white as snow: the disease the Jordan had washed off the foreigner now settled on the prophet's own servant, who had tried to sell what God had given away.
