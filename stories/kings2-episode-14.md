2 Kings Episode 14: Josiah and the Book Found
2 Kings 22-23, Berean Standard Bible

Josiah became king at eight, in the wreckage of Manasseh's palace, and the book's verdict on him reaches back past every king since David for its comparison: “he did what was right in the eyes of the LORD and walked in all the ways of his father David; he did not turn aside to the right or to the left.” In the eighteenth year of his reign he sent Shaphan the scribe to the temple to fund repairs, with the same trust his ancestor Joash's chest had embodied: “they need not account for the money put into their hands, since they work with integrity.” Then Hilkiah the high priest made the announcement that turns the whole book: “I have found the Book of the Law in the house of the LORD!” A book of God's law, lost inside God's house, through the reigns of kings who sacrificed in its courtyards. Shaphan read it, carried it to the king, reported the repair money first, and then read the book aloud in the king's presence. When Josiah heard the words, he tore his clothes, the gesture this book has used for guilt and grief from Ahaziah's messengers onward, and his command shows he had understood exactly what he had heard: “Go and inquire of the LORD for me, for the people, and for all Judah concerning the words in this book that has been found. For great is the wrath of the LORD that burns against us because our fathers have not obeyed the words of this book by doing all that is written about us.” He did not ask whether the book was authentic. He asked what its wrath meant for a nation that had been breaking it unread for generations.

The delegation went to Huldah the prophetess, in Jerusalem's Second District, and her answer came in two parts, doom and exception, and neither part bends. The doom confirms Manasseh's sentence without revising a word of it: “I am about to bring calamity on this place and on its people, according to all the words of the book that the king of Judah has read”, “because they have forsaken Me and burned incense to other gods, that they might provoke Me to anger with all the works of their hands. My wrath will be kindled against this place and will not be quenched.” The exception is personal, and it is earned by exactly one thing, the king's response to the reading: “because your heart was tender and you humbled yourself before the LORD when you heard what I spoke against this place and against its people, that they would become a desolation and a curse, and because you have torn your clothes and wept before Me, I have heard you”. “Therefore I will indeed gather you to your fathers, and you will be gathered to your grave in peace. Your eyes will not see all the calamity that I will bring on this place.” Josiah is promised the only mercy left in the book: he will not have to watch it.

What he did with a reprieve for himself and none for his nation is the greatest reform in the books of Kings. He gathered all Judah, small and great, read the whole Book of the Covenant aloud in their hearing, stood by the pillar, and covenanted the nation back to its God. Then he cleaned, and the cleaning is told as a catalogue that runs backward through the entire series. Out of the temple came every article made for Baal, Asherah, and the host of heaven, burned in the Kidron fields and their ashes carried to Bethel. The Asherah pole his grandfather had set in the house was ground to powder and its dust thrown on common graves. The idolatrous priests were deposed, the shrine prostitutes' quarters in the temple torn down, Topheth in the Valley of Ben-hinnom desecrated “so that no one could sacrifice his son or daughter in the fire to Molech”, the horses dedicated to the sun removed and their chariots burned, Manasseh's courtyard altars and Ahaz's roof altars pulverized. He went further back than his own dynasty: east of Jerusalem he desecrated the high places Solomon himself had built, for “Ashtoreth the abomination of the Sidonians”, for Chemosh, for Milcom, shrines that had stood for three centuries under every reforming king untouched. And at Bethel he tore down “the altar at Bethel, the high place set up by Jeroboam son of Nebat, who had caused Israel to sin”, ground it to powder, and burned human bones on it to defile it, and in doing so fulfilled, act for act, the prophecy an unnamed man of God had cried against that altar in the days of Jeroboam himself, in the sixth episode of 1 Kings, three hundred years before. Josiah, turning, noticed a tomb and asked, “What is this monument I see?” It was the man of God's own grave. His order was immediate: “Let him rest,” said Josiah. “Do not let anyone disturb his bones.” The reformer spared the bones of the prophet whose words he had just spent a campaign fulfilling.

Then he kept the Passover the book had been waiting centuries to record: “Keep the Passover of the LORD your God, as it is written in this Book of the Covenant.” “No such Passover had been observed from the days of the judges who had governed Israel through all the days of the kings of Israel and Judah.” The verdict follows, the second superlative of the book: “Neither before nor after Josiah was there any king like him, who turned to the LORD with all his heart and with all his soul and with all his strength, according to all the Law of Moses.” Hezekiah trusted like no other; Josiah turned like no other; the book gives each man his own summit and reconciles nothing. And the very next sentence is the coldest in Kings: “Nevertheless, the LORD did not turn away from the fury of His burning anger, which was kindled against Judah because of all that Manasseh had done to provoke Him to anger.” “I will remove Judah from My sight, just as I removed Israel. I will reject this city Jerusalem, which I chose”. The greatest reform in the monarchy's history changed the king's own end and nothing else. The sentence stood.

Josiah died at Megiddo, going out to confront Pharaoh Neco of Egypt, who was marching to the Euphrates to help a dying Assyria, and Neco killed him at the meeting, though the book gives the confrontation three verses and no battle. His servants carried him home, and the people of the land anointed his son Jehoahaz, who reigned three months before Neco imprisoned him at Riblah and hauled him to Egypt to die, taxing Judah a hundred talents of silver and a talent of gold. Neco renamed the next son, Eliakim became Jehoiakim, a king by a foreigner's appointment, paying Egypt's levy by taxing his own people man by man. He did evil, the book says, just as his fathers had done. The reform had lasted exactly one reign, and the reader who has followed the sentence from Manasseh's oracle knows the only question left is the manner and the date. Both arrive in the next episode, and the agent of them is not Egypt.
