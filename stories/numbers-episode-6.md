Numbers Episode 6: Water, a Serpent, and the End of the Old Road
Numbers 20 and 21, Berean Standard Bible

Something is missing at the start of chapter 20, and the missing thing is about thirty-eight years.

The last episode closed with the camp digesting Korah's rebellion, still in the second year out of Egypt. This chapter opens in the first month, the whole congregation entering the wilderness of Zin and staying at Kadesh, and the first thing the text records is a burial.

"In the first month, the whole congregation of Israel entered the Wilderness of Zin and stayed in Kadesh. There Miriam died and was buried."

No year is named. The forty years of the sentence had nearly run. Miriam the prophetess, who had watched a baby in a basket on the Nile and led Israel's women in song on the far side of the sea, died at Kadesh with the land in sight, and the text gives her death one sentence and a grave. Between chapters 19 and 20 almost an entire generation had died the same way, unrecorded, one grave at a time in a wilderness that was never meant to hold them. The silence of those years is the loudest fact in the book of Numbers. It means the camp reading these chapters now was mostly a new people: the children of the spies chapter, grown, with their parents' graves behind them all the way back to Egypt.

And the first thing the new generation did at Kadesh was the old generation's act, word for word.

There was no water for the congregation, so they gathered against Moses and Aaron and quarreled with him. If only we had perished with our brothers before the LORD. Why have you brought the LORD's assembly into this wilderness for us and our livestock to die here? Why have you led us up out of Egypt to bring us to this wretched place?

"It is not a place of grain, figs, vines, or pomegranates" they said, "and there is no water to drink!"

Read that list against the book so far. Grain, figs, vines, pomegranates: it is the fruit of Canaan, the grapes of Eshcol and the spies' other samples, named by a generation that had grown up on manna within sight of the land that grew them. The complaint was inherited, but the thirst was real, and so was the place. Kadesh was where the spies had set out from. Israel was back at the scene of the sentence, nearly forty years later, making the same speech.

Moses and Aaron went to the entrance of the Tent of Meeting and fell facedown, and the glory of the LORD appeared to them. The instruction God gave was specific, and its details matter, because what Moses did next is measured against them.

"Take the staff and assemble the congregation. You and your brother Aaron are to speak to the rock while they watch, and it will pour out its water. You will bring out water from the rock and provide drink for the congregation and their livestock."

Moses took the staff from before the LORD, as commanded. Whether it was his own staff or Aaron's budding staff, kept in front of the Testimony as a sign, the text does not say, and the story does not depend on it. He and Aaron gathered the assembly in front of the rock. And then Moses spoke, not to the rock, but to the people.

"Listen now, you rebels, must we bring you water out of this rock?"

Then Moses raised his hand and struck the rock twice with his staff, and a great amount of water gushed out, and the congregation and their livestock drank. The miracle worked. God gave the water anyway, to the rebels and to the man who had disobeyed in front of them. And then God spoke to Moses and Aaron together.

"Because you did not trust Me to show My holiness in the sight of the Israelites, you will not bring this assembly into the land that I have given them."

These were the waters of Meribah, quarreling, where the Israelites quarreled with the LORD and He showed His holiness among them. The sentence that had fallen on a whole generation at the spies fell at the end of the wandering on the two men who had interceded for that generation at every step. Moses would see the land. He would not enter it. Aaron would not either. The text does not give Moses a speech of protest, or record Aaron saying anything at all. It moves on, the way the march moved on, and the next event is Israel asking Edom for the road.

Moses sent messengers from Kadesh to the king of Edom with a message built on family: thus says your brother Israel. It rehearsed the whole story, the hardship in Egypt, the cry God heard, the angel, the Exodus, and asked only to pass through on the king's highway, not turning into field or vineyard, not drinking well water, until they had passed through his territory. Edom refused, with a sword behind the refusal: you may not pass through, or I will come out with the sword against you. Israel offered to pay for any water they or their livestock drank, and to come up by the highway only. Edom came out against them with a large and powerful army, and Israel turned away. The descendants of Esau barred the road to the descendants of Jacob, and Israel, who would within the year take land from kings who fought them, would not force a brother's border.

From Kadesh the whole congregation journeyed to Mount Hor, on the border of Edom, and there the second of the three burials of Exodus' leadership was carried out in public, almost like a ceremony, because it was one. God told Moses that Aaron would be gathered to his people and would not enter the land, because both of them had rebelled against His command at the waters of Meribah. Aaron was to be taken up Mount Hor in the sight of the whole congregation, with Eleazar his son.

"So Moses did as the LORD had commanded, and they climbed Mount Hor in the sight of the whole congregation."

"After Moses had removed Aaron's garments and put them on his son Eleazar, Aaron died there on top of the mountain. Then Moses and Eleazar came down from the mountain."

The high priest's robes, the blue and purple and scarlet, the breastpiece with the stones, the gold plate on the turban, were taken off the old man on the mountaintop and put on his son in front of the watching nation below, and the new high priest walked back down the mountain wearing them, next to Moses, alone. When the whole congregation saw that Aaron had died, all the house of Israel mourned for him thirty days. Aaron was 123 years old when he died on Mount Hor. He had spoken for his brother before Pharaoh, built the calf, worn the garments, carried the censer between the living and the dead, and outlived two of his own sons. He died within sight of the land's border, in the office he handed over standing up.

Chapter 21 opens with Israel attacked for the first time by a king who came to them. The Canaanite king of Arad, in the Negev, heard that Israel was coming and attacked them and captured some prisoners. Israel vowed a vow: if God would deliver this people into their hands, they would devote their cities to destruction. God heard, and delivered up the Canaanites, and the place was named Hormah, destruction: the same Hormah where the counted generation had been routed when it tried to enter the land without God. The children won their first battle on the ground of their fathers' defeat.

Then the road bent away from the border again, around Edom, and patience broke for the last recorded time in the old style. The people grew impatient on the journey and spoke against God and against Moses.

"Why have you led us up out of Egypt to die in the wilderness? There is no bread or water, and we detest this wretched food!"

God sent venomous snakes among the people, and many Israelites were bitten and died. This time the people came to Moses with the confession ready: we have sinned, for we have spoken against the LORD and against you. Intercede with the LORD to take the snakes away from us. Moses prayed for the people, the same Moses, the same prayer, forty years in. God's remedy was strange and specific, and it was not to take the snakes away.

"Make a fiery serpent and mount it on a pole. When anyone who is bitten looks at it, he will live."

"So Moses made a bronze snake and mounted it on a pole. If anyone who was bitten looked at the bronze snake, he would live."

The camp was full of dying people looking up at a bronze copy of the thing that was killing them, lifted where everyone could see it, and living. The snakes stayed. The dying had a place to look. Centuries later, Israel's own kings would have to destroy that same bronze serpent, because the people had started burning incense to it, but that is Kings' story, not Numbers'. Here it is what God made it: the means of life, raised on a pole in a camp of bitten people.

From Mount Hor the journey turned, station by station, and the text starts singing. At Beer, the well, Israel sang a song to the water: spring up, O well, sing to it, the well the princes dug. A book in the narrative called the Book of the Wars of the LORD is quoted for a line about the Arnon, the river that marks Moab's border, because Israel was now moving through country with borders and kings, and the road was finally going somewhere.

Sihon king of the Amorites, who ruled in Heshbon, got the same message Edom had gotten: let us pass through your land, on the king's highway, turning aside into no field or vineyard. Edom had refused and Israel had turned away. Sihon refused differently. He gathered his whole army and went out to confront Israel in the wilderness, and fought them at Jahaz.

"And Israel put him to the sword and took possession of his land, from the Arnon to the Jabbok"

The advance stopped only at the border of the Ammonites, because that border was fortified.

It was not a skirmish. Israel took all his cities and settled in them, Heshbon and all its surrounding villages, land that Sihon himself had taken from a former king of Moab, as the proverb makers quoted in the text recalled. Moses sent spies to Jazer and Israel took its towns. Then the road turned up toward Bashan, and Og came out.

Og king of Bashan came out against them at Edrei, he and all his people, the second king in a row to march out and meet Israel in open battle. God's word to Moses before this fight was the word the spies' generation had died refusing to believe.

"Do not fear him, for I have delivered him into your hand, along with all his people and his land. Do to him as you did to Sihon king of the Amorites, who lived in Heshbon."

"So they struck down Og, along with his sons and his whole army, until no remnant was left. And they took possession of his land."

Two kings, two armies, and a stretch of country east of the Jordan, all before Israel had crossed the river. The generation that had seemed like grasshoppers in its own sight had died in the wilderness, down to nearly the last man. Its children had just taken the land of giants without a speech about grasshoppers anywhere in the chapter.

Then the Israelites traveled on and camped in the plains of Moab, across the Jordan from Jericho. From that camp, a king who had watched all of this happen went looking for a prophet to hire.
