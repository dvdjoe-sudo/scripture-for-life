Exodus Episode 3: Passover and the Sea
Exodus 11 to 15, Berean Standard Bible

Egypt had seen the Nile turn to blood. Frogs had filled the houses. Gnats had come from the dust. Flies had blackened Pharaoh's palace. Livestock had died, boils had broken out, hail had flattened the fields, locusts had eaten what the hail left, and darkness had covered the land for three days.

Pharaoh still would not let Israel go.

The LORD told Moses there would be one more plague. "I will bring upon Pharaoh and Egypt one more plague. After that, he will allow you to leave this place. And when he lets you go, he will drive you out completely."

Before Moses left Pharaoh, he announced what that plague would be. About midnight the LORD would go throughout Egypt. Every firstborn son in Egypt would die, from the firstborn of Pharaoh who sat on the throne, to the firstborn of the servant girl behind the hand mill, as well as the firstborn of all the cattle. There would be a great cry throughout Egypt, worse than there had ever been or ever would be again. But among the Israelites, not even a dog would snarl at man or beast. Then Egypt would know that the LORD makes a distinction between Egypt and Israel.

Moses told Pharaoh's officials that they would come down to him, bow before him, and say for Israel to get out, he and all the people who followed him. After that, Moses said, he would leave. Then Moses left Pharaoh in hot anger.

Now the LORD prepared Israel for the night that would break Egypt's hold.

The LORD told Moses and Aaron that this month would be the first month of the year for Israel. On the tenth day of the month, each man was to take a lamb for his family, one for each household. If a household was too small to eat a whole lamb, the family was to share one with its nearest neighbor, keeping count of the people and what each could eat. The animals chosen were to be year-old males without defect, taken from the sheep or the goats. They were to be kept until the fourteenth day of the month, when the whole assembly of Israel would slaughter them at twilight.

"They are to take some of the blood and put it on the sides and tops of the doorframes of the houses where they eat the lambs." That same night they were to eat the meat roasted over the fire, with bitter herbs and bread made without yeast. None of it was to be eaten raw or boiled in water. It was to be roasted over the fire, with the head, legs, and internal organs. None of it was to remain until morning. What remained was to be burned.

"This is how you are to eat it: You must be fully dressed for travel, with your sandals on your feet and your staff in your hand. You are to eat in haste; it is the LORD's Passover."

The LORD said that on that night He would pass through Egypt and strike down every firstborn, both people and animals, and bring judgment on all the gods of Egypt. "The blood on the houses where you are staying will be a sign; when I see the blood, I will pass over you. No plague will fall on you to destroy you when I strike the land of Egypt."

That day was to be kept as a memorial. Israel would keep it as a lasting ordinance through the generations. For seven days they would eat bread made without yeast. On the first day they would remove yeast from their houses. Anyone who ate anything with yeast in it during those seven days would be cut off from Israel. The first and seventh days would be sacred assemblies. No work was to be done on those days except to prepare food for everyone to eat.

At midnight the LORD struck down every firstborn in Egypt, from the firstborn of Pharaoh who sat on the throne, to the firstborn of the prisoner in the dungeon, as well as the firstborn of all the livestock. Pharaoh and all his officials and all the Egyptians got up during the night, and there was a loud wailing in Egypt, for there was not a house without someone dead.

During the night Pharaoh summoned Moses and Aaron. "Get up, leave my people, both you and the Israelites! Go, worship the LORD as you have requested." He told them to take their flocks and herds as they had said, and go. And he asked them to bless him too.

The Egyptians urged the Israelites to hurry and leave the country, for otherwise, they said, they would all die. So the people took their dough before the yeast was added, and carried it on their shoulders in kneading troughs wrapped in clothing. The Israelites had done as Moses told them and asked the Egyptians for articles of silver and gold and for clothing. The LORD had made the Egyptians favorably disposed toward the people, and they gave them what they asked for. In that way Israel plundered the Egyptians.

The Israelites journeyed from Rameses to Succoth. There were about 600,000 men on foot, besides women and children. Many other people went up with them as well, and also large droves of livestock, both flocks and herds. With the dough the Israelites had brought from Egypt, they baked loaves of unleavened bread. The dough was without yeast because they had been driven out of Egypt and did not have time to prepare food for themselves.

Now the time the Israelites had lived in Egypt was 430 years. At the end of the 430 years, to the very day, all the LORD's divisions left Egypt.

The people leaving Egypt were not only escaping Pharaoh. They were learning how to remember the night God had brought them out. The LORD set rules for the Passover. No foreigner was to eat it. A slave bought with money could eat it after he had been circumcised. A temporary resident or hired worker could not eat it. The lamb was to be eaten inside a house. None of the meat was to be taken outside. No bone of it was to be broken. The whole community of Israel had to take part. A foreigner living among them who wanted to celebrate the LORD's Passover could do so after all the males in his household had been circumcised. Then he could take part as one born in the land. No uncircumcised person could eat it. The same law applied to the native-born and to the foreigner living among them.

The LORD also claimed Israel's firstborn as His own. Every firstborn, both people and animals, belonged to Him, because by strength of hand He had brought Israel out of Egypt, out of the house of slavery. The firstborn of a donkey could be redeemed with a lamb, or its neck was to be broken if it was not redeemed. Every firstborn son among the Israelites was to be redeemed. In days to come, when a son asked his father what this meant, the father was to tell him that the LORD brought Israel out of Egypt with a mighty hand. When Pharaoh stubbornly refused to let them go, the LORD killed every firstborn in Egypt, both people and animals. That was why Israel sacrificed the firstborn males of every womb to the LORD and redeemed their firstborn sons. The sign would be on Israel's hand and a symbol on Israel's forehead, because the LORD had brought them out of Egypt with His mighty hand.

When Pharaoh let the people go, God did not lead them by the shorter road through the land of the Philistines. God said, "If the people face war, they might change their minds and return to Egypt." So God led the people around by the desert road toward the Red Sea. The Israelites left Egypt arrayed for battle.

Moses took the bones of Joseph with him. Joseph had made the sons of Israel swear an oath and had said, "God will surely attend to you, and then you must carry my bones with you from this place." The promise made in Genesis was moving again. Joseph's coffin was no longer staying behind in Egypt.

From Succoth the Israelites traveled to Etham, at the edge of the desert. By day the LORD went ahead of them in a pillar of cloud to guide them on their way, and by night in a pillar of fire to give them light, so they could travel by day or night. Neither pillar left its place in front of the people.

Then the LORD told Moses to have the Israelites turn back and camp near Pi Hahiroth, between Migdol and the sea, opposite Baal Zephon. Pharaoh would think the Israelites were wandering in confusion and trapped by the desert. God would harden Pharaoh's heart, and Pharaoh would pursue them. But God would gain glory through Pharaoh and his army, and the Egyptians would know that He is the LORD.

The king of Egypt was told that the people had fled. Pharaoh and his officials changed their minds about them. They asked what they had done in letting the Israelites go from serving them. Pharaoh had his chariot made ready and took his army with him. He took six hundred of the best chariots, along with all the other chariots of Egypt, with officers over all of them. The LORD hardened the heart of Pharaoh, and he pursued the Israelites, who were marching out boldly.

The Egyptians pursued Israel and overtook them as they camped by the sea. When Pharaoh approached, the Israelites looked up. There were the Egyptians marching toward them. The sea was in front of them. Pharaoh's army was behind them. They were terrified and cried out to the LORD.

They turned on Moses. Had he taken them out to die in the desert because there were no graves in Egypt? What had he done to them by bringing them out of Egypt? Had they not told him in Egypt to leave them alone and let them serve the Egyptians? It would have been better for them to serve Egypt than to die in the desert.

Moses answered the people, "Do not be afraid. Stand firm and you will see the LORD's salvation, which He will accomplish for you today; for the Egyptians you see today, you will never see again. The LORD will fight for you; you need only to be still."

The LORD told Moses to stop crying out to Him and tell the Israelites to move on. Moses was to raise his staff and stretch out his hand over the sea to divide the water, so the Israelites could go through the sea on dry ground. The Egyptians would follow them into the sea, and God would gain glory through Pharaoh and his whole army, through his chariots and his horsemen. The Egyptians would know that He is the LORD.

The angel of God, who had been traveling in front of Israel's army, withdrew and went behind them. The pillar of cloud also moved from in front of them and stood behind them, coming between the armies of Egypt and Israel. Throughout the night the cloud brought darkness to the one side and light to the other side, so neither went near the other all night long.

Then Moses stretched out his hand over the sea, "and all that night the LORD drove back the sea with a strong east wind that turned it into dry land. So the waters were divided," and the Israelites went through the sea on dry ground, with a wall of water on their right and on their left.

The Egyptians pursued them. All Pharaoh's horses and chariots and horsemen followed them into the middle of the sea.

During the last watch of the night, the LORD looked down from the pillar of fire and cloud at the Egyptian army and threw it into confusion. He jammed the wheels of the chariots so they had difficulty driving. The Egyptians said they would get away from the Israelites, because the LORD was fighting for Israel against Egypt.

Then the LORD told Moses to stretch out his hand over the sea so the waters would flow back over the Egyptians and their chariots and horsemen. Moses stretched out his hand over the sea, and at daybreak the sea returned to its normal state. The Egyptians tried to flee toward it, but the LORD swept them into the middle of the sea. "The waters flowed back and covered the chariots and horsemen, the entire army of Pharaoh that had chased the Israelites into the sea. Not one of them survived."

But the Israelites went through the sea on dry ground, with a wall of water on their right and on their left. That day the LORD saved Israel from the hand of the Egyptians, and Israel saw the Egyptians lying dead on the shore. "When Israel saw the great power that the LORD had exercised over the Egyptians, the people feared the LORD and believed in Him and in His servant Moses."

Then Moses and the Israelites sang to the LORD. "The LORD is my strength and my song, and He has become my salvation. He is my God, and I will praise Him, my father's God, and I will exalt Him." They sang that the LORD is a warrior. The LORD is His name. Pharaoh's chariots and his army He had thrown into the sea. The best of Pharaoh's officers were drowned in the Red Sea. The deep waters had covered them, and they sank to the depths like a stone. God's right hand was majestic in power. God's right hand had shattered the enemy.

Miriam the prophet, Aaron's sister, took a timbrel in her hand. All the women followed her, with timbrels and dancing. Miriam sang to them: "Sing to the LORD, for He is highly exalted; the horse and rider He has thrown into the sea."

After the song, Moses led Israel from the Red Sea into the Desert of Shur. For three days they traveled in the desert without finding water. When they came to Marah, they could not drink its water because it was bitter. That was why the place was called Marah. The people grumbled against Moses, asking what they were to drink.

Moses cried out to the LORD, and the LORD showed him a log. Moses threw it into the water, and the water became fit to drink. There the LORD gave Israel a statute and an ordinance, and there He tested them. He told them that if they listened carefully to His voice and did what was right in His eyes, if they paid attention to His commands and kept all His decrees, He would not bring on them any of the diseases He had brought on the Egyptians, for He is the LORD who heals them.

Then they came to Elim, where there were twelve springs and seventy palm trees. Israel camped there near the water.

The people who had once made bricks under Pharaoh's drivers were now camped by springs in the wilderness. Behind them lay Egypt, the dead army on the shore, and the night when God passed through the land. In front of them stood a desert, and over them still stood the cloud and fire of the LORD.
