Deuteronomy Episode 6: Firstfruits, Two Mountains, and the Choice Set Down
Deuteronomy 26 to 29, Berean Standard Bible

The long list of cases closed, and Moses turned from laws to ceremonies, from what Israel must do every day to what Israel must say out loud. Chapter 26 gave every farmer a speech.

"When you enter the land that the LORD your God is giving you as an inheritance, and you take possession of it and settle in it," the first produce of the soil was to go into a basket and up to the chosen place. The farmer handed it to the priest with a one-line declaration: "I declare today to the LORD your God that I have entered the land that the LORD swore to our fathers to give us." Then, with the basket set before the altar, he recited the whole story of his people in one paragraph, and Moses made every Israelite memorize the words generations before any of them farmed an acre.

"My father was a wandering Aramean, and he went down to Egypt few in number and lived there and became a great nation, mighty and numerous. But the Egyptians mistreated us and afflicted us, putting us to hard labor. So we called out to the LORD, the God of our fathers; and the LORD heard our voice and saw our affliction, toil, and oppression. Then the LORD brought us out of Egypt with a mighty hand and an outstretched arm, with great terror, signs, and wonders. And He brought us to this place and gave us this land, a land flowing with milk and honey. And now, behold, I have brought the firstfruits of the land that You, O LORD, have given me."

Jacob the wanderer, Egypt the furnace, the cry, the mighty hand, the land. A creed a man could say in under a minute, standing over his own harvest, tying his basket to Abraham. Then he bowed, and the rejoicing that followed the confession had its usual guest list written in: the farmer, the Levite, and the foreigner dwelling among them, rejoicing together "in all the good things the LORD your God has given to you and your household". History first, then gratitude, then the table shared.

The third year brought the second declaration, the tithe year, when the tenth stayed in the towns for the Levite, the foreigner, the fatherless, and the widow. The farmer who had given it stood before God and audited himself aloud: "I have removed from my house the sacred portion and have given it to the Levite, the foreigner, the fatherless, and the widow, according to all the commandments You have given me. I have not transgressed or forgotten Your commandments." Nothing eaten in mourning, nothing handled while unclean, nothing offered to the dead. And the declaration ended as a prayer: "Look down from Your holy habitation, from heaven, and bless Your people Israel and the land You have given us as You swore to our fathers".

Then Moses closed the chapter with an exchange of proclamations, the covenant spoken in both directions on the same day. "Today you have proclaimed that the LORD is your God and that you will walk in His ways, keep His statutes and commandments and ordinances, and listen to His voice. And today the LORD has proclaimed that you are His people and treasured possession as He promised". The promise attached to it was elevation: God would set Israel "high in praise and name and honor above all the nations He has made," a holy people, "as He has promised."

Chapter 27 moved the ceremony to the far side of the Jordan. "On the day you cross the Jordan into the land that the LORD your God is giving you," large stones were to be set up on Mount Ebal, coated with plaster, and written over with all the words of this law. Beside them, an altar, and the altar had a rule the stones did not: "You must not use any iron tool on them." Uncut stones only. On that altar, burnt offerings, and peace offerings eaten there in rejoicing, and the law on the plastered stones written, Moses insisted, distinctly, where a whole nation could read what it had agreed to. Then Moses and the priests called the camp to attention with a sentence that measured the day: "Be silent, O Israel, and listen! This day you have become the people of the LORD your God."

The tribes were then divided between two facing mountains. On Mount Gerizim, to bless the people: Simeon, Levi, Judah, Issachar, Joseph, and Benjamin. On Mount Ebal, to deliver the curse: Reuben, Gad, Asher, Zebulun, Dan, and Naphtali. Between them, the Levites were to proclaim the curses in a loud voice, and to every curse the whole people answered with one word: "Amen!"

The twelve curses ran like a roll of hidden sins dragged into the open. "Cursed is the man who makes a carved idol or molten image" and "sets it up in secret." Cursed, the man who dishonors his father or mother. Cursed, the man who moves his neighbor's boundary stone, the theft Moses had already forbidden in a whisper of a verse. Cursed, the man who lets a blind man wander in the road. Cursed, the man who withholds justice from the foreigner, the fatherless, or the widow. Then the curses from the forbidden beds, the father's wife, the animal, the sister, the mother-in-law. Cursed, the man who strikes down his neighbor in secret, and the man who accepts a bribe to kill an innocent person. Secret is the word that keeps returning: sins no court could catch, named aloud on a mountainside, with the entire nation answering for them. And the last curse covered all the rest: "Cursed is he who does not put the words of this law into practice." Amen, said everyone.

Chapter 28 then set the two futures down in full, and Moses built them as mirrors. The blessings came first, fourteen verses of abundance chasing a man down. "And all these blessings will come upon you and overtake you, if you will obey the voice of the LORD your God". Blessed in the city and blessed in the country. The fruit of the womb blessed, the produce of the land, the calves and the lambs. "Your basket and kneading bowl will be blessed. You will be blessed when you come in and blessed when you go out." Enemies would march out in one direction and flee in seven. God would decree blessing on the barns and on everything the hand touched, open the heavens, His storehouse, with rain in season, and make Israel "the head and not the tail", a lender to many nations that borrowed from none.

Then the mirror turned. "If, however, you do not obey the LORD your God by carefully following all His commandments and statutes I am giving you today, all these curses will come upon you and overtake you". The same basket, cursed. The same kneading bowl. Cursed coming in and cursed going out, the produce of land and womb cursed, enemies met in one direction and fled from in seven. And where the blessings had stopped at abundance, the curses kept going down past the mirror's edge, into plague that clings, wasting disease and fever, blight and mildew, until the weather itself became an enemy: "The sky over your head will be bronze, and the earth beneath you iron". The rain of the land would turn to dust and powder, falling until the people were destroyed.

The middle of the chapter is a catalogue of futility, and it is built from the very mercies of the earlier laws coming back reversed. In chapter 20 the army had sent home the man with an uninhabited house, an untasted vineyard, an unmarried bride. In chapter 28 those same men stay, and watch. "You will be pledged in marriage to a woman, but another man will violate her. You will build a house but will not live in it. You will plant a vineyard but will not enjoy its fruit." The ox slaughtered before its owner's eyes, the donkey taken and not returned, sons and daughters given to another nation while a father's eyes wore out watching for them, day after day, "with no power in your hand." The foreigner inside the gates, the man the law kept feeding, would rise higher and higher while Israel sank lower and lower, until he was the head and they were the tail and the lending ran the wrong way.

Under all of it Moses laid one charge, and it was not cruelty or impurity. "Because you did not serve the LORD your God with joy and gladness of heart in all your abundance," the people would serve enemies instead, in famine, thirst, nakedness, and destitution, an iron yoke on the neck. Then the siege, described with a plainness that needs no decoration: a nation from the ends of the earth swooping like an eagle, its language not understood, no respect for the old and no pity for the young, the walls trusted in fallen, and inside them hunger so complete that Moses described, in the gentlest and most refined men and women of the nation, the last horror of a besieged city eating its own children in secret. The text does not look away, and neither does the chapter.

The end of it was exile, and exile described as the anti-Exodus. The people as numerous as the stars would be left few in number. "Then the LORD will scatter you among all the nations, from one end of the earth to the other," to serve gods of wood and stone there, and among those nations find "no repose, not even a resting place for the sole of your foot", only "a trembling heart, failing eyes, and a despairing soul." Life hanging in doubt, fear night and day, morning wishing for evening and evening wishing for morning. And the final curse undid the first rescue of the whole story: "The LORD will return you to Egypt in ships by a route that I said you should never see again. There you will sell yourselves to your enemies as male and female slaves, but no one will buy you." Slavery again, and this time not even a buyer.

Chapter 29 gathered the camp a third time and made the covenant again, "in addition to the covenant He had made with them at Horeb." Moses began with what they had seen: the trials in Egypt, the signs and wonders, forty years in the wilderness in clothes and sandals that did not wear out, fed without bread and without wine. And then he said the strange, humbling sentence at the center of the chapter: "Yet to this day the LORD has not given you a mind to understand, eyes to see, or ears to hear." They had watched everything and understood, so far, only some of it. Seeing was not the same as perceiving, and Moses said so to their faces on the last day of the speeches.

The covenant roll was read by rank and then past rank: leaders of tribes, elders, officials, all the men of Israel, "your children and wives, and the foreigners in your camps who cut your wood and draw your water", everyone standing to enter the oath. And the covenant's reach went beyond the standing crowd. "I am making this covenant and this oath not only with you, but also with those who are standing here with us today in the presence of the LORD our God, as well as with those who are not here today." Unborn generations were sworn in on the plains of Moab.

Against that oath Moses set one man, the one he most feared. Somewhere in the crowd might stand a man or woman, a clan or tribe, whose heart was already turning to the gods of the nations they had passed, "no root among you that bears such poisonous and bitter fruit". His tell was what he would say to himself while the curses were being read: "I will have peace, even though I walk in the stubbornness of my own heart." A private exemption claimed in the middle of a public oath. For that man, Moses said, the LORD would not be willing to forgive; every curse written in the book would fall on him, his name blotted out from under heaven, singled out for disaster.

And Moses projected the scene forward, past the disaster, to the day when Israel's own sons and a foreigner from a distant land would stand in a burned country, soil a waste of sulfur and salt like Sodom and Gomorrah, and the nations would ask their question: "Why has the LORD done such a thing to this land? Why this great outburst of anger?" The answer, Moses said, would be common knowledge by then: "It is because they abandoned the covenant of the LORD, the God of their fathers, which He made with them when He brought them out of the land of Egypt." Uprooted in anger, cast into another land, "where they are today", as if the future exile were already a dateline.

The chapter, and with it the covenant in Moab, closed on a distinction instead of a threat. "The secret things belong to the LORD our God, but the things revealed belong to us and to our children forever, so that we may follow all the words of this law." What God had kept hidden was His. What He had spoken was theirs, and it was enough to live on. What Moses said next would begin with the disaster already assumed, and the way back from it.
