Exodus Episode 2: Let My People Go
Exodus 5 to 10, Berean Standard Bible

The elders believed. Moses had come back to Egypt with Aaron beside him, a staff in his hand, and a word from the mountain of God. Now that word had to enter Pharaoh's court.

Moses and Aaron went to Pharaoh and said, "This is what the LORD, the God of Israel, says: 'Let My people go, so that they may hold a feast to Me in the wilderness.'"

Pharaoh answered, "Who is the LORD that I should obey His voice and let Israel go? I do not know the LORD, and I will not let Israel go."

That question would hang over everything that followed. Pharaoh did not know the LORD. Egypt was about to learn His name by signs, plagues, and a land brought low.

Moses and Aaron told Pharaoh that the God of the Hebrews had met with them. They asked to go three days into the wilderness and sacrifice to the LORD their God, or He might strike them with plague or with the sword. The king of Egypt called them trouble for the labor force. Why were Moses and Aaron taking the people away from their work?

Then Pharaoh made the work harder.

That same day he commanded the slave drivers and the Israelite foremen. They were no longer to supply the people with straw for making bricks as they had done before. The Israelites had to go and gather straw for themselves. But the quota of bricks stayed the same. Pharaoh said the Israelites were idle, and that was why they kept crying out to go and sacrifice to their God. "Make the work harder on the men so they will be occupied and pay no attention to these lies."

The people scattered through Egypt to gather stubble for straw. The slave drivers pressed them to finish the daily work as when straw was given. The Israelite foremen, whom Pharaoh's slave drivers had set over the people, were beaten and asked why they had not met the brick tally either yesterday or today.

The foremen went to Pharaoh and cried out. Why was he treating his servants this way? No straw was given to them, yet they were told to make bricks. Their servants were beaten, but the fault belonged to Pharaoh's own people. Pharaoh called them idle again. Idle. That was why they wanted to go and sacrifice to the LORD. They were to get back to work. No straw would be given, and they would still deliver the full number of bricks.

As the foremen came out from Pharaoh, they met Moses and Aaron waiting to speak with them. The men turned the weight of the day onto the brothers. "May the LORD look upon you and judge you," they said, "for you have made us a stench before Pharaoh and his officials; you have placed in their hand a sword to kill us!"

Moses returned to the LORD with the failure in his hands. Pharaoh had brought more trouble on the people since Moses had gone to speak in God's name. Moses said, "You have not delivered Your people in any way."

The LORD answered Moses with the future Pharaoh had not yet seen. "Now you will see what I will do to Pharaoh, for because of My mighty hand he will let the people go; because of My strong hand he will drive them out of his land."

God spoke His name again. He was the LORD. He had appeared to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob as God Almighty, but He had not made Himself known to them by His name the LORD in the way He was now doing with Moses. He had established His covenant with them to give them the land of Canaan, the land where they lived as foreigners. He had heard the groaning of the Israelites, whom the Egyptians kept in slavery. He had remembered His covenant.

Moses was to tell Israel: "I am the LORD, and I will bring you out from under the yoke of the Egyptians and deliver you from their bondage. I will redeem you with an outstretched arm and with mighty acts of judgment. I will take you as My own people, and I will be your God."

God also promised to bring them into the land He had sworn to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and to give it to them as a possession.

Moses spoke those words to the Israelites, but they did not listen to him. Their spirits were broken, and their slavery was cruel.

The LORD told Moses to go and tell Pharaoh, king of Egypt, to let the Israelites leave his land. Moses answered that the Israelites had not listened to him. How would Pharaoh listen to him, Moses, who was faltering in speech? Then the text slows down and names the family through whom the command would move. Amram married Jochebed, and she bore Aaron and Moses. Aaron married Elisheba, and she bore Nadab, Abihu, Eleazar, and Ithamar. It was this Aaron and Moses to whom the LORD said to bring the Israelites out of Egypt by their divisions.

When Moses and Aaron went back to Pharaoh, Moses was eighty years old and Aaron was eighty-three.

God made the terms plain to Moses. He had made Moses like God to Pharaoh, and Aaron would be his prophet. Pharaoh's heart would be hardened. God would multiply His signs and wonders in Egypt, and Pharaoh would not listen until God laid His hand on Egypt and brought Israel out with mighty acts of judgment.

First came the sign in court.

Moses and Aaron went to Pharaoh and did as the LORD commanded. "Aaron threw his staff down before Pharaoh and his officials, and it became a serpent." Pharaoh called the wise men and sorcerers. The Egyptian magicians did the same by their secret arts. Each man threw down his staff, and the staffs became serpents. But Aaron's staff swallowed up their staffs. Pharaoh's heart was hardened anyway, and he would not listen, just as the LORD had said.

The next morning Moses met Pharaoh at the water. Aaron raised the staff and struck the water of the Nile in the presence of Pharaoh and his officials, "and all the water was turned to blood." The fish in the Nile died. The river stank, and the Egyptians could not drink its water. Blood was throughout the land of Egypt, in rivers, canals, ponds, and pools. The Egyptian magicians did the same by their secret arts, and Pharaoh's heart was hardened. He turned and went into his palace. The Egyptians dug along the Nile for water to drink, because they could not drink the river water. Seven full days passed after the LORD struck the Nile.

Then the LORD sent frogs.

Aaron stretched out his hand over the waters of Egypt, and the frogs came up and covered the land. They entered Pharaoh's palace, his bedroom, and his bed. They entered the houses of his officials and his people, the ovens and kneading bowls. The magicians also made frogs come up on the land by their secret arts.

This time Pharaoh called Moses and Aaron. He asked them to pray to the LORD to take the frogs away from him and his people, and he said he would let the people go to offer sacrifices to the LORD. Moses let Pharaoh set the time. Pharaoh said tomorrow. Moses said it would be as Pharaoh said, so that he would know there was no one like the LORD their God. The frogs would leave the houses, courtyards, and fields. Only the frogs in the Nile would remain.

Moses cried out to the LORD about the frogs He had brought on Pharaoh. The frogs died in the houses, courtyards, and fields. The Egyptians gathered them into piles, and the land stank. When Pharaoh saw there was relief, he hardened his heart and would not listen.

The LORD told Moses to tell Aaron to stretch out his staff and strike the dust of the ground, and it became gnats throughout Egypt. Gnats were on people and animals. The magicians tried by their secret arts to produce gnats, but they could not. They told Pharaoh, "This is the finger of God." Pharaoh's heart was hardened, and he would not listen.

Then came flies.

The LORD set Goshen apart. "But on that day I will give special treatment to the land of Goshen, where My people live; no swarms of flies will be found there." Thick swarms entered Pharaoh's palace and the houses of his officials. Throughout Egypt the land was ruined by the swarms. Pharaoh called Moses and Aaron and told them to go and sacrifice to their God within the land. Moses said that would not be right. The sacrifices Israel offered would be detestable to the Egyptians, and if the Egyptians saw them, they might stone Israel. Israel had to go three days into the wilderness and sacrifice to the LORD their God as He commanded.

Pharaoh said he would let them go to sacrifice in the wilderness, but only if they did not go very far away. He told Moses to pray for him. Moses prayed, and the LORD removed the swarms from Pharaoh, from his officials, and from his people. Not one remained. But Pharaoh hardened his heart this time also and would not let the people go.

Next the LORD struck Egypt's livestock in the field: horses, donkeys, camels, cattle, sheep, and goats. The next day the LORD did it. "All the livestock of the Egyptians died, but not one animal belonging to the Israelites died." Pharaoh sent men to investigate and saw that not one of Israel's animals had died. Yet Pharaoh's heart was unyielding, and he would not let the people go.

Then Moses took handfuls of soot from a furnace and threw it into the air in Pharaoh's sight. It became fine dust over all the land of Egypt and caused festering boils to break out on people and animals. The magicians could not stand before Moses because of the boils. The boils were on the magicians and on all the Egyptians. The LORD hardened Pharaoh's heart, and he would not listen, just as the LORD had said to Moses.

Before the next plague, Moses carried a warning. Early in the morning he stood before Pharaoh and told him to let God's people go. This time God could have struck Pharaoh and his people with a plague that would wipe them off the earth. Instead, for this reason God had raised Pharaoh up: to show him His power, and so that God's name might be proclaimed in all the earth. At this time tomorrow, God would send the worst hailstorm that had ever fallen on Egypt, from the day it was founded until now.

Moses told Pharaoh's officials and people to bring their servants and livestock in from the field. Whoever remained in the field would die when the hail fell. Some of Pharaoh's officials feared the word of the LORD and hurried to bring their servants and livestock to shelter. Those who ignored the word of the LORD left their servants and livestock in the field.

Moses stretched out his staff toward the sky. The LORD sent thunder and hail, and lightning flashed down to the ground. The hail struck down everything in the fields, people and animals alike, and it beat down every plant and stripped every tree. Only in Goshen, where the Israelites were, was there no hail.

Pharaoh summoned Moses and Aaron. "This time I have sinned," he said. "The LORD is righteous, and I and my people are wicked." He asked them to pray. There had been enough thunder and hail. He would let the people go. They would stay no longer.

Moses told Pharaoh that as soon as he left the city, he would spread out his hands in prayer to the LORD. The thunder would stop and there would be no more hail, so Pharaoh might know that the earth is the LORD's. Still, Moses said, he knew Pharaoh and his officials did not yet fear the LORD God.

Moses left Pharaoh and stretched out his hands to the LORD. The thunder and hail stopped, and the rain no longer poured down on the land. When Pharaoh saw that the rain, hail, and thunder had stopped, he sinned again. He and his officials hardened their hearts. Pharaoh would not let the Israelites go, just as the LORD had said through Moses.

Pharaoh's own officials finally spoke against him. They asked, "How long will this man be a snare to us? Let the people go, so that they may worship the LORD their God. Do you not yet realize that Egypt lies in ruins?"

Moses and Aaron were brought back to Pharaoh. Pharaoh told them to go and worship the LORD their God, then asked who would be going. Moses said Israel would go with young and old, with sons and daughters, with flocks and herds, because they were to celebrate a festival to the LORD. Pharaoh said the LORD would be with them if he ever let them go with their women and children. He could see that they were bent on evil. "No, only the men may go and worship the LORD, since that is what you have been requesting." Moses and Aaron were driven from Pharaoh's presence.

Then Moses stretched out his staff over Egypt, and the LORD made an east wind blow across the land all that day and all that night. By morning the wind had brought locusts. They invaded all Egypt and settled down in every area of the country. The ground was black with them. They devoured everything growing in the fields and all the fruit on the trees that the hail had left. Nothing green remained on tree or plant in all the land of Egypt.

Pharaoh quickly summoned Moses and Aaron. He said he had sinned against the LORD their God and against them. He asked them to forgive his sin once more and pray that the LORD would take this deadly plague away. Moses left Pharaoh and prayed to the LORD. The LORD changed the wind to a very strong west wind, which caught up the locusts and carried them into the Red Sea. Not a locust was left anywhere in Egypt. But the LORD hardened Pharaoh's heart, and he did not let the Israelites go.

Then darkness came over Egypt, darkness that could be felt. Moses stretched out his hand toward the sky, and total darkness covered all Egypt for three days. "No one could see anyone else, and for three days no one left his place. Yet all the Israelites had light in their dwellings."

Pharaoh summoned Moses and told him to go and worship the LORD. Even the women and children could go. Only the flocks and herds had to stay behind. Moses said Israel's livestock had to go with them too, because from them they had to take animals for sacrifices and burnt offerings to the LORD their God. Their animals would go with them. Not a hoof would be left behind.

The LORD hardened Pharaoh's heart, and he was not willing to let them go.

Pharaoh said to Moses, "Depart from me! Make sure you never see my face again, for on the day you see my face, you will die."

Moses answered, "As you say, I will never see your face again."

Pharaoh had asked at the beginning who the LORD was. By the time Moses left his presence, Egypt had been struck in its river, its homes, its dust, its air, its fields, its sky, and its light. The question had been answered in judgment after judgment. Still Pharaoh would not bow.
