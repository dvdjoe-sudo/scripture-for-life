1 Samuel Episode 4: The Kingdom Renewed, and the First Crack
1 Samuel 11 to 13, Berean Standard Bible

The test came from across the Jordan, from Ammon. Nahash the Ammonite besieged Jabesh-gilead, an Israelite town in the hills east of the river, and Jabesh offered to surrender and serve him, and Nahash named his price with the cruelty of a man composing an insult to a whole nation: "I will make a treaty with you on one condition, that I may put out everyone’s right eye and bring reproach upon all Israel." Half-blind every fighting man in Jabesh, and let the scar preach Ammon's victory forever. The elders begged seven days to send messengers through Israel, in case a deliverer existed, and Nahash, certain none did, let them. The messengers reached Gibeah, Saul's town, and the people wept, and Saul came in from the field behind his oxen, still a farmer, asking why everyone was crying. They told him. And the Spirit of God rushed on him as it had at Gibeah among the prophets, and he burned with anger, and the new king did something that belonged to the judges' era: he cut a pair of oxen into pieces and sent the pieces through Israel with a threat, march out behind Saul and Samuel or this happens to your oxen. The terror of the LORD fell on the people, and they came out as one man, three hundred thirty thousand of them at Bezek.

Saul's message to Jabesh deserves remembering for its plainness: deliverance will be yours tomorrow by the time the sun is hot. Jabesh lied beautifully to buy the night, telling Nahash they would come out in the morning to surrender, and at dawn Saul's army came through the Ammonite camp in three divisions instead, and slaughtered them until the heat of the day, until the survivors were so scattered that "no two of them were left together". Then the nation, drunk on its first royal victory, turned on the men who had sneered at Gibeah and demanded them for execution: "Who said that Saul should not reign over us? Bring those men here so we can kill them!" And here the book gives Saul his finest sentence, the high-water mark of the man, and it is worth weighing because everything later is measured against it: "No one shall be put to death this day, for today the LORD has worked salvation in Israel." The victory was God's, so the vengeance was nobody's. The king who had hidden among the baggage refused a purge and credited heaven. Samuel seized the moment and marched everyone to Gilgal, where the kingship was renewed with sacrifices and great joy, Saul publicly confirmed at last, the question of the worthless men answered by a battlefield instead of a scaffold.

Then Samuel stepped down, and his farewell address at Gilgal is a courtroom and a history lesson and a father's warning folded into one speech. He began with his own audit, an old man inviting prosecution: "Here I am. Bear witness against me before the LORD and before His anointed: Whose ox or donkey have I taken? Whom have I cheated or oppressed? From whose hand have I accepted a bribe and closed my eyes? Tell me, and I will restore it to you." The nation cleared him in one voice: "You have not cheated us or oppressed us," they replied, "nor have you taken anything from the hand of man." Against that clean record Samuel recited theirs: Egypt, Moses and Aaron, the forgetting, the times he sold them into enemy hands, Sisera and the Philistines and Moab, the cries, and the deliverers God sent, Jerubbaal and Barak and Jephthah and Samuel himself, the whole of Judges summarized as one pattern of rescue. And then he attached the pattern to their present: "But when you saw that Nahash king of the Ammonites was moving against you, you said to me," and Samuel quoted their demand back at them, "No, we must have a king to rule over us", and added the rider the whole book insists on, that they had said it even though the LORD their God was their king. Nahash had been the trigger. They had asked for a king because an Ammonite was coming, and the Ammonite had just been routed, and the king they got for the asking was now standing beside the prophet who was warning them both. The terms going forward were plain and they were conditional: fear the LORD, serve Him, obey, and it will go well; rebel, and His hand will be against you as it was against your fathers.

To seal it, Samuel did the thing that separated prophecy from oratory. It was wheat harvest, the dry season, when rain does not come. "I will call on the LORD to send thunder and rain, so that you will know and see what a great evil you have committed in the sight of the LORD by asking for a king." The thunder rolled in off a harvest sky and the rain fell in the wrong month, and the people stood in it and confessed the exact sin the sign was about: "For we have added to all our sins the evil of asking for a king." And Samuel, who had every right to say I told you so, pastored them instead. Do not be afraid. Serve the LORD with all your heart anyway. Do not chase worthless things. And then the old man made a promise that defines him as much as any prophecy: "As for me, far be it from me that I should sin against the LORD by ceasing to pray for you. And I will continue to teach you the good and right way." He would pray for the people who had rejected his life's work, and teach them, and he closed with the appeal that outlasts every system: "consider what great things He has done for you." But the warning stayed attached, and it named the king as well as the nation: "But if you persist in doing evil, both you and your king will be swept away."

Time passed. Saul built a standing force of three thousand, two thousand with himself at Michmash and a thousand under his son Jonathan at Gibeah. Jonathan struck the first blow of the reign, attacking the Philistine outpost at Geba, and the war Israel had wanted a king for arrived at full scale: chariots, six thousand horsemen, "and troops as numerous as the sand on the seashore", camped at Michmash. Israel's militia melted into the landscape, hiding in caves and thickets and cisterns, and some fled clear across the Jordan. Saul waited at Gilgal for Samuel, bound by the instruction given at his anointing: wait seven days for the prophet to come and offer the sacrifices and give the word. He waited the seven days. Samuel did not come within them, and the troops began to desert, and the Philistine army sat in the pass above him, and Saul did the math of a man watching his command dissolve. He called for the offerings. He offered the burnt offering himself, king doing priest's work, seizing with his own hands the favor he was supposed to wait to receive. "Just as he finished offering the burnt offering, Samuel arrived, and Saul went out to greet him."

His defense was a list of true facts arranged as an excuse. The troops were deserting. Samuel had not come at the appointed time. The Philistines were gathering. And his reasoning, quoted in the text as his very thought, was this: "Now the Philistines will descend upon me at Gilgal, and I have not sought the favor of the LORD." So I felt compelled, he said. I forced myself. Samuel's answer stripped the facts of their arrangement and named the act: "You have acted foolishly," Samuel declared. "You have not kept the command that the LORD your God gave you; if you had, the LORD would have established your kingdom over Israel for all time. But now your kingdom will not endure; the LORD has sought a man after His own heart and appointed him ruler over His people, because you have not kept the command of the LORD." A man after His own heart. The sentence is the first shadow of David, cast across Saul's reign while David is still an unknown shepherd boy years from Bethlehem's fields. Note what the crack was. It was not a lost battle or a moral scandal. It was the king reaching for the priest's office to save his position, taking, with liturgy, the one thing the warning in chapter 8 said kings take. Samuel went up to Gibeah and left him, and Saul counted what remained of the army that had numbered in the hundreds of thousands: about six hundred men. And the Philistines held the forges of the whole country, so that Israelite farmers sharpened their plowshares at Philistine smithies for a fee, and on the day of battle there was not a sword or a spear in Israel's ranks to be found, except two. Saul had one. Jonathan had the other.
