2 Samuel Episode 7: Amnon and Absalom
2 Samuel 13 to 14, Berean Standard Bible

Nathan had said the sword would never depart from David's house, and the first stroke of it fell inside the family, in a story the narrator tells with unbearable exactness. Amnon, David's firstborn, fell sick with desire for his half-sister Tamar, the full sister of Absalom, a virgin of the king's household, and he had a friend, Jonadab, the king's nephew, a very shrewd man, who read his cousin's misery in a morning and happily supplied the engineering for it. Feign illness, Jonadab advised. When the king comes to see you, ask for Tamar to come and make cakes in your sight, so that you may eat from her hand. David, who had never learned to suspect his own house, sent the order himself: Tamar was to go to her brother's house and prepare a meal for him. She came and kneaded dough and made cakes in his sight and baked them, a sister's ordinary kindness performed in full view of the trap, and when she set the pan before him he refused to eat, and cleared the room. Then he told her to bring the food into the bedroom, and when she did, he seized her and demanded that she lie with him.

Tamar's answer is one of the clearest moral speeches in the book, and it deserves to stand in her own words. She refused him to his face: "No, my brother!" And then the law and the shame of it, in her own words: "Do not violate me, for such a thing should never be done in Israel. Do not do this disgraceful thing!" She argued shame, his and hers: where could she ever take her shame, and he would be counted among the fools in Israel. She even argued the desperate legal fiction available to her, that he should speak to the king, who would not withhold her from him. It was a better claim than the law allowed and it would not have saved her, but it shows a mind working at full speed for any exit. Amnon refused to listen to her, and being stronger than she was, he violated her. And then the verse that has chilled readers ever since, because it exposes what the desire had actually been: "Then Amnon hated Tamar with such intensity that his hatred was greater than the love he previously had." The love had never been love. He ordered her out, and when she protested that sending her away was a greater wrong than the wrong already done, a dismissal that branded her as the guilty party and closed every door of redress, he had his attendant throw her out and bolt the door behind her. Tamar was wearing the robe of many colors that marked the king's virgin daughters. She put ashes on her head, tore the robe, and went away crying aloud with her hand on her head, grief performed in the street because the law had been bolted inside.

Her brother Absalom found her and read the whole story at a glance, and his response was the coldest sentence in the chapter: be quiet for now, my sister; he is your brother; do not take this thing to heart. She obeyed, and lived as a desolate woman in Absalom's house, and the text will remember her name one more time, years later, when Absalom names his own beautiful daughter Tamar. King David heard all of it and was furious. The narrator records the fury and then, devastatingly, nothing after it: no trial, no punishment, no word. The king who had once exploded in righteous anger at Nathan's story of a stolen lamb did not summon Amnon, and the house of David learned what the king's silence licensed. Absalom learned it best. He never said a word to Amnon, either good or bad, because he hated him for violating his sister Tamar, and he waited two full years in silence while his father let the matter lie.

The blow fell at a sheepshearing feast at Baal-hazor, the same festive cover his father-by-crime had used for Uriah's war. Absalom invited all the king's sons, pressed a reluctant David to release Amnon, and ordered his servants to watch until Amnon's heart was merry with wine, and then to strike him down, and not to be afraid, because the command was his. They did as ordered, and every other prince fled on his mule, and the report that ran ahead of them to Jerusalem exaggerated as bad news does: Absalom had struck down all the sons of the king. David tore his clothes and lay on the ground, and it was Jonadab, the engineer of the original crime, who stepped forward with the correction, delivered with a shrewd man's perfect calm: only Amnon was dead, and Absalom had planned it since the day Amnon violated his sister. The other princes arrived wailing, and the court wept bitterly, and Absalom was gone, fled to his grandfather Talmai, king of Geshur, his mother's people, where he stayed three years. David mourned for his son every day, and in time he was consoled over Amnon's death and longed to go to Absalom. Absalom had done what David's justice had declined to do, and David's heart went after the murderer, which was also true.

What broke the exile was theater, arranged by Joab, who perceived that the king's heart longed for Absalom and decided, as ever, to manage his king. He fetched a wise woman from Tekoa and put words in her mouth: a widow, two sons, one dead by the other's hand in a field with no one to separate them, and now the clan demanded the surviving son's life for blood, which would extinguish her one remaining ember and cut off her husband's name from the earth. David rose to the case exactly as he had risen to Nathan's, and faster, swearing by the living LORD that not a hair of her son's head would fall to the ground. Then the woman turned the parable on its maker, as Nathan had taught this court that parables can turn: why had the king devised such a thing against the people of God, convicting himself by his own verdict, since he had not brought back his own banished son? Her ground was theology, and it is one of the great sentences in the book: "For we will surely die and be like water poured out on the ground, which cannot be recovered. Yet God does not take away a life, but He devises ways that the banished one may not be cast out from Him." David saw through the production in a single question, was Joab's hand behind this, and when she confessed it, he granted the request. Go, bring back the young man Absalom.

Bringing him back was where the reconciliation failed. Absalom returned to Jerusalem and to his own house, but the king's condition stood: he must not see my face. So the most praised man in Israel lived in the capital two full years in a restoration that restored nothing, father of three sons and of a daughter he named Tamar, famous for a beauty the narrator describes without a single flaw from sole to crown, famous also for the annual shearing of his hair, weighed out at two hundred shekels by the royal standard, a head of hair that this book wants you to remember. Shut out, Absalom sent for Joab twice and was twice refused, and then he did the arithmetic of a man who had learned in his father's house that only escalation gets an audience: he had his servants set Joab's barley field on fire. Joab came at once, demanding an explanation, and got the exile's whole case in one question: why had he come back from Geshur at all, when it would have been better to remain there? Let me see the king's face, Absalom said, and if there is iniquity in me, let him kill me. Joab carried it to David, and David summoned his son, and Absalom bowed facedown before the king, and the king kissed him. It reads like an ending. This book has taught you by now to count what is missing from its scenes of reunion: no confession was named, no crime was spoken, no dead were mentioned, and a kiss closed over two years of silence and a field of burned barley. Absalom walked out of his father's presence absolved of everything and reconciled to nothing, and he began, presently, to build a chariot.
