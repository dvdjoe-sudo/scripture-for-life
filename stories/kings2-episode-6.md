2 Kings Episode 6: The Shunammite's Land, and Hazael
2 Kings 8, Berean Standard Bible

The episode opens with a story told inside the story, and its timing is the point. Before a seven-year famine, Elisha had warned the Shunammite woman to leave the land: “Arise, you and your household; go and live as a foreigner wherever you can. For the LORD has decreed that a seven-year famine will come to the land.” She had obeyed, lived seven years among the Philistines, and came back to find her house and land in other hands, so she went to appeal to the king. The king, at that very hour, was asking Gehazi to relate all the great things Elisha had done, and Gehazi was in the middle of the story of the boy restored to life when the woman herself walked in to make her appeal. Gehazi's introduction is the book's own stage direction: “My lord the king, this is the woman, and this is the son Elisha restored to life.” The king questioned her, she confirmed the account, and he appointed an officer with a restitution order that went past the land itself: “Restore all that was hers, along with all the proceeds of the field from the day that she left the country until now.” Seven years of harvests she never planted, repaid. The woman who once asked for nothing and said she had a home among her own people received, in the king's court, the full value of the years she had spent as a foreigner for obeying a prophet's warning.

Then Elisha went to Damascus, into the capital of Israel's enemy, and the errand there ran the opposite direction. Ben-hadad, king of Aram, lay sick, and hearing the man of God had come, he sent Hazael, his courtier, with forty camel loads of gifts and one question: “Will I recover from this illness?” Elisha's answer was a riddle with a knife inside it: “You will surely recover.” “But the LORD has shown me that in fact he will die.” Then the prophet did something stranger than the riddle. He fixed his gaze on Hazael and held it until the courtier grew uncomfortable, and then the man of God wept. Hazael asked why, and Elisha told him what he saw coming, not for Ben-hadad but from Hazael himself: “Because I know the evil you will do to the Israelites,” Elisha replied. “You will set fire to their fortresses, kill their young men with the sword, dash their little ones to pieces, and rip open their pregnant women.” Hazael protested with the humility of a man who has not yet done it: “But how could your servant, a mere dog, do such a monstrous thing?” And Elisha answered: “The LORD has shown me that you will be king over Aram.” Hazael went back to his master and delivered the safe half of the message: “He told me that you would surely recover.” The next day he took a thick cloth, dipped it in water, spread it over the sleeping king's face, and took the throne.

The chapter's second half drops south to Judah, and the tone darkens into ledgers. Jehoram of Judah had married a daughter of Ahab, and he walked in the ways of the house of Ahab in Jerusalem. The book keeps one light burning through the record: “the LORD was unwilling to destroy Judah”, for David's sake, because of the promised lamp. Under Jehoram, Edom broke free of Judah and set up its own king, and Libnah followed. His son Ahaziah, whose mother was Athaliah, Omri's granddaughter, also walked in the ways of the house of Ahab, “for he was a son-in-law of the house of Ahab.” Ahaziah went north with Joram of Israel to fight Hazael at Ramoth-gilead, the new king of Aram already drawing Israelite blood, and Joram was wounded and carried back to Jezreel to recover. Ahaziah went down to Jezreel to visit him, an act of family loyalty between two kings of Ahab's house, and the book closes the chapter there, with both kings in one city and Elisha's anointing errand about to be sent. The stage is set so precisely because the next episode needs no setup at all.
