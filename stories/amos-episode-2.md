Amos Episode 2: Hear This Word
Amos 3-4, Berean Standard Bible

The second movement of the book opens with the address the first movement was circling toward: “Hear this word that the LORD has spoken against you, O children of Israel, against the whole family that I brought up out of the land of Egypt”. And the first sentence of the indictment is not about Israel's sins but about Israel's privilege, stated as the very grounds of the punishment: “Only you have I known from all the families of the earth; therefore I will punish you for all your iniquities.” Election, in Amos, is not a shield. It is an exposure. The one nation God had known out of all the families of the earth is the one nation held to account for everything, because it alone had been given the standard it broke. Then the prophet argues his own commission out of the way things work in the world, in a chain of cause and effect any farmer could follow. “Can two walk together without agreeing where to go?” A lion does not roar over nothing; a bird does not drop into a snare with no bait; a trap does not spring empty; a trumpet in a city means trouble; and “If calamity comes to a city, has not the LORD caused it?” The chain lands on two sentences that stand back to back as the book's charter: “Surely the Lord GOD does nothing without revealing His plan to His servants the prophets.” “The lion has roared”, and then the question, “who will not fear?” “The Lord GOD has spoken”, and then the question, “who will not prophesy?” Amos is not volunteering. He is the last link in the chain: the roar happened, so the shepherd speaks.

Then God does something a courtroom would recognize: He calls witnesses, and the witnesses He calls are Israel's enemies. The citadels of Ashdod and Egypt are invited to assemble on Samaria's mountains and look at what Israel does to its own: “Assemble on the mountains of Samaria; see the great unrest in the city and the acts of oppression in her midst.” The verdict on what the foreigners would see is one of the saddest lines in the book, because it is about competence at goodness itself: “For they know not how to do right,” declares the LORD. “They store up violence and destruction in their citadels.” The palaces are storehouses, and what is stored is violence. The sentence follows: an enemy will surround the land and plunder the strongholds. And the rescue, when it comes, is described in Amos's own trade, a shepherd's arithmetic of loss: “As the shepherd snatches from the mouth of the lion two legs or a piece of an ear, so the Israelites dwelling in Samaria will be rescued having just the corner of a bed or the cushion of a couch”. Proof of the kill, not a flock saved. A corner of a bed. A couch cushion. The summons ends at the sanctuary and the great houses together: at Bethel, “the horns of the altar will be cut off, and they will fall to the ground”, and in the cities, “the houses of ivory will also perish, and the great houses will come to an end”.

Chapter four opens with the book's most famous insult, aimed at the wealthy women of the capital: “Hear this word, you cows of Bashan on Mount Samaria, you women who oppress the poor and crush the needy, who say to your husbands”, and then their whole theology in four words, “Bring us more to drink.” Bashan was pasture country east of the Jordan, famous for fat cattle, and the comparison is an insult and an indictment in one: well-fed, self-indulgent, and driving the oppression personally, demanding to be kept in drink. The LORD has sworn by His holiness against them: “the days are coming when you will be taken away with hooks, and your posterity with fishhooks”. Carried out like fish, through the breaches in broken walls, each one straight ahead, cast out. Then, without pausing, the voice turns to the whole nation's worship and the irony runs like acid: “Go to Bethel and transgress; rebel even more at Gilgal! Bring your sacrifices every morning, your tithes every three days.” Offer the leavened bread, proclaim the freewill offerings loudly, “For that is what you children of Israel love to do”. Religion enthusiastically performed at the rival shrines, generosity announced, devotion on display, and every word of the invitation means the opposite: this is what your worship has become, a love of the performance, and God counts the performance itself as the transgression.

What follows is the book's saddest refrain, five sent disasters counted off like a case history, each one ending on the same four-beat line. First famine: “I afflicted all your cities with cleanness of teeth and all your towns with lack of bread, yet you did not return to Me,” declares the LORD. Clean teeth because there was nothing to eat with them. Then drought, rain mocked by its own selectivity, one city watered and its neighbor withered, people staggering from city to city for water and going unsatisfied, “yet you did not return to Me”. Then blight and mildew on the growing gardens: “I struck you with blight and mildew”, the locust took the fig and olive trees, “yet you did not return to Me”. Then plague and war, plagues like Egypt's, young men killed with the sword, the stench of the camp in their nostrils, “yet you did not return to Me”. Last, overthrow itself: “you were like a firebrand snatched from a blaze, yet you did not return to Me”. Some of them had been pulled out of burning like a stick out of a fire, Sodom's own escape, and even that did not turn them. The refrain is the point: every disaster had an address and a purpose, and the purpose failed, five times, in writing.

So the episode closes where the case history was always heading, on a meeting. “prepare to meet your God, O Israel!” Not prepare to meet an army, or a famine, or a conqueror. The disasters were couriers; the sender is coming Himself. And the God Israel is summoned to meet is then named in the book's first doxology, quoted whole because nothing shorter carries it: “He who forms the mountains, who creates the wind and reveals His thoughts to man, who turns the dawn to darkness and strides on the heights of the earth”, “the LORD, the God of Hosts, is His name”. The former of mountains and the maker of wind, the God who turns morning into darkness by walking the heights, has announced a meeting with a nation of altar-side pledge-seizers and bowl-drinking ease, and the shepherd from Tekoa is the one carrying the summons.
