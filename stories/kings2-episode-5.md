2 Kings Episode 5: The Siege of Samaria
2 Kings 6-7, Berean Standard Bible

Elisha's war ministry opened with a borrowed axe. The sons of the prophets were cutting timber at the Jordan for a larger dwelling when one man's iron axe head flew off into the water, and his cry was about the debt, not the tool: “it was borrowed!” Elisha cut a stick, threw it where the iron had sunk, and the iron floated, and the man took it out. Then the book scales the same gift up to armies. Aram was raiding Israel, and every ambush the king of Aram planned, Elisha reported to Israel's king in advance: “Be careful not to pass by this place, for the Arameans are going down there.” Aram's king, enraged, demanded to know which of his own servants was the traitor, and the answer named the real problem: “Elisha, the prophet in Israel, tells the king of Israel the very words you speak in your bedroom.” So a great army came by night to Dothan to capture one prophet. Elisha's servant went out at dawn, saw horses and chariots ringing the city, and cried: “Oh, my master, what are we to do?” Elisha answered: “Do not be afraid,” “for those who are with us are more than those who are with them.” Then he prayed, “O LORD, please open his eyes that he may see.” And the young man saw “the hills were full of horses and chariots of fire all around Elisha.” Elisha's second prayer struck the Arameans blind, and the prophet walked out and offered to lead them to the man they were seeking, and led them ten miles into Samaria itself before their eyes were opened. Israel's king, looking at an army delivered into his capital, asked twice: “My father, shall I kill them? Shall I kill them?” Elisha forbade it: “Would you kill those you have captured with your own sword or bow? Set food and water before them, that they may eat and drink and then return to their master.” The king feasted them and sent them home. “And the Aramean raiders did not come into the land of Israel again.”

Some time later the raids became a siege, and the story darkens past anything in the book so far. Ben-hadad brought his entire army against Samaria and shut it in until the famine priced a donkey's head at eighty shekels of silver and a quarter cab of dove's dung at five. As the king walked the wall, a woman cried out: “Help me, my lord the king!” His answer was a starving man's theology: “If the LORD does not help you, where can I find help for you? From the threshing floor or the winepress?” Then she told him her matter. Another woman had proposed eating her son one day and the other's the next: “Give up your son, that we may eat him, and tomorrow we will eat my son.” “So we boiled my son and ate him,” she said, and on the second day the other woman had hidden her child: “But she had hidden her son.” The king tore his clothes, and the people watching saw that under them, against his skin, he was already wearing sackcloth. His grief turned on the prophet: “May God punish me, and ever so severely, if the head of Elisha son of Shaphat remains on his shoulders through this day!” Elisha, sitting with the elders in his house, knew the executioner was coming before the messenger arrived: “Do you see how this murderer has sent someone to cut off my head?” Then the king himself arrived with the despair that had replaced his oath: “This calamity is from the LORD. Why should I wait for the LORD any longer?”

Elisha's answer was a price list for the next morning: “About this time tomorrow at the gate of Samaria, a seah of fine flour will sell for a shekel, and two seahs of barley will sell for a shekel.” The officer on whose arm the king leaned answered for every reasonable person in the city: “Look, even if the LORD were to make windows in heaven, could this really happen?” Elisha's reply fixed the man's place in the story: “You will see it with your own eyes,” “but you will not eat any of it.”

That night the deliverance was discovered by the four hungriest men in Samaria, lepers camped at the gate because the city could not take them in. Their reasoning was the siege's arithmetic stripped bare: “Why just sit here until we die?” In the city, famine; at the gate, death; in the Aramean camp, a chance: “If they let us live, we will live; if they kill us, we will die.” They went at twilight and found the camp empty, because the LORD had made the Arameans hear the sound of chariots and horses and a great army, and the whole force had fled believing “the king of Israel must have hired the kings of the Hittites and Egyptians to attack us.” The lepers ate and drank in one tent, carried off silver and gold and clothing and hid them, entered a second tent and did the same, and then conscience caught up with appetite: “We are not doing what is right. Today is a day of good news.” “If we are silent and wait until morning light, our sin will overtake us.” They called the news up to the gatekeepers: “We went to the Aramean camp and no one was there”, “only tethered horses and donkeys, and the tents were intact.”

The king suspected a trap and sent scouts, who followed a road littered with discarded clothing and equipment all the way to the Jordan. Then the city poured out and plundered the camp, and by the gate the prices Elisha had named were the prices in the market, a seah of fine flour for a shekel, two seahs of barley for a shekel, according to the word of the LORD. The officer on whose arm the king leaned had been put in charge of that gate. He saw it all, exactly as promised. “The people trampled him in the gateway, and he died.”
