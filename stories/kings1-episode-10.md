1 Kings Episode 10: Naboth's Vineyard and the Last Battle
1 Kings 21-22, Berean Standard Bible

Next to Ahab's palace at Jezreel was a vineyard, and the vineyard belonged to Naboth. Ahab wanted it for a vegetable garden, because it was close by, and he made the owner a fair offer by any commercial standard: a better vineyard in exchange, or the value in silver, whichever Naboth preferred. Naboth refused on grounds that were not commercial at all: “The LORD forbid that I should give you the inheritance of my fathers.” In Israel the land was not a commodity. It was the family's share of the gift of God, held in trust from the fathers for the sons, and a permanent sale of the inheritance was not Naboth's to make, whatever the price. It was a lawful refusal, even a holy one, and it was final. Ahab went home to his palace, the text says, in the same two words that had closed the Aramean war, sullen and angry, and he lay down on his bed and turned his face to the wall and refused to eat. The king who had faced armies and prophets and drought lay starving himself over a vegetable garden he could not buy. Jezebel came in and asked why he was so sullen he would not eat, and he told her, and her answer was the marriage in one line: “Do you not reign over Israel? Get up, eat some food, and be cheerful, for I will get you the vineyard of Naboth the Jezreelite.”

What Jezebel did next was done with a king's stationery. She wrote letters in Ahab's name and sealed them with his seal, to the elders and nobles of Naboth's own city, and the letters turned the machinery of Israel's religion and law into the murder weapon. “Proclaim a fast and give Naboth a seat of honor among the people.” A fast, a public act of mourning over some unnamed guilt in the community, with the intended victim seated in honor at its center. Then two scoundrels were to be seated opposite him and testify to the one charge that carried death by stoning and forfeiture: “You have cursed both God and the king!” The elders and nobles of Jezreel did exactly what the letters said. The fast was proclaimed. Naboth was honored. The scoundrels testified. He was taken outside the city and stoned to death, and the city fathers sent the queen a report of five words in the BSB's flat telling: “Naboth has been stoned to death.” Jezebel brought her husband the news as a gift: “Get up and take possession of the vineyard of Naboth the Jezreelite, who refused to give it to you for silver. For Naboth is no longer alive, but dead.” And Ahab got up from his bed at last, and went down to the vineyard to take possession of it. He had not written the letters, asked for the witnesses, or thrown a stone. He had only sulked, and let his wife interpret the sulk, and now he walked into the stolen field to claim it, and the man waiting for him in the rows of vines was Elijah. God had told the prophet where the king would be: in the vineyard, taking possession. “Have you not murdered a man and seized his land?” That was the whole indictment, both crimes in one breath, the murder Ahab had not ordered and the seizure he was committing at that moment fused into a single act with his name on it. And the sentence matched the crime scene to the ground it happened on: “In the place where the dogs licked up the blood of Naboth, there also the dogs will lick up your blood”. Ahab's greeting to the prophet in the vineyard is the truest thing he ever said to him: “So you have found me out, my enemy.” Elijah accepted the title and the charge behind it: “I have found you out because you have sold yourself to do evil in the sight of the LORD.” Then came the whole sentence, and it was the largest yet: Ahab's house would be consumed, every male cut off, and “I will make your house like that of Jeroboam son of Nebat and like that of Baasha son of Ahijah, because you have provoked My anger and caused Israel to sin.” Jeroboam's house and Baasha's house, the two exterminated dynasties, now had a third name added to their pattern. And Jezebel, who was not present, got her own sentence in her absence: “The dogs will devour Jezebel by the wall of Jezreel.” The narrator pauses the story to give the book's considered verdict on the man standing in the vineyard: “Surely there was never one like Ahab, who sold himself to do evil in the sight of the LORD, incited by his wife Jezebel.”

Then Ahab did the one thing no one in the book saw coming. He tore his clothes, put on sackcloth, fasted, lay down in sackcloth, and walked around meekly, the sulking king turned penitent in the dust. And God, who had just pronounced the largest sentence in the book, sent Elijah back with an amendment built entirely on that humility: “Have you seen how Ahab has humbled himself before Me? Because he has humbled himself before Me, I will not bring the calamity during his days, but I will bring it upon his house in the days of his son.” The worst king in the book bought his house one generation of delay with sackcloth. The dogs and the wall and the extermination stood, but they stood postponed, and Elijah, who had run from Ahab's wife and wept for death under a tree, was the messenger of the postponement too.

Three years passed without war, and in the third year Jehoshaphat king of Judah came down to Samaria on a state visit, and the two kingdoms, split since Rehoboam, acted as allies for the first time in the book. Ahab wanted Ramoth-gilead back from Aram, and Jehoshaphat pledged himself in full: “I am as you are, my people are your people, and my horses are your horses.” But Jehoshaphat, the good king of David's line, added one request: “Please inquire first for the word of the LORD.” So Ahab assembled his prophets, about four hundred of them, and asked whether to go up, and they answered with one voice that “the Lord will deliver it into the hand of the king”, and one of them, Zedekiah, had made himself iron horns to act the prophecy out. Jehoshaphat, listening to four hundred men say the same words in the same tone, asked the question that defines the whole scene: was there not still a prophet of the LORD here, of whom they could inquire? Ahab's answer is the most self-knowing sentence he ever speaks: “There is still one man through whom we can inquire of the LORD, but I hate him because he never prophesies anything good for me, but only bad. He is Micaiah son of Imlah.” They fetched him, and the messenger coached him on the way, all the prophets are favorable, be favorable. Micaiah's oath answered for him before he reached the throne: “As surely as the LORD lives, I will speak whatever the LORD tells me.” Brought before the two kings, he gave Ahab the court's answer back in the court's own words. “Go up and triumph,” Micaiah replied. And Ahab, who hated him for never prophesying good, heard the false good and knew it was false, and demanded the real thing in an outburst that exposes his whole relationship with prophecy: “How many times must I make you swear not to tell me anything but the truth in the name of the LORD?” He did not hate Micaiah's bad news. He hated that he could not trust the good news, and could not stop asking.

So Micaiah told him. He had seen all Israel scattered on the hills “like sheep without a shepherd”, and the LORD saying of the army, “These people have no master; let each one return home in peace.” Ahab turned to his ally with vindication curdled into complaint: “Did I not tell you that he never prophesies good for me, but only bad?” But Micaiah was not finished, and the second vision is the deepest and strangest thing in the book, told straight, exactly as the text tells it, with no explanation offered then or now. He had seen the LORD on His throne, and all the host of heaven standing by, and the LORD asking the council, “Who will entice Ahab to march up and fall at Ramoth-gilead?” And a spirit had come forward and offered to be a lying spirit in the mouths of all Ahab's prophets, and the LORD had sent him out to do it and prosper. “So you see, the LORD has put a lying spirit in the mouths of all these prophets of yours, and the LORD has pronounced disaster against you.” The four hundred who had spoken with one accord had, on Micaiah's account, one spirit between them, and it was sent. Zedekiah, of the iron horns, stepped up and struck Micaiah across the face in front of both kings, demanding, “Which way did the Spirit of the LORD go when He departed from me to speak with you?” Micaiah told him he would find out on the day he went to hide in an inner room. Ahab ordered the prophet imprisoned: “Put this man in prison and feed him only bread and water until I return safely.” And Micaiah, on his way out, staked everything on the condition in that sentence: “If you ever return safely, the LORD has not spoken through me.” Then he addressed the crowd, the kings, the court, the four hundred: “Take heed, all you people!”

Ahab went up anyway. He had heard the truth plainly, from the only prophet he trusted to tell it, and he went up, with one precaution that admitted everything: he would disguise himself in the battle, while Jehoshaphat wore his royal robes. The king of Aram had ordered his thirty-two chariot commanders to fight no one, small or great, except the king of Israel, and the commanders converged on the king in the robes, and Jehoshaphat cried out, and they saw he was the wrong king and turned away. Then the disguised king's luck ran out on the most ordinary event in war. “However, a certain man drew his bow without taking special aim, and he struck the king of Israel between the joints of his armor.” No name, no aim, no hatred, just a bow drawn at random into the melee, and the arrow found the one gap in the armor of the one man in disguise. Ahab told his charioteer, “Turn around and take me out of the battle, for I am badly wounded!” But he did not leave the field. He was propped up in his chariot facing the Arameans the whole day while the battle raged, and “the blood from his wound ran out onto the floor of the chariot, and that evening he died”. At sunset the cry went through the leaderless army, every man to his own city and his own land, the shepherd-less sheep of Micaiah's vision scattering home exactly as foretold. They carried the king to Samaria and buried him, and the chariot was washed out at the pool of Samaria, “and the dogs licked up Ahab’s blood, according to the word that the LORD had spoken”, in the place of Naboth's blood, by the word spoken in the vineyard. The rest of his acts, the ivory house he built, the cities, went into the chronicles. His son Ahaziah reigned in his place, and the formula closes the book without mercy or surprise: he walked in the ways of his father and his mother and of Jeroboam, and “Ahaziah served and worshiped Baal, provoking the LORD, the God of Israel, to anger, just as his father had done.”

Jehoshaphat of Judah gets the book's last full notice, and it lands like a held breath. He reigned twenty-five years in Jerusalem and walked in the ways of his father Asa, did right in the eyes of the LORD, banished the last of the shrine prostitutes, and the refrain of his reign is the book's oldest refrain, unhealed since Solomon was young: the high places, however, were not removed; the people still sacrificed and burned incense on the high places. His one venture in Solomon's footsteps, a fleet of Tarshish ships built at Ezion-geber to sail for Ophir's gold, wrecked in harbor and never sailed, as if the age of fleets and gold had closed with his great-grandfather. So the book of the kings opens with an old man shivering in bed while his sons maneuver for the throne, and it closes with the throne divided, the sentence on Ahab's house deferred one generation, Elijah still alive in Israel with a successor at his side and three commissions only one of them finished, and a boy king in Samaria worshiping Baal. Nothing is resolved. The story runs straight on into the next book without pausing, and the lamp in Jerusalem, against all of it, is still lit.
