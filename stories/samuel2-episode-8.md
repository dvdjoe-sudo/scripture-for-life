2 Samuel Episode 8: The Rebellion
2 Samuel 15 to 17, Berean Standard Bible

After the kiss, Absalom built himself the equipment of a rival court: a chariot with horses and fifty men to run ahead of him, a rolling announcement that Israel had another address for its hopes. Then he worked the gate. He rose early and stood beside the road where every grievance in Israel came to petition the king, and he intercepted the petitioners before they ever reached David. Your claims are good and right, he would tell each one, but the king has no deputy to hear you. If only someone would appoint me judge in the land, then everyone with a dispute could come to me, and I would give him justice. And when a man approached to bow before the king's son, Absalom would stop the bow, take hold of the man, and kiss him, royal courtesy weaponized into intimacy. The narrator's verdict is a single plain sentence: in this way Absalom stole the hearts of the men of Israel. The raw material he worked with was real, and the book does not hide it. David's justice had grown remote inside his palace, the same palace where a sister's rape had gone unjudged and a murder had been answered with longing. Absalom did not manufacture the vacancy. He camped in it. Four years of this, and then the request that set the trap: permission to go to Hebron to pay a vow he claimed to have made in exile. David blessed him and sent him off with the words this family had used before a fall before. Go in peace. At Hebron the trumpets sounded across the tribes, Absalom reigns in Hebron, and two hundred guests who had come innocently from Jerusalem found themselves exhibits in a coronation. And the conspiracy gained strength, because Absalom had sent for the one man whose counsel was treated in those days like an oracle of God: Ahithophel the Gilonite, David's counselor. The text never explains his defection. It gives one thread and leaves it visible: Bathsheba's father Eliam stands in the roster of David's mighty men as a son of Ahithophel the Gilonite. The wisest man in Israel was the grandfather of the woman on the roof, and he went over to the rebellion with his counsel intact.

The messenger's report to David was six words of summary: the hearts of the men of Israel are with Absalom. David's decision was instant and it was the decision of the fugitive of twenty years before, not of a king defending a throne: arise and let us flee, or we will not escape. He left ten concubines to keep the palace, a detail the story is not finished with, and the court streamed out of the city eastward, past the last house, across the Kidron, toward the wilderness, while the countryside wept loudly at the sight. The book slows the procession down to name its loyalists, and the naming matters. The king's foreign bodyguard marched past, Cherethites and Pelethites and six hundred Gittites, Philistines who had followed him from Gath, and David stopped their commander, Ittai, a foreigner and an exile who had arrived only yesterday, and released him from any obligation: go back, stay with the new king. Ittai's answer is the covenant loyalty of Jonathan translated into a soldier's oath: "As surely as the LORD lives, and as my lord the king lives, wherever my lord the king may be, whether it means life or death, there will your servant be!" A man from Gath swore to David in exile what Israel at the gate would not. Then came Zadok and Abiathar with the ark on the Levites' shoulders, and David sent the ark back into the city, in the anti-climax of his entire career: no talisman this time, no carrying God into his emergency. "If I find favor in the eyes of the LORD, He will bring me back and let me see both it and His dwelling place again." And if the verdict went the other way, if God should say of him, "I do not delight in you", David's answer was already settled: "here I am; let Him do to me whatever seems good to Him". He submits to a verdict he has not heard, from the God who had already forgiven him a capital sin and kept the sentence standing. The priests went back as his eyes in the city, their sons as runners, and David climbed the Mount of Olives weeping, head covered, barefoot, while everyone with him covered their heads and wept as they went. The Psalter preserves a song for this slope: Psalm 3 carries the heading, a psalm of David, when he fled from his son Absalom. At the summit, where David used to worship, two pieces of news arrived in sequence. First, that Ahithophel was among the conspirators, and David prayed the shortest prayer of his life, asking God to turn Ahithophel's counsel into foolishness. Second, Hushai the Archite, his friend, robe torn and dust on his head, and David, thinking faster than his grief, sent him back into the city as the answer to that prayer: go, present yourself as Absalom's servant, and defeat the counsel of Ahithophel from inside the room.

On the far side of the summit the fugitives met supply and slander in the same hour. Ziba came down with loaded donkeys, two hundred loaves, raisins, summer fruit, wine, a steward's perfect instincts on full display, and the king asked where his master was. Ziba's report was short: Mephibosheth was staying in Jerusalem, because he had said that today the house of Israel would restore to him his grandfather's kingdom. David, exhausted, betrayed by his son that week, believed the servant's word about the lame man he had seated at his table, and on the spot he transferred everything Mephibosheth owned to Ziba. The book does not tell you yet whether Ziba lied. It files the grant and moves on, leaving a king's hasty sentence in wartime to be weighed later, when the king comes back over this same road and two men tell two stories. Around the next bend waited Shimei son of Gera, of Saul's family, at Bahurim, and he came out cursing and throwing stones at the king and his mighty men, dust in the air, shouting that the LORD had paid David back for all the blood of the house of Saul, and delivered the kingdom to Absalom, and that David had come to ruin because he was a man of bloodshed. Some of the charge was false: David had shed no blood of Saul's house, and had executed the men who did. Some of it landed nearer than David's guards could admit, on a road away from a city where a Hittite's blood was still unaccounted for. Abishai asked leave to cross over and take the head off this dead dog. David refused, and his reasoning is the theological center of the flight: if Shimei cursed because the LORD had told him to curse David, then who could ask the man, "Why did you do this?" His own son, his flesh and blood, sought his life; how much more this Benjamite. Leave him alone. "Perhaps the LORD will see my affliction and repay me with good for the cursing I receive today." So the king walked on under the stones, and Shimei kept pace along the ridge, cursing and throwing dust, until the company reached the Jordan, exhausted, and refreshed themselves there.

In Jerusalem, the rebellion crowned itself with an act of policy. Hushai arrived as Absalom entered the city and survived his first interview with a courtier's nerve, swearing loyalty to the one chosen by the LORD and by Israel. Then Absalom asked Ahithophel what to do, and Ahithophel's first counsel was not military. Sleep with your father's concubines, the ones he left to keep the palace, in the sight of all Israel: it would make Absalom a stench to his father beyond reconciliation, and strengthen every hand that had joined him. They pitched a tent on the roof, the same roof in this story's architecture, and Absalom went in to his father's concubines before the eyes of the nation, and Nathan's sentence, given in a quiet room years before, fulfilled itself clause by clause: what David had done in secret was answered in broad daylight before all Israel. Ahithophel's second counsel was the war itself, and it was perfect: twelve thousand men, tonight, while David was weak and weary; strike only the king; the people would scatter and could then be brought back like a bride to her husband, with the life of one man the whole cost. It pleased Absalom and every elder in the room. Then Absalom, on an impulse the book attributes at the deepest level to God's purpose, asked to hear Hushai as well. Hushai's counter-counsel was flattery built into strategy: this time, he said, Ahithophel's advice was not sound. David was a man of war, fierce as a bear robbed of her cubs, and he would not be sleeping with the troops; a first bloody check would melt Israel's courage. Instead, gather all Israel from Dan to Beersheba, a multitude like the sand on the seashore, and let Absalom himself lead them down on David like dew, until not one man remained. Absalom and all the men of Israel chose Hushai's counsel, and the narrator steps out from behind the story to tell you why the worse plan won: "For the LORD had purposed to thwart the good counsel of Ahithophel in order to bring disaster on Absalom." The prayer on the Mount of Olives had been answered inside the enemy's council chamber, by delay.

Delay was all David needed. The priests' sons ran the warning out of the city by a servant girl's errand, hid in a well in a Bahurim courtyard under a covering scattered with grain while Absalom's men searched above them, and reached the king: cross the Jordan tonight. By daybreak there was no one left who had not crossed. And Ahithophel, reading the rejection of his counsel as the verdict it was, saddled his donkey, rode home to Giloh, put his affairs in order, and hanged himself, and was buried in his father's tomb, the counselor of Israel exiting the story with the same cold competence he had brought to it. David reached Mahanaim, where Saul's pretender had once been crowned, and found the country rallying to the fugitive as it had in the old days: Shobi from Rabbah, Machir of Lo-debar, the same man who had sheltered Mephibosheth, and Barzillai the Gileadite brought beds and basins and wheat and beans and honey and cheese to the camp, because they said the people had become hungry and exhausted and thirsty in the wilderness. Absalom crossed the Jordan after him with all Israel, having set Amasa, Joab's own cousin, over his army in Joab's place. The two hosts stood in Gilead, and the rebellion moved toward the forest of Ephraim.
