Leviticus Episode 3: Clean, Unclean, and the Day That Cleanses All
Leviticus 11 to 16, Berean Standard Bible

Aaron had been told to distinguish between the holy and the common, between the clean and the unclean, so he could teach Israel. The next five chapters are that teaching, and it reaches into places a reader does not expect a law book to go: the pot, the birthing bed, the skin, the walls of a house.

None of it is random, and the book says why at the end of the first list.

The list is food. Of the animals on the land, Israel could eat any animal that has a split hoof completely divided and that chews the cud. The camel, the rabbit, and the rock badger chew the cud but do not have a split hoof, so they were unclean. The pig has a split hoof but does not chew the cud, so it was unclean. In the waters, anything with fins and scales could be eaten. What lived in the water without them was detestable. Named birds were barred, the eagle, the vultures, the raven, the owls, the hawk, the osprey, the stork, the hoopoe, and the bat among them. Flying insects were detestable, except the hopping kind with jointed legs: the locust, the katydid, the cricket, and the grasshopper could be eaten. Creatures that crawl and swarm on the ground were unclean, and touching their carcasses made a person unclean until evening. A dead one falling into a clay pot made the pot and everything in it unclean, and the pot had to be broken.

Chapter 11 does not leave the list sitting there as kitchen rules. It closes with the reason, spoken twice.

"For I am the LORD your God; consecrate yourselves, therefore, and be holy, because I am holy."

"For I am the LORD, who brought you up out of the land of Egypt so that I would be your God; therefore be holy, because I am holy."

The God who brought them out of Egypt would be marked off from other gods by what His people ate and touched at an ordinary table. The aim of the whole section is stated in its last line.

"You must distinguish between the unclean and the clean, between animals that may be eaten and those that may not."

Chapter 12 is short, and it is about the start of life. A woman who gave birth to a son was unclean for seven days, and on the eighth day the boy was circumcised. She continued in purification from her bleeding for thirty-three days, touching nothing sacred and not going into the sanctuary. For a daughter, the times doubled: two weeks, then sixty-six days. When the days of her purification were complete, she brought a year-old lamb for a burnt offering and a young pigeon or a turtledove for a sin offering, to the priest at the entrance to the Tent of Meeting, and the priest presented them before the LORD and made atonement for her, and she was cleansed.

The sliding scale from the sin offering chapters is here too, applied to a new mother.

"But if she cannot afford a lamb, she shall bring two turtledoves or two young pigeons," one for the burnt offering and one for the sin offering, and the priest would make atonement for her, and she would be clean. A rich mother and a poor mother left the tent entrance the same way.

Chapters 13 and 14 are the longest stretch of inspection law in the book, and their main character is not the sick person. It is the priest, working slowly.

When someone had a swelling or a rash or a bright spot on the skin that might be an infectious skin disease, he was brought to Aaron or to one of his sons. The priest examined the infection. If the hair in it had turned white and the sore appeared deeper than the skin, it was a skin disease, and the priest pronounced him unclean. If the spot was white but not deeper than the skin and the hair had not turned white, the priest did not decide on the spot. He isolated the person for seven days.

On the seventh day he examined him again. If the infection was unchanged and had not spread, he isolated him for another seven days. If on the next examination the sore had faded and had not spread, the priest pronounced him clean. It was only a rash. The man washed his clothes and was clean. Boils and burns and scalps and beards each had their own signs to read, and the verdicts changed with what the priest could see. A bald head by itself was clean. A reddish-white sore on the bald scalp was not.

The law is patient in a way worth noticing. Again and again the priest's first act is not a verdict. It is a week of waiting, and then a second look.

For the person pronounced unclean with a skin disease, the terms were hard and public. He had to wear torn clothes and let his hair hang loose. He had to cover his mouth and cry out as he went.

"Unclean, unclean!"

"As long as he has the infection, he remains unclean. He must live alone in a place outside the camp."

The same inspection reached cloth and leather. Mildew in wool or linen or a leather article was shut up for seven days too. If it spread, the article was burned, because the mildew was harmful. If it faded after washing, the article could be washed again and pronounced clean.

Chapter 14 is the way back in, and it begins with the priest going out. On the day of his cleansing, the man who had been afflicted was brought to the priest, and the priest went outside the camp to examine him. If the disease had left him, the priest ordered two live clean birds, cedar wood, scarlet yarn, and hyssop. One bird was slaughtered over fresh water in a clay pot. The live bird, with the cedar wood, the scarlet yarn, and the hyssop, was dipped in the blood of the slaughtered bird. Seven times the priest sprinkled the man who was being cleansed. Then he pronounced him clean, and released the live bird into the open field.

The cleansed man washed his clothes, shaved off all his hair, and bathed in water, and then he could come into the camp, staying outside his own tent for seven more days. On the seventh day he shaved again, head, beard, eyebrows, all of it, washed, and bathed. On the eighth day he brought his offerings: two unblemished male lambs, a year-old ewe lamb without blemish, grain mixed with oil, and a log of olive oil, and the priest made atonement for him before the LORD, putting blood and oil on the right ear, right thumb, and right big toe that the ordination had marked on the priests. And again the law counted the cost for the poor man. If he could not afford the lambs, one lamb and a tenth of an ephah of flour and two birds, whichever he could afford, would do, and the priest would make atonement for him, and he would be clean.

Houses got the same treatment as skin and cloth, in a section that speaks about a land Israel had not entered yet. When Israel came into Canaan and a contamination of mildew appeared in a house, the owner told the priest. The priest ordered the house emptied before he examined it, so everything in it would not be pronounced unclean with it. He looked at the walls, at greenish or reddish depressions deeper than the surface, and shut the house for seven days. If the contamination had spread, the stones it was on were pulled out and thrown in an unclean place outside the town, the inside was scraped, and new stones and new mortar went in. If the mildew returned and spread after all that, the house was torn down, stones, timbers, and all its plaster, and carried outside the town to an unclean place. If it did not return, the priest pronounced the house clean, and cleansed it with the same rite as the man: two birds, cedar wood, scarlet yarn, and hyssop, one bird slaughtered, the live one released into the open fields outside the town.

Chapter 15 is briefer and more private. It deals with bodily discharges, a man's discharge, a woman's monthly period, and any flow of blood beyond it. What the unclean person sat on or lay on became unclean, and whoever touched those things washed and was unclean until evening. When the discharge stopped, the person counted seven days, washed, bathed in fresh water, and on the eighth day brought two birds, and the priest made atonement before the LORD. The chapter gives its reason in one sentence, and the sentence points back at the tent in the center of the camp.

"You must keep the children of Israel separate from their uncleanness, so that they do not die by defiling My tabernacle, which is among them."

That is where five chapters of lists have been heading. Food, birth, skin, cloth, houses, and bodies all carry the same pressure: a holy God lives in the middle of this camp, and the uncleanness of ordinary life cannot be allowed to pile up against His tent unchecked. But the daily laws handled uncleanness case by case, a pot here, a person there. Once a year, the whole debt was dealt with at once, all the way into the room no one entered.

Chapter 16 opens by naming the two dead men from the last episode.

"Now the LORD spoke to Moses after the death of two of Aaron's sons when they approached the presence of the LORD."

Then God spoke about Aaron, their father, and the room behind the veil.

"Tell your brother Aaron not to enter freely into the Most Holy Place behind the veil in front of the mercy seat on the ark, or else he will die, because I appear in the cloud above the mercy seat."

Aaron could enter. He could not enter freely. This is how he was to come: with a young bull for a sin offering and a ram for a burnt offering, for himself. He was to bathe himself with water and put on the sacred linen tunic, with linen undergarments, a linen sash, and a linen turban. On this day he did not wear the gold and blue and scarlet of his office, the breastpiece with the stones, the robe with the bells, the gold plate. He wore plain linen, washed, like a servant dressed for work no one would watch. From the congregation he was to take two male goats for a sin offering and one ram for a burnt offering.

Aaron presented the two goats before the LORD at the entrance to the Tent of Meeting and cast lots for them: one lot for the LORD, and the other for the scapegoat.

First, Aaron presented the bull and made atonement for himself and his household, and slaughtered it. No man could carry Israel's sins into that room carrying his own unatoned.

Then he took a censer full of burning coals from the altar before the LORD, and two handfuls of finely ground fragrant incense, and took them inside the veil. He put the incense on the fire before the LORD.

"And the cloud of incense will cover the mercy seat above the Testimony, so that he will not die."

He took some of the bull's blood and sprinkled it with his finger on the east side of the mercy seat, and sprinkled some of it seven times before the mercy seat. From the time he went in until he came out, no one else was allowed in the Tent of Meeting at all. The whole congregation's cleansing hung on one man, alone, behind a veil, in a cloud of incense, sprinkling blood toward the cover of the ark where God had promised to meet His people from above the cherubim.

The goat chosen by lot for the LORD was slaughtered as the sin offering for the people. Aaron brought its blood behind the veil and did with it as he had done with the bull's blood, sprinkling it against the mercy seat and in front of it. So he made atonement for the Most Holy Place itself, because of the impurities and rebellious acts of the Israelites in regard to all their sins, and he did the same for the Tent of Meeting, which stood among them in the midst of their impurities. The tent needed cleansing too. A year of Israel's camp life had pressed against it.

He came out to the altar before the LORD and made atonement for it, putting blood from the bull and blood from the goat on all its horns and sprinkling blood on it seven times, to cleanse it and consecrate it from the uncleanness of the Israelites.

Then, when Aaron had finished purifying the Most Holy Place, the Tent of Meeting, and the altar, he brought forward the live goat, the one left standing at the entrance through all of it.

He laid both his hands on the head of the live goat. Both hands, where the other offerings had taken one. And he confessed over it all the iniquities and rebellious acts of the Israelites in regard to all their sins. Not the unintentional sins only, the ones the sin offering covered when a man became aware of them. All of them, named out loud over the animal's head, the rebellions included. He put them on the goat's head, and sent it away into the wilderness by the hand of a man appointed for the task.

"The goat will carry on itself all their iniquities into a solitary place, and the man will release it into the wilderness."

The goat was not slaughtered. No blood of it touched any altar. It walked out of the camp alive, carrying the year's confessed sins on its head, into a place where no one lived, and it did not come back.

Aaron went back into the Tent of Meeting, took off the linen garments, and left them there. He bathed in a holy place, put on his own clothes, the garments of his office, and went out and sacrificed his burnt offering and the people's burnt offering, and burned the fat of the sin offering on the altar. The bull and the goat whose blood had been brought into the Most Holy Place were taken outside the camp and burned, hides, flesh, and dung. The men who burned them and the man who released the goat each washed and bathed before coming back into the camp.

The day had a date, and it was fixed forever.

"On the tenth day of the seventh month, you shall humble yourselves and not do any work," whether the native or the foreigner who lived among them. It was a Sabbath of complete rest, a permanent statute.

"Because on this day atonement will be made for you to cleanse you, and you will be clean from all your sins before the LORD."

The priest anointed to succeed Aaron as high priest would do the same in his turn, in the sacred linen garments, for the priests and all the people of the assembly.

"This is to be a permanent statute for you, to make atonement once a year for the Israelites because of all their sins."

Five chapters of distinguishing end here, on one day a year when the distinguishing reaches the center and the whole people, clean and unclean, careful and rebellious, are cleansed together and start again with an empty ledger. Aaron has come out of the room his sons died approaching wrongly, alive, bathed and dressed again in the garments of his office, and the goat is a speck somewhere out in the solitary places with a year of Israel's sins on its head.

What has not been touched yet is the rest of the week. The offerings run at the tent. The cleanness runs at the tent and the camp boundary. But Israel also marries, earns, plants, harvests, judges, and buries in between the festivals and the tenth day of the seventh month, and the LORD had as much to say about those hours as about the day behind the veil.
