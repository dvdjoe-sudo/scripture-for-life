Numbers Episode 5: Korah and the Staff That Blossomed
Numbers 16 to 19, Berean Standard Bible

The sentence had been handed down. The counted generation would die in the wilderness, and the camp had settled into the first stretch of those forty years. The next rebellion did not come from the edges of the camp, the way the complaints about food had. It came from the center, from the family that carried the ark.

Korah was a Levite, a son of Kohath: one of the men whose family bore the most holy things on their shoulders, covered, forbidden to touch them or look at them and live. With him came some Reubenites, Dathan and Abiram, sons of Eliab, and On son of Peleth, and behind all of them stood two hundred and fifty leaders of the congregation, men of renown, chosen from the assembly. The Reubenites' tents stood on the south side of the tabernacle, next to the Kohathites. The rebellion had an address, and the address was as close to the tent as a layman's tent could be.

They came together against Moses and Aaron with a charge built out of a truth.

"You have taken too much upon yourselves! For everyone in the entire congregation is holy, and the LORD is in their midst. Why then do you exalt yourselves above the assembly of the LORD?"

Everyone in the congregation was holy. God had said so Himself, in almost those words, back at the mountain. The claim was the claim Miriam and Aaron had made in smaller form, now with 250 leaders behind it: if God speaks to all, why do two brothers stand between Him and everyone else? Moses, who had once begged God not to make him a nation in Israel's place, heard the charge that he had made himself a king.

When Moses heard this, he fell facedown. Then he answered with a test instead of a defense. Tomorrow morning the LORD would reveal who belongs to Him and who is holy, and He would bring that person near to Himself. Korah and his company were to take censers, put fire and incense in them, and come before the LORD. The man God chose would be the holy one. Moses added the point the whole rebellion had stepped around: a censer is a priest's instrument. The Levites were about to try to do at the tent's entrance what Nadab and Abihu had died doing inside it.

Moses sent for Dathan and Abiram, and they refused the summons with a speech that showed how far the memory of Egypt had rotted. Is it not enough that you have brought us up out of a land flowing with milk and honey to kill us in the wilderness, that you also make yourself ruler over us? Egypt, called a land flowing with milk and honey: the phrase God used for Canaan, handed to the slave pits. And you have not brought us into a land flowing with milk and honey, they said, or given us an inheritance of fields and vineyards. Will you gouge out the eyes of these men? We will not come.

Moses was greatly angered, and he said to the LORD: do not accept their offering. I have not taken one donkey from them, and I have not wronged a single one of them. Then he spoke to Korah again, and this time his question went to the root of the man's own story. Moses reminded the sons of Levi what they already had: separated from the congregation of Israel, brought near to God to do the work of the tabernacle and stand before the congregation to minister to them, Korah and all his fellow Levites with him. Was that a small thing? He and his company were seeking the priesthood too.

"Therefore, it is you and all your followers who have conspired against the LORD! As for Aaron, who is he that you should grumble against him?"

The next morning Korah gathered his whole assembly against Moses and Aaron at the entrance to the Tent of Meeting, and every man of the 250 stood with his censer and his fire. The glory of the LORD appeared to the whole congregation, and God spoke the sentence Moses had heard at the calf and at the spies: separate yourselves from this congregation, so that I may consume them in an instant.

Moses and Aaron fell facedown, and their intercession this time had a lawyer's precision. O God, the God of the spirits of all flesh, will You be angry with the whole congregation when only one man has sinned? God narrowed the judgment to the households involved. The congregation was to move away from the dwellings of Korah, Dathan, and Abiram.

Moses went to the Reubenites' tents with the elders of Israel following him, and he warned the congregation to move back, and to touch nothing that belonged to these men, or be swept away in all their sins. The people moved back. Dathan and Abiram came out and stood at the entrances of their tents, with their wives, their sons, and their little children, a whole household lined up behind its father's rebellion. Korah stood with them there. Then Moses announced how the camp would know whose claim was true. If these men die a natural death, the LORD has not sent me. 

"But if the LORD brings about something unprecedented, and the earth opens its mouth and swallows them and all that belongs to them so that they go down alive into Sheol, then you will know that these men have treated the LORD with contempt."

As soon as Moses finished saying all this, the ground beneath them split open. The earth opened its mouth and swallowed their households, Korah and all his men, and everything they owned. They went down alive into Sheol with all they owned, and the earth closed over them, and they vanished from the assembly. All Israel around them fled at their cries, shouting that the earth might swallow them too. And fire came forth from the LORD and consumed the 250 men who were offering the incense.

On son of Peleth, the fourth name in the rebellion's first verse, is never mentioned again in the chapter. The text does not say whether he went home before the morning, or what became of him. He simply disappears from the story between the conspiracy and the ground opening, and the record leaves him there.

The censers of the dead men were not thrown away. Eleazar son of Aaron gathered the bronze censers from the fire, the censers of men who had sinned at the cost of their lives, and had them hammered out as plating for the altar: a covering on the altar itself, a memorial to the Israelites that no outsider who is not a descendant of Aaron should come near to burn incense before the LORD, or he would become like Korah and his company. The altar the priests served at every morning now wore the flattened censers of the men who had tried to take the priesthood by claim. Every sacrifice after that was offered on top of the warning.

The next day, the whole congregation grumbled against Moses and Aaron with a sentence that shows how little the ground opening had settled.

"You have killed the LORD's people!"

God's anger answered it with a plague, and the plague moved faster than the argument. Get away from this congregation so that I may consume them in an instant, God said, and Moses and Aaron fell facedown. But this time Moses did not stay down. He told Aaron to take his censer, put fire from the altar and incense in it, and carry it quickly into the congregation to make atonement for them, because wrath had gone out from the LORD and the plague had begun. Aaron ran. He stood in among the dying with smoke rising from a priest's censer, the very instrument the 250 had died holding wrongly, and the text gives the picture in one line.

"He stood between the living and the dead, and the plague was halted."

The dead from the plague numbered 14,700, besides those who had died on account of Korah. Aaron walked back to Moses at the entrance of the Tent of Meeting. The priesthood Korah had called a brother's power grab had just saved the congregation that was accusing it, with the high priest's own body as the wall.

One more sign was given, quieter than the earth and the fire, because the grumbling needed an answer that could be kept. Each leader of the twelve tribes was to bring a staff, his name written on it, Aaron's name on the staff of Levi. The staffs were laid up in the Tent of the Testimony before the LORD. The staff belonging to the man God chose would sprout.

"The next day Moses entered the Tent of the Testimony and saw that Aaron's staff, representing the house of Levi, had sprouted, put forth buds, blossomed, and produced almonds."

A dead stick, cut from a tree and carved into a walking staff, had run the whole course of a living branch in one night: buds, blossoms, and ripe almonds on the same wood. Moses brought all the staffs out and the Israelites looked at them, and each man took his own back, eleven pieces of dead wood and one that had flowered. The LORD told Moses to put Aaron's staff back in front of the Testimony, kept as a sign for the rebellious, to put an end to their grumbling. Israel's answer, when they saw it, was fear rather than peace: we are perishing, we are all perishing, everyone who comes near the tabernacle will die. Are we all to die?

Chapters 18 and 19 answer that fear with the structure Leviticus had built and Numbers now explained from the inside. Aaron and his sons bore responsibility for the sanctuary and the priesthood, a service given to them as a gift, with the warning that any outsider who came near would be put to death. The priests would have no inheritance of land among Israel.

"You will have no inheritance in their land, nor will you have any portion among them. I am your portion and your inheritance among the Israelites."

Their living came from the altar: the holy offerings, the wave offerings, the firstfruits and the firstborn given to them as an everlasting covenant of salt. The Levites received Israel's tithe, a tenth of everything, as their inheritance in return for their work at the tent, and from that tenth the Levites themselves gave a tenth of the best to the priests. The men Episode 1 had counted as a ring around the tent now had their whole economy explained: no fields, no vineyards, and a share of everything the other tribes grew and raised.

And for a camp that was going to spend forty years burying its own generation, chapter 19 gave the rite for death's uncleanness itself. A red heifer, unblemished, without defect, that had never been placed under a yoke, was given to Eleazar outside the camp, slaughtered in his presence, its blood sprinkled seven times toward the front of the Tent of Meeting. The whole animal was burned with cedar wood, hyssop, and scarlet wool thrown into the fire. A man who was clean gathered the ashes and stored them in a clean place outside the camp, kept by the congregation for the water of purification. Mixed with fresh water, the ashes of one burned heifer cleansed the man who had touched a corpse, a bone, or a grave, sprinkled on the third day and the seventh, after which he washed and was clean at evening. The one who refused the water was cut off, because he had defiled the sanctuary of the LORD.

It was provision for the book's long middle. The camp that had just buried 14,700 of its own in a day was going to live among graves for decades, and every burial made someone unclean. God's answer was not to move the camp away from death. It was to keep a jar of ashes outside the camp, ready, so that death never had the last word on whether a man could come back in.
