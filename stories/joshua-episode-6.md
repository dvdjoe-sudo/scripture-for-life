Joshua Episode 6: Lots at Shiloh and the Cities of Refuge
Joshua 16 to 21, Berean Standard Bible

After Judah, the lots turned to Joseph's house, the double tribe. Ephraim took the central hills from the Jordan at Jericho up through Bethel and down to Gezer and the sea, with cities of its own sprinkled inside Manasseh's borders. Manasseh took the long northern stretch from the sea up past Shechem and the Jordan, with assigned cities reaching into Issachar and Asher: Beth-shean, Ibleam, Dor, Endor, Taanach, Megiddo. And inside these two strong allotments the honest refrain of this whole section starts sounding. At Gezer, Ephraim's border city: "But they did not drive out the Canaanites who lived in Gezer. So the Canaanites dwell among the Ephraimites to this day, but they are forced laborers." At Manasseh's northern cities the record is plainer still: the tribe "were unable to occupy these cities, because the Canaanites were determined to stay in this land", and when Israel grew stronger it settled for forced labor, "but they failed to drive them out completely."

Into the middle of Manasseh's allotment walk five women with a court ruling in their favor from a generation earlier. Zelophehad's daughters, Mahlah, Noah, Hoglah, Milcah, and Tirzah, approached Eleazar, Joshua, and the leaders with a claim that quoted God's own decision: "The LORD commanded Moses to give us an inheritance among our brothers." They received it, shares among their father's brothers, and Manasseh's portion rose to ten shares because of them. The case those sisters had won before Moses in the wilderness was still paying out in land west of the Jordan.

Joseph's sons also brought Joshua a complaint, and his answer to it is the Caleb scene played backward. "Why have you given us only one portion as an inheritance? We have many people, because the LORD has blessed us abundantly." Joshua told them to take the forested hill country and clear it. They objected that the hill country was not enough and the valley was dangerous: "all the Canaanites who live in the valley have iron chariots, both in Beth-shean with its towns and in the Valley of Jezreel." Caleb, at eighty-five, had asked for the giants' hill country by name. The house of Joseph, numerous and strong, asked for a second portion that did not require fighting chariots. Joshua's final word gave them more land and no sympathy at all: "You have many people and great strength. You shall not have just one allotment, because the hill country will be yours as well. It is a forest; clear it, and its farthest limits will be yours. Although the Canaanites have iron chariots and although they are strong, you can drive them out."

The dividing then moved to Shiloh and slowed down. The whole congregation assembled there and set up the Tent of Meeting, the traveling sanctuary now planted in the land, "and though the land was subdued before them, there were still seven tribes of Israel who had not yet received their inheritance." Joshua's patience with the pause ran out in public: "How long will you put off entering and possessing the land that the LORD, the God of your fathers, has given you?" His method replaced waiting with walking: three men from each of the seven tribes, twenty-one surveyors, were sent to travel the remaining land, map it city by city in a written document, and divide it into seven portions, Judah holding the south and Joseph the north. They came back to Shiloh with the map, and Joshua cast the lots there, "in the presence of the LORD."

Benjamin drew first of the seven, a narrow inheritance wedged between Judah and Joseph, Jericho and Bethel and Gibeon among its twenty-six cities, and Jebus, which is Jerusalem. Then the lots finished the map in a run: Simeon, whose inheritance lay inside Judah's oversized territory; Zebulun toward the northern valleys; Issachar below Mount Tabor; Asher along the northern coast; Naphtali in the far north hills; and Dan, whose allotment proved too tight to hold, so that the Danites later went up and took Leshem in the far north and renamed it Dan after their father. Last of everyone, the leader was given his own: "the Israelites gave Joshua son of Nun an inheritance among them, as the LORD had commanded. They gave him the city of Timnath-serah in the hill country of Ephraim, as he requested. He rebuilt the city and settled in it." The man who had divided a country asked for one town, and the survey closed: "So they finished dividing up the land."

Two institutions were planted before the division stood complete, and both were about the kind of country this would be. The first was the cities of refuge, designated "so that anyone who kills another unintentionally or accidentally may flee there. These will be your refuge from the avenger of blood." The procedure was law, not sanctuary by sentiment: the fugitive stated his case at the city gate before the elders, was taken in and given a place to live, and could not be surrendered to the avenger "because that man killed his neighbor accidentally without prior malice." He stayed until he stood trial before the assembly and until the death of the high priest, and only then went home. Six cities were set apart: Kedesh in Galilee, Shechem in Ephraim's hills, and Hebron in Judah, west of the Jordan; Bezer, Ramoth in Gilead, and Golan in Bashan, east of it. And the provision was written for "all the Israelites and foreigners among them," the same accidental killer, the same gate, the same hearing.

The second institution gave the landless tribe its scattering. The Levites came to Shiloh asking for what Moses had promised them, cities to live in with pastureland for their animals, and Israel gave them towns out of every tribe's inheritance. Aaron's priestly line drew thirteen, including Hebron, now a city of refuge, though its surrounding fields stayed Caleb's, as the text is careful to note. The rest of Kohath drew ten, Gershon thirteen, Merari twelve. The count closes at forty-eight cities in all, "within the territory of the Israelites", the tribe with no territory sprinkled through everyone else's.

Then the narrator steps back and reads the whole allotment as a verdict, and these are the sentences the division was built to reach: "Thus the LORD gave Israel all the land He had sworn to give their fathers, and they took possession of it and settled in it. And the LORD gave them rest on every side, just as He had sworn to their fathers. None of their enemies could stand against them, for the LORD delivered all their enemies into their hand." And the last word: "Not one of all the LORD's good promises to the house of Israel had failed; everything was fulfilled."

The same chapters had just recorded Canaanites holding Gezer, Megiddo, Beth-shean, and Jerusalem. The verdict and the failures stand in the same book without apology, because they measure different things. The allotment was complete and every tribe held its deed; the possession would take generations and some of it would never be finished. What had not failed was a promise. The land was given. What Israel would do with the giving was the question the rest of the Bible would spend itself answering. And the first test of the divided nation's unity was already on its way home, eastward, across the Jordan, where the two and a half tribes were about to build something by the river that their brothers would see as treason.
