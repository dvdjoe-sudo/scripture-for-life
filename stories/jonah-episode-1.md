Jonah Episode 1: The Prophet Who Ran
Jonah 1-2, Berean Standard Bible

Jonah son of Amittai was already a prophet of standing when this story opens. The book of Kings names him as the man through whom the LORD had promised that Israel's borders would be restored, and under Jeroboam II they were, in the most prosperous reign the north ever had. He was, as far as the record shows, a prophet of good news for his own people. Then the word of the LORD came to him with a different assignment entirely: “Get up! Go to the great city of Nineveh and preach against it, because its wickedness has come up before Me.” Nineveh was the great city of Assyria, the empire whose armies were the terror of every small nation in the region, Israel included. Jonah's response is told in a single contrast: “Jonah, however, got up to flee to Tarshish, away from the presence of the LORD.” The book will not explain the flight until its final chapter, in Jonah's own words. Here it only tracks the direction, and the direction is down. He went down to Joppa, found a ship bound for Tarshish, “So he paid the fare and went aboard to sail for Tarshish, away from the presence of the LORD”, the phrase sounded twice in one verse, as if the text wants the reader to hear the distance being bought.

The LORD answered the run with weather: He hurled a great wind upon the sea, and the storm rose until the ship was in danger of breaking apart. The sailors did everything their world knew to do. Each man cried out to his own god, and they threw the cargo into the sea to lighten the load, a crew watching its living sink crate by crate to keep the hull alive. Below all of it, in the lowest part of the vessel, the prophet of the LORD lay in a deep sleep, and it was the pagan captain who came and shook the sleeper awake with a sermon in two sentences: “How can you sleep? Get up and call upon your God. Perhaps this God will consider us, so that we may not perish.” Above deck the sailors cast lots to find the guilty man, because a storm like this had to be somebody's fault, and the lot fell on Jonah. Their questions came in a volley, who was to blame, what was his occupation, where had he come from, what was his country, who were his people. His answer gave them more than they had bargained for: “I am a Hebrew,” replied Jonah. “I worship the LORD, the God of the heavens, who made the sea and the dry land.” That confession is the whole comedy and horror of the chapter in one line: the man fleeing across the sea confesses that his God made the sea. The sailors understood at once, because he had already told them he was running, and their fear doubled: “What have you done?”

The sea kept growing worse, and the sailors, to their credit, asked the fugitive what to do with him rather than simply doing it. His answer is the most honest thing he says in the chapter: “cast me into the sea, so it may quiet down for you. For I know that I am to blame for this violent storm that has come upon you.” Even then the crew would not do it. They rowed hard for dry land, men who owed this stranger nothing straining at the oars to save the life of the man whose flight was killing them, and the sea raged against them more and more. Only when the rowing failed did they do the thing he had asked, and they prayed first, to Jonah's God, in Jonah's God's own terms: “Please, O LORD, do not let us perish on account of this man’s life! Do not charge us with innocent blood! For You, O LORD, have done as You pleased.” Then they picked him up and cast him into the sea, “and the raging sea grew calm”. The chapter's last word on the sailors is the one the reader least expects from pagans in a prophet's story: “the men feared the LORD greatly, and they offered a sacrifice to the LORD and made vows to Him”. The prophet had run from preaching to the nations, and the nations on the deck had ended up worshiping, while he sank.

What waited under the water was not death but appointment: “the LORD had appointed a great fish to swallow Jonah, and Jonah spent three days and three nights in the belly of the fish”. From inside the fish, Jonah prayed, and the prayer is the episode's second peak, and its strangest feature is its mood. It is not a plea to get out. It is a thanksgiving, built line by line out of Israel's psalms, sung by a man still inside the fish, recounting a drowning that has not yet finished ending. “In my distress I called to the LORD, and He answered me. From the belly of Sheol I called for help, and You heard my voice.” “For You cast me into the deep, into the heart of the seas, and the current swirled about me; all Your breakers and waves swept over me.” He describes the descent the way the sailors never saw it, down past the surface storm into the deep itself: “I have been banished from Your sight; yet I will look once more toward Your holy temple.” “The waters engulfed me to take my life; the watery depths closed around me; the seaweed wrapped around my head.” And then the prayer turns on its hinge, from the roots of the mountains to the rescue stated as already done: “But You raised my life from the pit, O LORD my God!”

The prayer's close draws the line the whole book is about, between the nations' worthless idols and the God who keeps loving devotion: “Those who cling to worthless idols forsake His loving devotion.” “But I, with the voice of thanksgiving, will sacrifice to You. I will fulfill what I have vowed. Salvation is from the LORD!” Those are the last words spoken inside the fish, and the answer to them is not commentary but an act of God: “the LORD commanded the fish, and it vomited Jonah onto dry land”. The prophet who had been carried as far down as a man can go, to the belly of Sheol and the roots of the mountains, is deposited on a beach, alive, unharmed, and still holding the same assignment he had run from. The word of the LORD is about to come to him a second time, and this time the whole great city is waiting on the other end of it.
