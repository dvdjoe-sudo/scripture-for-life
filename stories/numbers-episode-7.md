Numbers Episode 7: Balaam and the Fall at Peor
Numbers 22 to 25, Berean Standard Bible

Moab watched Israel take the land of two kings, and Moab was afraid. The Moabites said to the elders of Midian that this horde would devour everything around them, as an ox licks up the grass of the field. Balak son of Zippor, king of Moab, did not march out the way Sihon and Og had marched out. He went shopping for a weapon armies could not answer: a curse, professionally delivered.

His messengers went to Pethor, by the Euphrates, to Balaam son of Beor, a man with an international reputation in exactly that trade.

"Behold, a people has come out of Egypt," said Balak. "They cover the face of the land and have settled next to me. So please come now and put a curse on this people, because they are too mighty for me. Perhaps I may be able to defeat them and drive them out of the land; for I know that those you bless are blessed, and those you curse are cursed."

The elders of Moab and Midian came to Balaam with the fees for divination in their hands, and Balaam did the professionally correct thing: stay the night, and I will bring back word, as the LORD may speak to me. God came to Balaam that night and asked who the men were, and gave His answer before the negotiation could start.

"Do not go with them. You are not to curse this people, for they are blessed."

Balaam sent the princes home with a polite refusal: the LORD has refused to let me go with you. Balak, who understood the market, sent a second delegation, more princes and more distinguished than the first, carrying a bigger offer. I will honor you richly and do whatever you say. Balaam's answer to the servants is the answer of a man saying the right thing and hoping the circumstances change.

Balaam's reply is on the record: "If Balak were to give me his house full of silver and gold, I could not do anything small or great to go beyond the command of the LORD my God."

Then he added the sentence that undid it: stay here tonight as well, and I will find out what else the LORD will speak to me. God came that night and permitted the trip, with a leash on it. Since these men have come to summon you, get up and go with them, but you must only do what I tell you. Balaam saddled his donkey in the morning and went. And God's anger was kindled because he went, a sentence the text states flatly after stating the permission flatly, and leaves standing there, unexplained, the way this book leaves its hardest sentences. The road itself delivered the commentary.

The donkey saw what the seer could not. The angel of the LORD was standing in the road with a drawn sword in his hand, and the donkey turned off the path into a field, and Balaam beat her to return her to the path. The angel stood in a narrow lane between vineyards, wall on this side and wall on that, and the donkey pressed against the wall and crushed Balaam's foot against it, and he beat her again. The angel moved ahead to a narrow place where there was no way to turn right or left, and the donkey lay down under Balaam, and Balaam's anger burned and he beat her with his staff.

Then the LORD opened the donkey's mouth, and she said to Balaam: "What have I done to you that you have beaten me these three times?"

Balaam answered the animal, mid-argument, before the strangeness of it seems to have reached him: because you have made a fool of me. If I had a sword in my hand, I would kill you right now. He was standing in a road telling his donkey he wished he had a sword, while a drawn sword he could not see was the whole reason for the conversation.

But the donkey said to Balaam: "Am I not the donkey you have ridden all your life until today? Have I ever treated you this way before?" He replied: "No."

Then the LORD opened Balaam's eyes, and he saw the angel of the LORD standing in the road with the sword drawn, and Balaam bowed low and fell facedown. The angel asked him the donkey's question in heaven's wording: why have you beaten your donkey these three times? I have come out to oppose you, because your way is perverse before me. The donkey saw me and turned away these three times. If she had not turned away, I would surely have killed you by now, and spared her.

"I have sinned," Balaam said to the angel of the LORD, "for I did not realize that you were standing in the road to confront me. And now, if this is displeasing in your sight, I will go back home."

The offer to go back was allowed to pass. Go with the men, the angel said, but you are to speak only what I tell you. The prophet who charged kings for curses arrived in Moab with his fee on the table and his mouth under orders, and he told Balak the terms to his face.

"See, I have come to you," Balaam replied, "but can I say just anything? I must speak only the word that God puts in my mouth."

Balak built the curse like a siege. At Bamoth-baal, from which Balaam could see the outskirts of the camp, Balak sacrificed oxen and sheep, and Balaam had him build seven altars and prepare seven bulls and seven rams. Balaam went off alone to a barren height to meet God, and God met him, and put a word in his mouth. He came back and stood beside the burnt offering, with all the princes of Moab standing there waiting for a curse, and he opened his mouth and delivered the first oracle. Balak brought me from Aram to curse Jacob and denounce Israel, it began. How can I curse what God has not cursed? From the top of the rocks I see him, a people dwelling alone, not counting itself among the nations.

"Who can count the dust of Jacob or number even a fourth of Israel? Let me die the death of the righteous; let my end be like theirs!"

Balak's response was commercial, not theological: what have you done to me? I brought you to curse my enemies, and behold, you have only blessed them. Balaam reminded him of the leash. Balak, like any buyer, changed the location and ordered another attempt: come to another place, where you will see the edge of them but not all of them, and curse them for me from there. Seven more altars on the top of Pisgah, seven more bulls, seven more rams. God met Balaam again. The second oracle is the one preachers have quoted ever since, and it landed in a hired curse-session on a mountaintop in Moab.

"God is not a man, that He should lie, or a son of man, that He should change His mind. Does He speak and not act? Does He promise and not fulfill?"

"I have indeed received a command to bless; He has blessed, and I cannot change it."

He sees no trouble for Israel, the oracle went on. The LORD their God is with them, and the shout of the King is among them. God brought them out of Egypt with the strength of the wild ox. There is no spell against Jacob and no divination against Israel. Balak tried a third arrangement, and this time his instructions show a man managing a defective product: do not curse them at all, and do not bless them at all, either. Balaam answered that he had told the messengers from the beginning that he could only speak what the LORD said. Balak took him up anyway, to the top of Peor, overlooking the wilderness. Seven altars again. Twenty-one bulls and twenty-one rams had now been burned across three mountaintops to purchase one sentence against Israel, and the sentence would not come.

At Peor, Balaam stopped waiting for a chance encounter. He saw that it pleased the LORD to bless Israel, and he did not go as at other times to seek omens, but set his face toward the wilderness. He lifted up his eyes and saw Israel camping tribe by tribe, the square of standards from Episode 1 laid out below him, and the Spirit of God came upon him. The third oracle opens on the camp itself.

"How lovely are your tents, O Jacob, your dwellings, O Israel!"

Like valleys stretching out, like gardens beside a river, like aloes the LORD has planted. Water will flow from their buckets and their seed will have abundant water. Their king will be greater than Agag, and their kingdom exalted. And it closed with a line Balak had paid a fortune trying to reverse.

The oracle pictured Israel crouching and lying down "like a lion, like a lioness" that no one dares to rouse, and it ended where it had to end for Balak: "Blessed are those who bless you and cursed are those who curse you."

Balak's anger burned against Balaam, and he struck his hands together. I summoned you to curse my enemies, and behold, you have persisted in blessing them these three times. Now flee to your homeland. I said I would honor you richly, but the LORD has held you back from honor. Balaam reminded him, one more time, of the fine print he had announced to every messenger: even if Balak gave him his house full of silver and gold, he could not go beyond the command of the LORD. And before he left, he gave Balak, unasked, one final oracle, about what this people would do to Moab in the latter days. Out of it comes the book's most far-reaching sentence, spoken by a hired prophet on his way home unpaid.

"I see him, but not now; I behold him, but not near. A star will come forth from Jacob, and a scepter will arise from Israel. He will crush the skulls of Moab and strike down all the sons of Sheth."

Oracles followed over Edom, Amalek, the Kenites, Asshur, and Eber, and then Balaam rose and went back to his place, and Balak also went his way. The curse had failed completely. No spell, no divination, no mountain, no fee had moved God's word one inch. Israel, camped in its square below, had not heard a syllable of any of it. The whole war had been fought over their heads, on three mountains, and they had won it without knowing it was happening.

What they could not survive was their own camp, at a place called Shittim.

While Israel was staying in Shittim, the men began to indulge in sexual immorality with the daughters of Moab. The women invited the people to the sacrifices of their gods, and the people ate and bowed down to their gods.

"So Israel joined in worshiping Baal of Peor, and the anger of the LORD burned against them."

The LORD told Moses to take all the leaders of the people and execute them in broad daylight before the LORD, so that His fierce anger would turn away from Israel, and Moses told Israel's judges to each kill his own men who had joined in worshiping Baal of Peor. A plague was already moving through the camp. And while the whole congregation was weeping at the entrance to the Tent of Meeting, an Israelite man walked in front of Moses and everyone, bringing a Midianite woman into camp, to his family. The text names him when the act is done: Zimri son of Salu, a leader of a Simeonite family. The woman was Cozbi, daughter of Zur, a leader of a Midianite clan. A prince of Simeon and a princess of Midian, in the middle of a plague, in front of the weeping congregation, a deliberate display.

The text records that Phinehas son of Eleazar, the son of Aaron the priest, got up from the assembly and took a spear in his hand. He followed the Israelite into the tent and drove the spear through both of them, through the Israelite and on through the belly of the woman. "So the plague against the Israelites was halted."

The dead from the plague numbered 24,000. Phinehas was Aaron's grandson, Eleazar's son, the third generation of the priesthood Leviticus had ordained, and God's word on his act was not a rebuke for violence but a covenant.

"Phinehas son of Eleazar, the son of Aaron the priest, has turned My wrath away from the Israelites; for he was zealous for My sake among them, so that I did not consume the Israelites in My zeal."

"Declare, therefore, that I am granting him My covenant of peace. It will be a covenant of permanent priesthood for him and his descendants, because he was zealous for his God and made atonement for the Israelites."

And the book's last word on the whole Moab affair is a command that reaches forward into Episode 8's generation: attack the Midianites and strike them dead, because they assailed Israel deceitfully when they seduced them in the matter of Peor, and in the matter of Cozbi, their sister, the woman killed on the day the plague came because of Peor.

Set the two halves of this episode side by side, because the book sets them that way. On the mountains, a pagan king spent twenty-one bulls, twenty-one rams, and a promised fortune, and could not buy one curse against Israel. In the valley, Israel cursed itself for the price of a dinner invitation and a god's feast, and 24,000 died. The camp Balaam had blessed from above, tribe by tribe in its perfect square, was counted again in the next chapter, because after Peor a new census was not paperwork anymore.

It was a roll call of who was left.
