2 Samuel Episode 10: The Last Things
2 Samuel 21 to 24, Berean Standard Bible

The last four chapters of this book are not the next four things that happened. They are the book's appendix, gathered out of sequence the way the closing chapters of Judges were gathered, a famine from somewhere in the reign's middle, battles from its wars, a song from the day of deliverance looking back over everything, and a plague near the end. Read them as the author's final exhibits, set side by side so that the reign can be weighed whole.

The first exhibit is a famine, three successive years of it, and David sought the face of the LORD, and the answer was a blood debt inside the national past: "It is because of the blood shed by Saul and his family, because he killed the Gibeonites." The Gibeonites were the people who had tricked Israel into a covenant in Joshua's day, and Israel had sworn to them before God, and the oath had held for centuries, until Saul, in his zeal for Israel and Judah, tried to exterminate them. A dead king's zeal had become a living land's drought. David summoned the Gibeonites and asked what he could do, how he could make amends so that they might bless the inheritance of the LORD. They refused silver and gold; money was not the currency of this debt. For the man who had consumed them and plotted to wipe them from every border of Israel, they asked seven of his male descendants, to be hanged before the LORD at Gibeah of Saul. David gave them. The covenant with Jonathan fenced off one name first: the king spared Mephibosheth because of the oath between them. The seven taken were two sons of Rizpah, Saul's concubine, the same woman whose treatment by Abner had once cracked the house of Saul open, and five sons of Saul's daughter Merab. They were hanged on the hill in the first days of the barley harvest, all seven together, and then their mother did the thing this appendix is remembered for. Rizpah took sackcloth and spread it on a rock and kept vigil over the bodies from the beginning of harvest until the rains came, months on a rock, driving the birds from them by day and the beasts by night, a concubine of a fallen house performing with her own body the honor that nations had refused her sons. When David was told what she had done, he went and gathered the bones of Saul and Jonathan from the men of Jabesh-gilead, who had rescued them from the square at Beth-shan a generation before, and the bones of the hanged men with them, and buried them all together in the tomb of Saul's father Kish. After they had done everything the king commanded, the text says, "God answered their prayers for the land." The famine ends not with the executions but with the burial, and it is Rizpah's vigil that moves the king to it. The book keeps its pattern to the end: God hears, and the lowly are the ones who show the king his duty.

The second exhibit is David's strength running out. The Philistines came to war again, and David went down with his men and fought, and became exhausted, and a giant named Ishbi-benob, with a bronze spearhead of three hundred shekels and a new sword, resolved to kill him, and would have, had Abishai not come to his aid and struck the Philistine down. Then David's men swore an oath of their own to their king: "You must never again go out with us to battle, so that the lamp of Israel may not be extinguished." The shepherd who ran toward Goliath was now the treasure the army refused to spend. Three more battles against the giants of Gath follow in a rapid honor roll: Saph, killed by Sibbecai; a second giant at Gob killed by Elhanan of Bethlehem, whom this text names as the killer of the brother of Goliath the Gittite, a reading the Chronicles account shares, whatever older tellings made of it; and a taunting giant with six fingers on each hand and six toes on each foot, killed by Jonathan, David's nephew. Four descendants of Rapha fell, the text concludes, at the hands of David and his servants. The giant-killing that began with one boy and a sling had become the house trade.

Then the appendix sets down its centerpiece: the song. David sang it, the heading says, on the day the LORD delivered him from all his enemies and from the hand of Saul, and the same song stands in the Psalter as Psalm 18, one song with two addresses, in the king's story and in the nation's worship. It opens with a pile of names for God heaped like stones: "The LORD is my rock, my fortress, and my deliverer." It remembers drowning, the waves of death and the cords of Sheol, and the cry that reached God's temple, and then it remembers rescue as the sky tearing open, God coming down with dark clouds under His feet, thundering, scattering enemies with arrows of lightning, reaching down from on high to draw one man out of deep waters. "He brought me out into the open; He rescued me because He delighted in me." It is the old man's theology of his own survival, and he states its principle without embarrassment: God deals with people as they deal with Him, faithful to the faithful, and it remembers what God made of him, feet like a deer on the heights, arms trained to bend a bow of bronze. "For You, O LORD, are my lamp; the LORD lights up my darkness." And the song ends where chapter 7 began it, on the covenant, the forever promise now worn smooth by a lifetime of testing: "He shows loving devotion to His anointed, to David and his descendants forever."

Beside the song stand the last words, and beside them the honor roll. The last words call David the oracle of the man raised on high, the anointed of the God of Jacob, the sweet psalmist of Israel, and they define the king he had tried and failed and tried to be: "He who rules the people with justice, who rules in the fear of God, is like the light of the morning at sunrise of a cloudless dawn". And they rest, at the end, not on the reign's record but on the covenant: "For He has established with me an everlasting covenant, ordered and secured in every part." Then come the mighty men, the Thirty and the Three above them: Josheb-basshebeth, who killed eight hundred at one time; Eleazar, who fought until his hand froze to his sword; Shammah, who held a lentil field alone while Israel fled. There is the story of the three who broke through the Philistine garrison at Bethlehem to bring David water he had longed for aloud, and David, who would not drink it, who poured it out to the LORD saying, "Is this not the blood of the men who risked their lives?" There is Benaiah, who killed a lion in a pit on a day of snow. And the roll closes, in the book's last accounting of honor, with the name it can neither promote nor omit: and Uriah the Hittite. Thirty-seven in all.

The final exhibit is a sin of the king's old age, and this book's last word about power. The anger of the LORD burned against Israel, and David was stirred to count the fighting men, a census Joab and the commanders were sent to execute from Dan to Beersheba. Remarkably, it is Joab who protests, Joab of the bloody hands and the unerring instinct for what a king should not be seen doing: "But why does my lord the king want to do such a thing?" The king's word prevailed. Nine months and twenty days the counters walked the land, and the totals came back, eight hundred thousand in Israel and five hundred thousand in Judah, an empire numbered like property. And after he had numbered the troops, David's conscience was stricken, the same conviction that had once struck him over a corner of a robe, and he confessed in the old plain words: "I have sinned greatly in what I have done. Now, O LORD, I beg You to take away the iniquity of Your servant, for I have acted very foolishly." The word of the LORD came through Gad the prophet with a choice of sentences, three years of famine, three months of flight before enemies, or three days of plague, and David chose with the last great act of theological judgment in the book: "I am deeply distressed. Please, let us fall into the hand of the LORD, for His mercies are great; but do not let me fall into the hands of men." The plague fell, and seventy thousand died from Dan to Beersheba, and as the angel stretched out his hand toward Jerusalem the LORD relented and spoke the mercy David had banked on: "Enough! Withdraw your hand now!" The angel stood at the threshing floor of Araunah the Jebusite. David, seeing the angel, made the intercession of a shepherd king and reversed Uriah's account at last: the sin was his, the sheep had done nothing; let God's hand fall on him and his father's house. Gad sent him up to build an altar on that threshing floor, and Araunah came out bowing and offered everything as a gift, oxen and sledges and yokes. The king refused the gift with the sentence that ends his story's account of worship: "I insist on paying a price, for I will not offer to the LORD my God burnt offerings that cost me nothing." He bought the floor and the oxen for fifty shekels of silver, built the altar, offered the sacrifices, and the plague was halted, and the LORD answered the prayers on behalf of the land. The book of David does not end in the palace or on a battlefield. It ends on a purchased threshing floor above the city, where the king paid full price to stand between his people and judgment. The story will say later what that ground became. David's own book leaves him there, at an altar, in the last place his sin and God's mercy ever met.
