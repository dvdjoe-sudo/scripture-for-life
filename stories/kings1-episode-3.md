1 Kings Episode 3: Building the House
1 Kings 5-7, Berean Standard Bible

The temple began as a letter between two kings who had inherited a friendship. Hiram of Tyre had always been a friend of David, and when he heard Solomon was anointed he sent envoys, and Solomon sent back the explanation of why this building belonged to his generation and not his father's. David could not build a house for the Name of the LORD because of the wars waged on all sides against him, until God put his enemies under his feet. “But now the LORD my God has given me rest on every side, and there is no adversary or crisis.” So the son of the man of war would build in the peace the father had won, exactly as God had told David he would. What Solomon needed was Lebanon's cedars and the Sidonians' skill with timber, men who could do what no one in Israel could, and he offered to pay Hiram's servants whatever wages Hiram set. Hiram's answer came back rejoicing: “Blessed be the LORD this day! He has given David a wise son over this great people!” The bargain was struck. Hiram's crews would haul the logs down to the sea and float them as rafts to wherever Solomon named, and year after year Solomon would send Tyre twenty thousand cors of wheat and twenty thousand baths of pure olive oil for Hiram's household. The two kings made a treaty, and the timber began to move south on the water.

Behind the treaty stood a workforce the size of a small nation, and the text gives its numbers without flinching. Solomon conscripted a labor force of thirty thousand men from all Israel and sent them to Lebanon in shifts of ten thousand, one month in the mountains and two months at home. Seventy thousand porters carried burdens and eighty thousand stonecutters worked in the mountains, watched by three thousand three hundred foremen, and over the whole levy stood the same name from the last chapter, Adoniram, in charge of the forced labor. The foundation stones were quarried large and costly and dressed at the quarry itself. The date the book assigns the work is stated the way the text states it, and left there: “In the four hundred and eightieth year after the Israelites had come out of the land of Egypt”, in the fourth year of Solomon's reign, in the month of Ziv, he began to build the house of the LORD. Four centuries and more between the Exodus and this foundation, according to the book's own reckoning.

The building itself the narrator walks through in measurements, and the measurements can be carried in a breath. The house was sixty cubits long, twenty wide, thirty high, roughly ninety feet by thirty by forty-five, with a portico across the front and three stories of side rooms built against its walls on ledges, so that no beam was cut into the sanctuary wall itself. One detail in the middle of the dimensions has outlasted all the numbers: “The temple was constructed using finished stones cut at the quarry, so that no hammer or chisel or any other iron tool was heard in the temple while it was being built.” The stone arrived ready. The holy ground stayed quiet. And while the walls were still rising, the word of the LORD came to Solomon with the condition again, the same condition from the deathbed charge and the dream at Gibeon, now attached to the building itself: “As for this temple you are building, if you walk in My statutes, carry out My ordinances, and keep all My commandments by walking in them, I will fulfill through you the promise I made to your father David.” Then the promise that the whole structure existed for: “And I will dwell among the Israelites and will not abandon My people Israel.” Inside, the walls were cedar carved with gourds and open flowers. “Everything was cedar; not a stone could be seen.” At the rear stood the Most Holy Place, a cube of twenty cubits overlaid with pure gold, and inside it two cherubim of olive wood, each ten cubits tall, their wings spread so that one wingtip touched each wall and the other two met in the middle of the room. The floors were gold. The doors were carved and hammered with gold. In the eleventh year of Solomon's reign, in the month of Bul, the temple stood finished in every detail. “So he built the temple in seven years.”

Then the book sets a second number next to the first and lets the two numbers talk to each other. “Solomon, however, took thirteen years to complete the construction of his entire palace.” Seven years for the house of God, thirteen for the house of the king: the House of the Forest of Lebanon with its forty-five cedar pillars, the Hall of Justice where he judged, paneled floor to ceiling in cedar, his own residence behind it, and a palace built like it for Pharaoh's daughter. The text offers no comment on the difference. It simply reports both figures, in adjacent chapters, and moves on to the bronze. For the bronze work Solomon brought a master craftsman from Tyre, Huram, son of a widow of Naphtali and a Tyrian bronze worker, a man the text credits with skill and understanding and knowledge for every kind of bronze work. Huram cast the two great pillars for the portico, eighteen cubits high, their capitals laced with chainwork and ringed with two hundred pomegranates apiece, and he gave them names, the way the pillars of a gate get named when they are meant to be remembered: “The pillar to the south he named Jachin, and the pillar to the north he named Boaz.” He cast the Sea, a round basin ten cubits across, standing on twelve bronze oxen facing outward to every point of the compass, and ten wheeled stands engraved with lions and oxen and cherubim, and basins and pots and shovels and bowls in such quantity that the bookkeeping gave up: “Solomon left all these articles unweighed, because there were so many. The weight of the bronze could not be determined.” The gold furnishings followed, the altar and the table for the Bread of the Presence and ten lampstands of pure gold, five on the right and five on the left. And when all the work was complete, Solomon closed the account his father had opened: he brought in the silver and gold and furnishings David had dedicated from his wars, the spoils the warrior had set aside for a house he was not permitted to build, and placed them in the treasuries of the house of the LORD. The building stood. The next thing it needed was the ark, and a king on his knees.
