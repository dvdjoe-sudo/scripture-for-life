Judges Episode 9: Micah's Gods, and Dan's Migration
Judges 17 to 18, Berean Standard Bible

The last five chapters of Judges contain no judge, no oppressor, and no cry. They are placed at the end of the book, and the first thing to know about them is that they did not happen at the end of the era. Their own markers put them early: the priest in this story will turn out to be a grandson of Moses, and in the next story Phinehas, Aaron's grandson, is still ministering before the ark. These are tales from the first generation or two after Joshua, kept for the end of the book the way you save the diagnosis for after the symptoms. The narrator hangs a refrain over them like a sign over a door: in those days there was no king in Israel, and everyone did what was right in his own eyes. Samson's personal creed, now posted as the condition of the whole nation.

A man named Micah, from the hill country of Ephraim, stole eleven hundred shekels of silver from his own mother. The sum is the one the lords of the Philistines offered Delilah, each of them; in this story it is a widow's whole fortune. She uttered a curse on the thief, not knowing he was her son, and Micah, who heard the curse, came and confessed: "I have the silver here with me; I took it." His mother reversed the curse on the spot into a blessing, "Blessed be my son by the LORD!", and then she did the thing that defines the age. She dedicated the recovered silver to the LORD, in so many words, for the making of an idol: "I wholly dedicate the silver to the LORD for my son's benefit, to make a graven image and a molten idol. Therefore I will now return it to you." To the LORD, for an idol. The sentence does not notice its own contradiction, and neither does she. She took two hundred of the shekels to a silversmith, and the images were made and installed in Micah's house, where he already kept a private shrine complete with an ephod and household idols, and had ordained one of his own sons as his priest. A stolen fortune, a mother's blessing, a homemade god, a boy priest. The narrator posts the sign over the scene: "In those days there was no king in Israel; everyone did what was right in his own eyes."

Then a genuine Levite walked into the story, a young man from Bethlehem in Judah who had left home "to settle where he could find a place," a priest of the LORD's own tribe drifting for work. Micah hired him the day he arrived, offering him the title and the terms: the young man was to "be my father and priest," at a wage of "ten shekels of silver per year, a suit of clothes, and your provisions." Ten shekels a year, for a Levite, bought with the leftovers of stolen silver a silversmith had already skimmed. The Levite agreed and settled in and became like a son of the house, and Micah ordained him over the shrine, and Micah's conclusion from the whole arrangement is the theology of the era stated as a proverb: "Now I know that the LORD will be good to me, because a Levite has become my priest." A real Levite on the payroll made the idols official, in Micah's accounting. God could be hired, housed, and pointed at a shrine, and His goodness could be known in advance from the staffing.

The tribe of Dan enters the story still homeless. "In those days there was no king in Israel, and the tribe of the Danites was looking for territory to occupy. For up to that time they had not come into an inheritance among the tribes of Israel." Dan's coastal allotment had never been taken; the Amorites had penned the tribe into the hills back in chapter 1, and Samson's lifetime of skirmishing had not changed the map. So Dan sent five men of valor from Zorah and Eshtaol, Samson's own towns, to spy out a new country, and on their way north they lodged at Micah's house and recognized the young Levite's voice. They interrogated him: "Who brought you here? What are you doing in this place? Why are you here?" He told them plainly: "Micah has done this and that for me," he replied, "and he has hired me to be his priest." Then the spies asked the hired priest for the service he was hired for: "Please inquire of God to determine whether we will have a successful journey." And the priest told them: "Go in peace. The LORD is watching over your journey." Whether God had spoken any such thing, the text does not say. The oracle cost nothing and pleased the customer, which in Micah's shrine may have been the same thing.

The spies went on to Laish, at the far north, and found a city living the way Israel was supposed to make its neighbors afraid to live: secure, "quiet and unsuspecting," with nothing lacking, no oppressive ruler, far from Sidon and holding no alliance with anyone. They came home with the good spies' report, in the very cadences Caleb once used for the promised land: "Come on, let us go up against them, for we have seen the land, and it is very good. Why would you fail to act? Do not hesitate to go there and take possession of the land! When you enter, you will come to an unsuspecting people and a spacious land, for God has delivered it into your hand. It is a place where nothing on earth is lacking." Every word of the conquest's faith, aimed at a peaceful city Israel had no quarrel with, no command against, and no claim on. The forms of devotion had survived intact. They were now available for hire, like the priest.

Six hundred Danites marched north with their families, armed for war, and camped at Kiriath-jearim, a place called Mahaneh-dan, the camp of Dan, to this day, the same camp name where the Spirit had stirred Samson. From there they came to Micah's house, and the five spies briefed their brothers with the inventory: "Did you know that one of these houses has an ephod, household gods, a graven image, and a molten idol? Now think about what you should do." What they did was efficient. While the six hundred armed men stood at the gate with the priest, the five went inside and took the graven image, the ephod, the household idols, and the molten idol. The Levite protested: "What are you doing?" The Danites made him a better offer than Micah's, and phrased it as wisdom: put your hand over your mouth, "and come with us and be a father and a priest to us. Is it better for you to be a priest for the house of one person or a priest for a tribe and family in Israel?" A promotion, from one man's shrine to a whole tribe's. "So the priest was glad and took the ephod, the household idols, and the graven image, and went with the people." Glad. He carried the gods out himself.

They set their children, livestock, and possessions in front of the column, expecting trouble behind them, and Micah came as expected, with the men of his neighborhood mobilized, shouting after his property. The Danites turned and played innocent with an army at their backs: "What is the matter with you that you have called out such a company?" Micah's answer is the most nakedly honest line in the appendix, a man telling the truth about what his religion was: "You took the gods I had made, and my priest, and went away. What else do I have?" The gods I had made. He knew where they came from; he had watched the silversmith pour them. They were his gods anyway, and with them gone he had nothing, which was exactly what he had had with them, minus the silver. The Danites dropped the innocence for a death threat: "Do not raise your voice against us, or angry men will attack you, and you and your family will lose your lives." Micah saw they were too strong for him, and turned and went home, and that is the last the book says of him: a man robbed of the gods he manufactured, by the tribe that would worship them for centuries.

The Danites came to Laish, "to a quiet and unsuspecting people, and they struck them with their swords and burned down the city." The narrator adds the sentence that measures it: "There was no one to deliver them, because the city was far from Sidon and had no alliance with anyone." Deliver is the book's word for what God did for Israel through judge after judge. Here it appears in the negative, for a city of strangers, because Israel had become the kind of danger Israel used to cry out against. They rebuilt the city and named it Dan, after their forefather, and set up Micah's idols as the tribal shrine. And then the narrator names the priest at last, and the name is the story's detonation, saved for its final verse: "The Danites set up idols for themselves, and Jonathan son of Gershom, the son of Moses, and his sons were priests for the tribe of Dan until the day of the captivity of the land." Jonathan son of Gershom, son of Moses. The wandering Levite who sold his priesthood for ten shekels and a promotion was the grandson of the man who received the Law, and his sons inherited the idol's altar at Dan for generations, until the Assyrians emptied the land centuries later. And the closing note sets the two shrines side by side without comment: Micah's graven image stood at Dan "the whole time the house of God was in Shiloh." The true sanctuary and the stolen idol operated in parallel, in the same small country, for the entire age of the judges, and both were staffed by Levites, and Israel saw no crisis in it at all.

There is one story left in the book, and it makes this one look like a dispute over property. It also begins with a Levite far from home, in the same hill country of Ephraim, and a household, and a journey. It ends with a tribe of Israel nearly erased from the earth, by Israel.
