Judges Episode 7: Samson: The Riddle and the Foxes
Judges 13 to 15, Berean Standard Bible

The longest oppression in the book gets the quietest opening. "Again the Israelites did evil in the sight of the LORD, so He delivered them into the hand of the Philistines for forty years." Forty years, and for the first time in the cycle the text records no cry. Israel had stopped groaning. The Philistines were not a raid or a tax anymore; they were the weather. And into that silence, before any deliverer was asked for, God sent an announcement to a barren woman in Zorah, of the clan of Dan, the tribe the Amorites had penned into the hills in Judges 1.

The angel of the LORD appeared to the wife of Manoah with a promise and a regimen in the same breath: "It is true that you are barren and have no children; but you will conceive and give birth to a son. Now please be careful not to drink wine or strong drink, and not to eat anything unclean. For behold, you will conceive and give birth to a son. And no razor shall touch his head, because the boy will be a Nazirite to God from the womb, and he will begin the deliverance of Israel from the hand of the Philistines." Begin. Hold that word against everything Samson does. No deliverer before him was given a partial assignment; Othniel finished his war, Ehud finished his. Samson's commission is to begin a deliverance that Israel would need another generation and a king to complete, and the Nazirite vow over him, no razor, no wine, no unclean food, begins before he is born and binds his mother first.

She reported it to her husband with a field report's plainness: "A man of God came to me. His appearance was like the angel of God, exceedingly awesome." Manoah prayed for a second visit, and his prayer is the most parental sentence in the book: "Please, O Lord, let the man of God You sent us come to us again to teach us how to raise the boy who is to be born." The angel returned, to the woman again, in the field, and Manoah ran after her and asked the practical question: "When your words come to pass, what will be the boy's rule of life and mission?" The answer repeated the regimen and added nothing. When Manoah pressed for hospitality and then for the visitor's name, he got the refusal that tells him who he is hosting: "Why do you ask my name," said the angel of the LORD, "since it is beyond comprehension?" The goat and grain offering went onto a rock, the flame rose, and "the angel of the LORD ascended in the flame." Manoah fell apart in the correct theological direction: "We are going to die," he said to his wife, "for we have seen God!" And his wife out-reasoned him, the second woman in this book to be its calmest theologian: "If the LORD had intended to kill us, He would not have accepted the burnt offering and the grain offering from our hands, nor would He have shown us all these things or spoken to us this way." The boy was born and named Samson, and the LORD blessed him, and the Spirit of the LORD "began to stir him at Mahaneh-dan, between Zorah and Eshtaol." Stir is the text's word, an unsettled pushing, and it is the right word for everything that follows.

Samson's first recorded act as a grown man is a demand. Down in Timnah, a Philistine town a few miles from home, he saw a woman, and came home and told his parents: "I have seen a daughter of the Philistines in Timnah. Now get her for me as a wife." His parents objected on the exact ground the book has been building since Joshua: "Can't you find a young woman among your relatives or among any of our people? Must you go to the uncircumcised Philistines to get a wife?" Samson's answer is the creed of the whole era in one line, though he applies it only to himself: "Get her for me, for she is pleasing to my eyes." Right in his own eyes. The narrator steps in with the book's most startling parenthesis: his parents did not know this was from the LORD, who was seeking an occasion against the Philistines. God intended to use the marriage as a provocation. It changes nothing about the marriage's wisdom; it explains its place in the war.

On the road down to Timnah, at the vineyards, a young lion came roaring at him, "and the Spirit of the LORD came powerfully upon him, and he tore the lion apart with his bare hands as one would tear a young goat." A Nazirite at a vineyard, where his vow said he should not be, kills a lion with strength that arrives like weather. He told no one. Later, returning to take the woman, he turned aside to see the carcass and found bees and honey in it, death housing sweetness, and he ate, and fed his parents, "but he did not tell them that he had taken the honey from the lion's carcass." A Nazirite must not touch a dead body, any dead body; Samson scooped honey out of one and served it at home. The vow is eroding in private, in small spoonfuls, long before Delilah.

The wedding feast ran seven days, with thirty Philistine companions assigned to the bridegroom, and Samson entertained them with a wager: "Let me tell you a riddle," Samson said to them. "If you can solve it for me within the seven days of the feast, I will give you thirty linen garments and thirty sets of clothes. But if you cannot solve it, you must give me thirty linen garments and thirty sets of clothes." "Tell us your riddle," they replied. "Let us hear it." The riddle was his secret, unwitnessed and therefore unfair, a lion and honey nobody else had seen: "Out of the eater came something to eat, and out of the strong came something sweet." Three days they worked at it and failed. On the fourth day the companions went around Samson to his wife with the book's recurring currency, fire: "Entice your husband to explain the riddle to us, or we will burn you and your father's household to death. Did you invite us here to rob us?"

She wept on him for the rest of the feast, with the argument every spouse in the book seems to use sooner or later: "You hate me! You do not really love me! You have posed to my people a riddle, but have not explained it to me." Samson's first defense was sound: "I have not even explained it to my father or mother, so why should I explain it to you?" But seven days of tears outlasted it. On the seventh day, "because she had pressed him so much, he told her the answer. And in turn she explained the riddle to her people." Before sunset the men of the city presented their solution as a counter-riddle: "What is sweeter than honey? And what is stronger than a lion?" Samson's answer named what they had done in the language of the farm: "If you had not plowed with my heifer, you would not have solved my riddle!"

Then the Spirit came mightily on him, and the deliverance God had promised to begin looked like this: he went down to Ashkelon, a Philistine city miles away, killed thirty of its men, stripped their clothes, and paid his wager with them. Thirty men who had never heard the riddle died for it. "And burning with anger, Samson returned to his father's house," and his wife was given to one of the companions, the best man. The occasion God sought had arrived, and it looked less like a war of liberation than a feud, which is precisely the texture of Samson's whole judgeship: the Spirit's power and the man's appetites pulling the same chariot.

Some time later, at wheat harvest, Samson took a young goat as a peace offering and went to visit his wife, expecting to go in to her. Her father blocked the door: "I was sure that you thoroughly hated her," said her father, "so I gave her to one of the men who accompanied you. Is not her younger sister more beautiful than she? Please take her instead." Samson's reply draws the line he would operate on for the rest of his life: "This time I will be blameless in doing harm to the Philistines." This time. The retaliation ladder starts here, and every rung of it will be, in Samson's accounting, the Philistines' fault. He caught three hundred foxes, tied them tail to tail in pairs with a torch fastened between each pair, lit the torches, and let them run through the standing grain of the Philistines at harvest, "burning up the piles of grain and the standing grain, as well as the vineyards and olive groves." Food, wine, and oil in one fire. When the Philistines learned why, that the Timnite's daughter had been given to another man, they went up and burned her and her father to death, carrying out on Samson's wife the exact threat her cooperation was meant to escape. The book does not pause over her. It lets the arithmetic speak: she betrayed her husband to avoid being burned, and was burned anyway, by her own people, for being betrayed by her father.

Samson's vengeance for that was personal and announced: "Because you have done this, I will not rest until I have taken vengeance upon you." He struck them "ruthlessly with a great slaughter," and withdrew to a cave in the rock of Etam, inside Judah's own territory. The Philistines camped in Judah and deployed near Lehi, and when Judah asked why, the answer was an arrest warrant: they had come to seize Samson and repay him. Here the book delivers its verdict on the forty years, not through a prophet but through Israel's own troops. Three thousand men of Judah went down to Etam, not to join Samson but to arrest him: "Do you not realize that the Philistines rule over us? What have you done to us?" Three thousand Israelites against one Israelite, on behalf of the Philistines. Samson's reply is the feud's whole logic in one sentence: "I have done to them what they did to me," he replied. He made them swear not to kill him themselves, and let his own tribe bind him with two new ropes and carry him up to the enemy like tribute.

At Lehi the Philistines came out shouting, "and the Spirit of the LORD came mightily upon him. The ropes on his arms became like burnt flax, and the bonds broke loose from his hands." He found the fresh jawbone of a donkey, another dead thing in the Nazirite's hand, "and struck down a thousand men." Then he sang, the only song of his the book records, two lines of wordplay on the word for donkey and the word for heap: "With the jawbone of a donkey I have piled them into heaps. With the jawbone of a donkey I have slain a thousand men." He named the place Ramath-lehi, jawbone hill, threw the bone away, and immediately nearly died of the most ordinary need in the world. His prayer, when it finally comes, is the first time Samson speaks to God in the book, and it is a complaint with a claim staked on it: "You have accomplished this great deliverance through Your servant. Must I now die of thirst and fall into the hands of the uncircumcised?" God split open a hollow place in Lehi and water came out, and Samson drank and revived, and named it En-hakkore, the spring of the one who called. It stands in Lehi to this day, says the narrator.

"And Samson judged Israel for twenty years in the days of the Philistines." Twenty of the forty years of oppression, with the deliverance still only begun, its champion a man who had never led an army, never summoned a tribe, never held a court under a palm tree, fighting a private war at irregular intervals with whatever came to hand. Israel had not cried out, and God had sent a deliverer anyway, and the deliverer was a riddle in the shape of a man: a Nazirite who ate from a corpse, a judge who took no counsel, strength without a governor on it. The Philistines had learned to fear his arms. What none of them yet knew, Israelite or Philistine, was that the way through Samson was never his strength. It was his tongue, loosened by whoever shared his pillow, and the next woman in his life would not stop at one secret.
