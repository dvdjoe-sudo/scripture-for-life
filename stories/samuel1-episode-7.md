1 Samuel Episode 7: Ten Thousands, and the Son of the King
1 Samuel 18 to 20, Berean Standard Bible

The first person in Saul's house to see David clearly was Saul's heir. After the giant, David finished speaking with the king, and the text says the souls of Jonathan and David were knit together, "and Jonathan loved him as himself". What the crown prince did next can be read as friendship, and it can also be read as abdication performed in clothing. Jonathan made a covenant with David, and stripped off the robe he was wearing and gave it to David, "along with his tunic, his sword, his bow, and his belt". The prince's robe. The prince's weapons. Whether Jonathan understood the full transfer he was enacting, the narrator lays the garments out one by one and lets them preach: the son of Saul handed the future to the son of Jesse, and loved him while he did it. Saul, meanwhile, kept David at court permanently, and sent him out on campaign after campaign, and David prospered at everything, and it pleased the people, and it pleased Saul's own officers.

Then the army came home and the women of Israel sang the king into his obsession. Out of every city they poured with tambourines and dancing to meet the returning troops, and their song was a comparison, because victory songs always are: "Saul has slain his thousands, and David his tens of thousands." Saul heard arithmetic, and the arithmetic had a remainder. "They have ascribed tens of thousands to David," he said, "but only thousands to me. What more can he have but the kingdom?" It is the most lucid sentence he speaks in his decline. He was not wrong about the trajectory; he was wrong about the response, which was murder. "And from that day forward Saul kept a jealous eye on David." The next day the distressing spirit came on the king while David played the harp, and Saul, spear in hand, hurled it twice at the musician, thinking, the narrator says, to pin him to the wall. Twice David slipped it. And the fear underneath the jealousy is named in words that summarize the whole transfer: Saul was afraid of David, "because the LORD was with David but had departed from Saul".

So Saul tried distance as a weapon. He gave David a command of a thousand and sent him out to the wars, which was exposure strategy, sending the rival where spears were thickest, and it backfired completely, because David kept winning, and "all Israel and Judah loved David, because he was leading them out to battle and back". Then Saul tried family. He offered his older daughter Merab for the bride-price of valor in the LORD's battles, thinking the Philistines would do his killing for him, and when the time came he gave Merab to another man instead. His younger daughter Michal loved David, and Saul heard that with pleasure, because a king's daughter could be set as a snare. The dowry he demanded was designed to be fatal: no gold, just a hundred Philistine foreskins, proof of a hundred kills up close. David went out before the deadline and brought back two hundred. Saul gave him Michal, and the chapter's accounting closes on the king like a cell door: when Saul saw "that the LORD was with David and that his daughter Michal loved David", he was afraid all the more: "So from then on Saul was David’s enemy."

The attempts now became orders. Saul commanded Jonathan and all his servants to kill David, and Jonathan, who had everything to lose by it, warned David and then stood before his father and argued the case on the facts: "He took his life in his hands when he struck down the Philistine, and the LORD worked a great salvation for all Israel. You saw it and rejoiced, so why would you sin against innocent blood by killing David for no reason?" It worked. Saul swore an oath by the LORD that David would not be put to death, and David returned to court as before. It did not hold. War came, David won again, the spirit came again, and the spear came a third time while the harp played, and this time David fled into the night and did not come back. Saul sent men to watch his house and kill him at dawn, and Michal lowered him from the window and laid a household idol in his bed with goat hair at its head to buy him the dark hours. When the messengers finally forced the room, they found the idol, and Saul's order to the second squad tells you how far the oath had rotted: "Bring him up to me in his bed so I can kill him." The psalm Israel still sings about that night of watching and escape is Psalm 59, whose heading ties it to this very errand; the book leaves the song to the Psalter and keeps its camera on the flight. David ran to the one address left to him: Samuel, at Ramah, the rejected prophet who had anointed him, and told him everything.

What happened at Ramah is the strangest protective custody in the Bible. Saul sent messengers to Naioth to seize David, and they found Samuel presiding over a company of prophets prophesying, and the Spirit of God fell on the arrest squad and they prophesied too. A second squad: the same. A third squad: the same. So Saul went himself, and the Spirit came on him on the road and he prophesied the whole way to Naioth, and there the king of Israel stripped off his robes and prophesied before Samuel and collapsed naked on the ground for a day and a night, while his quarry stood safe a few feet away. The proverb coined in wonder on the day of his anointing was quoted again, and the second time it is an obituary for his dignity: "Is Saul also among the prophets?" The God who would not let Saul's spears land would not let his errands land either.

David still could not believe the breach was final, which is why the last of these three chapters belongs to the arrows. He came to Jonathan with the question innocence asks power: "What have I done? What is my iniquity? How have I sinned against your father, that he wants to take my life?" And then the plainest self-assessment in his story: "there is but a step between me and death". Jonathan promised the sentence that defined him: "Whatever you desire, I will do for you." They built a test around the New Moon feast. David's seat would be empty; Jonathan would offer the Bethlehem excuse; the king's temperature would tell the truth. And before they parted, Jonathan renewed the covenant, and this time he aimed it past David at the future, at a throne he would never sit on: "And as long as I live, treat me with the LORD’s loving devotion, that I may not die, and do not ever cut off your loving devotion from my household". He set the term himself, at the far end of the revolution: "when the LORD cuts off every one of David’s enemies from the face of the earth." The prince was asking the shepherd to spare the prince's children after the revolution. David swore it. The word is hesed again, loyal kindness, the word that carried Ruth's book, passing now between the heir who would lose the kingdom and the shepherd who would gain it.

At the feast, the empty seat passed one day unremarked. On the second day Saul asked after him, and Jonathan delivered the excuse, and the king exploded at his own son with a fury that laid the whole dynasty bare: "You son of a perverse and rebellious woman! Do I not know that you have chosen the son of Jesse to your own shame and to the shame of the mother who bore you? For as long as the son of Jesse lives on this earth, neither you nor your kingdom shall be established." The math was exact and Saul knew it: David alive meant Jonathan dispossessed. When Jonathan demanded what David had done to deserve death, Saul threw his spear at his own heir. The prince left the table in fierce anger, eating nothing, grieving, the text says, over his father's shameful treatment of David. In the morning he went to the field and shot his arrows past the stone Ezel, and called to the boy the words that were really for the man hiding behind the stone: "Isn’t the arrow beyond you?" And then, with an urgency the boy could not understand: "Hurry! Make haste! Do not delay!" The boy gathered the arrows and went back to the city, and David came out of hiding and fell facedown and bowed three times, and the two of them wept together, and the text says David wept the more. Jonathan sent him away with the covenant as their last word, the promise they had sworn between themselves and their descendants: "May the LORD be a witness between you and me, and between your descendants and mine forever." Then David departed, a fugitive now for the rest of Saul's reign, and Jonathan went back into the city, to his father's table, to eat beside the man who had thrown a spear at him, carrying a secret that would outlive them both.
