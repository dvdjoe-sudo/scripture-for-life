Jonah Episode 2: Forty More Days
Jonah 3-4, Berean Standard Bible

“Then the word of the LORD came to Jonah a second time”, and the second call is the first call repeated almost word for word, as if the fish and the storm had changed nothing about the assignment: “Get up! Go to the great city of Nineveh and proclaim to it the message that I give you.” This time Jonah got up and went. The text measures the city before it lets him speak in it: “Now Nineveh was an exceedingly great city, requiring a three-day journey.” Three days just to walk its breadth. On the first day of that journey, one day in with two days of city still ahead of him, Jonah opened his mouth and delivered the whole of the message he had been given, five words in the BSB's English and not one of them softened: “Forty more days and Nineveh will be overturned!” No call to repentance is recorded. No promise is attached, no explanation given, no name of God spoken in the sermon as the book reports it. It is a sentence pronounced over a city, by a prophet who had already decided how the story should end.

What happened next is the most complete repentance in the Old Testament, and it happened in the capital of Israel's cruelest enemy, in response to the shortest sermon in it. “the Ninevites believed God. They proclaimed a fast and dressed in sackcloth, from the greatest of them to the least.” The belief ran downhill from no one; it began everywhere at once, and when the word reached the king of Nineveh, whose name the text never gives, he answered with his body before he answered with his throne: he got up from the throne, took off his royal robe, covered himself with sackcloth, and sat down in ashes. Then he made the city's grief a decree, and the decree reaches even the animals: “Let no man or beast, herd or flock, taste anything at all. They must not eat or drink.” “let both man and beast be covered with sackcloth, and have everyone call out earnestly to God. Let each one turn from his evil ways and from the violence in his hands.” The king's theology, from a pagan throne, is a question laid over the whole fast: “Who knows? God may turn and relent; He may turn from His fierce anger, so that we will not perish.” And God saw it, “that they had turned from their evil ways”, and the book says the rest in one unadorned sentence: “He relented from the disaster He had threatened to bring upon them.” The sentence was not carried out. The prophet's five words had done what he never wanted them to do.

The next verse of the book is a turn so sharp that readers have stumbled on it for as long as the book has been read: “Jonah, however, was greatly displeased, and he became angry.” Angry at a city's rescue. And now, at last, in a prayer, the book gives the reason for the flight to Tarshish that it withheld for three chapters, in Jonah's own words: “O LORD, is this not what I said while I was still in my own country? This is why I was so quick to flee toward Tarshish. I knew that You are a gracious and compassionate God, slow to anger, abounding in loving devotion”, “One who relents from sending disaster.” Those are Israel's own creed words, the confession spoken of God since Sinai, and Jonah hurls them back as an indictment: he had not run because he feared Nineveh. He had run because he feared this, that God would be God, to the wrong people. His prayer ends where Elijah's once did under a tree in the wilderness, asking to die: “And now, O LORD, please take my life from me, for it is better for me to die than to live.” God's whole answer, this time, is a question: “Have you any right to be angry?”

Jonah did not answer it. He left the city and sat down east of it, built himself a shelter, and settled into its shade “to see what would happen to the city”, a man camped in hope that the sentence he had preached might still fall. Then the appointments began again, the same word the book used for the fish. “the LORD God appointed a vine, and it grew up to provide shade over Jonah’s head to ease his discomfort, and Jonah was greatly pleased with the plant”, the only time in the book the prophet is recorded as glad about anything. At dawn the next day God appointed a worm, and the worm attacked the plant and it withered. As the sun rose God appointed a scorching east wind, and the sun beat down on Jonah's uncovered head until he grew faint and wished to die for the second time in the chapter: “It is better for me to die than to live.” God asked him the question again, aimed now at the small thing: “Have you any right to be angry about the plant?” Jonah answered this one, and his answer is the angriest line in the book: “I am angry enough to die!”

Then God drew the comparison the whole story had been building, between a plant and a city, a night's growth and 120,000 lives: “You cared about the plant, which you neither tended nor made grow. It sprang up in a night and perished in a night.” “So should I not care about the great city of Nineveh, which has more than 120,000 people who cannot tell their right hand from their left, and many cattle as well?” That question is the last sentence of the book of Jonah. Jonah never answers it, and the text never answers it for him. The prophet who preached five words and converted a city is left sitting in the sun outside its walls, holding his anger, while the final word belongs to the God he had accused of being too merciful, asking whether mercy over a city full of people who cannot tell right from left is not exactly what He should care about. The book closes the cover on the question and leaves it standing, addressed to everyone who reads it.
