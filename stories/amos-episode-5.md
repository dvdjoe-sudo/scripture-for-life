Amos Episode 5: No Escape, and the Booth of David
Amos 9, Berean Standard Bible

The last vision of the book is not shown to Amos at a distance. “I saw the Lord standing beside the altar”, and the first words of the vision are a demolition order spoken from inside the sanctuary itself: “Strike the tops of the pillars so that the thresholds shake. Topple them on the heads of all the people, and I will kill the rest with the sword. None of those who flee will get away; none of the fugitives will escape.” Every earlier vision had a frame around it, locusts in a field, a fire, a plumb line, a basket. This one is a building coming down on the worshipers, commanded by the God they had come to worship, and the theme the whole vision sounds is the theme the episode is named for: there is nowhere left to run. The book that began with a roar from Zion ends its visions in the one place Israel believed was safest, and the altar gives no cover.

What follows is the most relentless pursuit passage in the prophets, and it is quoted at its own length because its accumulation is its meaning: “Though they dig down to Sheol, from there My hand will take them; and though they climb up to heaven, from there I will pull them down.” “Though they hide themselves atop Carmel, there I will track them and seize them; and though they hide from Me at the bottom of the sea, there I will command the serpent to bite them.” Down into the grave, up into the sky, to the summit of the green mountain, to the floor of the sea, even into captivity itself, where the sword is commanded to find them there too. It closes on a sentence that reverses one of Scripture's warmest assurances and means to: “I will fix My eyes upon them for harm and not for good.” The watching of God, which Israel sang about as protection, is here announced as pursuit. No prophet in the run has yet spoken of judgment with nowhere outside it; Jonah ran to the sea and found God already there, but he found Him appointing a fish, not a serpent.

Then, in the middle of the pursuit, the book stops for its third and last doxology, as if to say who it is that cannot be escaped: “He who touches the earth and it melts, and all its dwellers mourn”, the land rising and sinking like the Nile under His hand. “He builds His upper rooms in the heavens and founds His vault upon the earth. He summons the waters of the sea and pours them over the face of the earth. The LORD is His name.” And it is this God who then asks Israel the question that quietly removes the book's last remaining shelter, the assumption that the exodus made Israel untouchable: “Are you not like the Cushites to Me, O children of Israel?” declares the LORD. “Did I not bring Israel up from the land of Egypt, the Philistines from Caphtor, and the Arameans from Kir?” Other nations have exoduses too. Philistines and Arameans, Israel's oldest enemies, were also brought up and settled by the same hand, and election was never a fence. Yet inside the same breath the sentence carries its own limit, stated as an oath: “Yet I will not utterly destroy the house of Jacob,” declares the LORD. The sifting image that follows explains the difference: “I will shake the house of Israel among all the nations as grain is sifted in a sieve; but not a pebble will reach the ground.” Shaken among the nations like grain in a sieve, everything loose falling through, and not one solid kernel lost. The sinners who said disaster would never draw near die by the sword in the same oracle. The sieve is not mercy for everyone; it is precision, a judgment that can tell grain from rubble.

Then the book turns, and the turn is total. The last oracle of Amos begins with a day and a ruin: “In that day I will restore the fallen tent of David. I will repair its gaps, restore its ruins, and rebuild it as in the days of old”. David's house, divided since Rehoboam and fallen to a tent, a booth, a shepherd's shelter in this book's own vocabulary, is to be raised and mended, so that the restored people possess the remnant of Edom and all the nations that bear God's name. The harvest that follows is described in impossibilities: “the plowman will overtake the reaper and the treader of grapes, the sower of seed. The mountains will drip with sweet wine, with which all the hills will flow.” The crops come faster than they can be gathered, the sowing catches up with the harvest, abundance measured as a calendar collapsing. And the people of the sentence: “they will rebuild and inhabit the ruined cities. They will plant vineyards and drink their wine; they will make gardens and eat their fruit.” Every clause of it answers the book's own curses. In chapter five they were told they would never live in the houses they built or drink from the vineyards they planted; here they rebuild and inhabit, plant and drink, garden and eat. The exile beyond Damascus is reversed, not merely ended.

The book of Amos ends on a planting. The same voice that roared from Zion in the first verse, that summoned Israel to prepare to meet its God, that swore it would never forget a single deed, speaks the final sentence of the book, and the final sentence is a promise about roots: “I will firmly plant them in their own land, never again to be uprooted from the land that I have given them,” says the LORD your God. Planted is the last image, and uprooted is the last threat, and the threat is the one denied. No answer from Israel is recorded. The book closes the way it opened, on God's voice carrying the whole weight, but the voice that began by withering the summit of Carmel ends by planting a people in their own soil for good, and the shepherd who carried the word goes unnamed in its final lines, his work done, the word outliving the messenger exactly as he said it would.
