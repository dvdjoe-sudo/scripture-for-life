2 Samuel Episode 2: King of Judah, War with the House of Saul
2 Samuel 2 to 4, Berean Standard Bible

The lament was sung. Then David did what he had done at every hinge of his life since the cave of Adullam: he inquired of the LORD. Should he go up to one of the towns of Judah? The answer was yes. Where? To Hebron. So the fugitive went up out of the ashes of Ziklag with his two wives, Ahinoam and Abigail, and his six hundred men with their households, and settled in the towns around Hebron, Abraham's old city. And the men of Judah came and anointed David king over the house of Judah. It was a partial crown, one tribe out of twelve, and David's first act with it tells you the kind of king he meant to be. He was told it was the men of Jabesh-gilead who had buried Saul, the town across the Jordan that Saul had rescued in his first flush of kingship and that had now repaid the debt at the risk of their lives. David sent them a blessing in the name of the LORD and a promise in his own: because they had shown kindness to Saul their lord, the LORD would show them loving devotion and faithfulness, and David would show them the same favor. "Now then, be strong and courageous, for though Saul your lord is dead, the house of Judah has anointed me as their king." Before he fought the house of Saul, he honored the men who had buried its head.

But Saul had a son, and Saul had a general. Abner son of Ner, commander of Saul's army, took Ish-bosheth across the Jordan to Mahanaim and made him king over the north and east, over Gilead and Ephraim and Benjamin, over all Israel except Judah. Ish-bosheth was forty years old and reigned two years, though the war dragged on far longer than his reign, and the text gives the whole conflict its summary in a single sentence: the war between the house of Saul and the house of David was protracted, and David grew stronger and stronger, while the house of Saul grew weaker and weaker. Ish-bosheth was a king the way a flag is an army. Abner was the power, and everyone in both camps knew it.

The war's ugliest day began as a game. The two armies met at the pool of Gibeon and sat down on opposite sides of the water, and Abner proposed to Joab, David's commander, that the young men get up and compete before them. Twelve against twelve came forward, and each man grabbed his opponent by the head and drove his sword into his opponent's side, and all twenty-four fell together in a place named ever after the Field of Blades. Then the real battle joined, and Abner's Israelites broke and ran. Among David's men that day were the three sons of Zeruiah, David's nephews: Joab, Abishai, and Asahel, the youngest, fleet of foot like a wild gazelle. Asahel fixed on Abner himself and ran him down and would not turn aside to the right or to the left. Twice Abner warned him off, and the second warning was almost a plea, because Abner could see the whole future in this chase: "Stop chasing me. Why should I strike you to the ground? How could I show my face to your brother Joab?" Asahel refused to turn away, and Abner drove the butt of his spear backward into his stomach, and the young man fell dead on the spot, and every soldier who came to the place where he lay stopped and stood still. Joab and Abishai hunted Abner until sunset, when Abner rallied his Benjamites on a hilltop and called across the dark the truest words spoken in that war: "Must the sword devour forever? Do you not realize that this will only end in bitterness?" Joab answered that had Abner not spoken, the pursuit would have run till morning, and he blew the ram's horn, and the armies separated. Joab counted his missing: nineteen men, and Asahel. They buried Asahel in his father's tomb in Bethlehem and marched all night, reaching Hebron at daybreak. But the account between Joab and Abner was not closed by any trumpet. It had merely been opened.

While the war ground on, sons were born to David in Hebron, and the narrator lists six of them by six mothers in the flat tone of a census. Hold the names, because this book will: the firstborn Amnon, and the third son Absalom, and the fourth Adonijah. The succession that would tear David's house apart was being born in the same years he was winning it. The end of the war, when it came, began not with a battle but with an insult. Ish-bosheth accused Abner of taking Rizpah, Saul's concubine, a charge that amounted to claiming the dead king's throne rights. Abner exploded. All this time he had been loyal to the house of Saul, he said, and now he was accused over a woman; so he swore to do for David what the LORD had sworn to him, to transfer the kingdom from the house of Saul and set David's throne over Israel and Judah from Dan to Beersheba. Ish-bosheth did not dare say another word to him. The king of Israel had just learned who his king was.

Abner's messengers came to David proposing a covenant, and David agreed, with one requirement that reached back across the whole story: Abner was not to appear before him unless he brought Michal, Saul's daughter, David's first wife, the woman who had lowered him in a basket through a window and saved his life, whom Saul had since given to another man as a political erasure. She was taken from her husband Paltiel, who followed her weeping all the way to Bahurim until Abner told him to go back, and he went. Abner then did the politics, conferring with the elders of Israel and with his own tribe of Benjamin, and came to Hebron with twenty men to a feast in David's house, and left in peace with a promise to gather all Israel to the king. Hours later Joab returned from a raid and learned that Abner had been in Hebron and had been sent away in peace, and he went to the king in fury: Abner had come to deceive him, to track his movements. Then, without David's knowledge, Joab sent messengers to recall Abner, and at the gateway of Hebron he pulled him aside as if for a private word and stabbed him in the stomach. The text names the motive without varnish: Abner died on account of the blood of Joab's brother Asahel.

David's response was a campaign of innocence conducted in public. He declared himself and his kingdom forever guiltless before the LORD of Abner's blood, and laid a curse on the house of Joab with the particularity of a curse meant to be quoted. He ordered Joab himself and all the people to tear their clothes and put on sackcloth and walk in mourning, and the king walked behind the bier, and wept aloud at the tomb, and sang a lament whose sting was that Abner had died unbound and unresisting: "As a man falls before the wicked, so also you fell." He refused food until sundown under oath. And all the people took note, and all Israel was convinced that the king had no part in the murder of Abner. Then David said the sentence that hangs over the rest of his reign, to his own servants, about the nephews he could neither punish nor dismiss: "And I am weak this day, though anointed as king, and these men, the sons of Zeruiah, are too fierce for me. May the LORD repay the evildoer according to his evil!" A king who cannot bring his own commander to justice has announced the limit of his crown, and this book remembers it.

What remained of the house of Saul collapsed within days. When Ish-bosheth heard that Abner was dead, he lost courage, and two of his own raiding captains, Rechab and Baanah, came into his house in the heat of the day while he slept his midday sleep, stabbed him, cut off his head, and carried it all night down the Arabah road to Hebron, expecting the same reward the Amalekite had expected. David answered them with the Amalekite's story. When a man told him Saul was dead, thinking he brought good news, David had seized him and put him to death at Ziklag; that was his reward for his news. "As surely as the LORD lives, who has redeemed my life from all distress", how much more would he require the blood of wicked men who had killed a righteous man in his own house and on his own bed? The brothers were executed, their bodies displayed by the pool in Hebron, and Ish-bosheth's head was buried with honor in Abner's tomb. On the way to that tomb the narrator pauses for four verses of pure setup, and they matter more than the battle reports: Jonathan son of Saul had a son who was lame in his feet. He was five years old when the news came from Jezreel, and his nurse had fled with him and dropped him in the hurry, and he was lame from that day. His name was Mephibosheth. The house of Saul now consisted of a dead king, a murdered pretender, and a crippled boy in hiding. The throne of Israel stood open.
