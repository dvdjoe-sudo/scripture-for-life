2 Samuel Episode 9: The Forest of Ephraim, and a Father's Cry
2 Samuel 18 to 20, Berean Standard Bible

David reviewed his troops at Mahanaim and divided them in thirds, under Joab, under Abishai, and under Ittai the Gittite, the foreigner of a week's loyalty now commanding a third of the kingdom's army. The king announced that he would march out with them himself, and the army talked him out of it on grounds that were love and arithmetic at once: if they fled, no one would care; if half of them died, no one would care; but David was worth ten thousand of them, and the enemy's whole war was one man's death. So David stood beside the gate while the army marched out by hundreds and by thousands, and in the hearing of every commander and, the text is careful to say, of all the people, he gave the order that frames the day: "Treat the young man Absalom gently for my sake." Then the battle was joined in the forest of Ephraim, and the slaughter was great, twenty thousand men, and the battle spread over the whole countryside, and the narrator delivers the day's epitaph in a sentence no historian would dare invent: that day the forest devoured more people than the sword.

Absalom the forest took personally. Riding his mule through the trees in the rout, he met some of David's servants, and as the mule passed under the thick branches of a large oak, his head was caught fast in the tree, and the mule kept going, and the king's son hung suspended in midair. The book had once paused a whole paragraph over that head, the flawless beauty of it, the hair sheared yearly and weighed at two hundred shekels; now the head is the snare. A soldier saw him hanging and reported it to Joab, and Joab's response tells you the war he thought he was fighting: why had the man not struck Absalom to the ground on the spot; there would have been ten shekels of silver and a warrior's belt in it. The soldier's refusal is the moral summit of the chapter. Even if a thousand shekels were weighed into his hands, he said, he would not raise his hand against the son of the king, because in their hearing the king had commanded Joab and Abishai and Ittai: "Protect the young man Absalom for my sake." And he added the clause that measured Joab exactly: if he had done it and betrayed his own life, nothing being hidden from the king, Joab would have abandoned him. Joab announced that he was not going to wait like this, took three spears in his hand, and thrust them through the heart of Absalom while he was still alive in the oak. Ten of Joab's armor-bearers finished it. The trumpet blew, the pursuit stopped, and they took Absalom down and threw him into a large pit in the forest and piled a huge mound of stones over him, the burial of an executed criminal. In his lifetime Absalom had set up a pillar for himself in the King's Valley near Jerusalem, because he said he had no son to preserve the memory of his name, though this same book records three sons born to him and does not stop to explain the difference. The pillar still stands in the telling with his name on it. The man is under stones in a pit, in a forest, unwept by the army that marched home without him.

David sat between the two gates of Mahanaim waiting for the news, and the book slows down to the pace of a watchman's eyes. A runner alone: good news, said the king, if he is alone, he bears good news. A second runner behind him. The watchman recognized the first man's stride, Ahimaaz son of Zadok, and David said he was a good man and came with good news. Ahimaaz had begged Joab for the run over Joab's refusal, Joab knowing precisely what the news would do to the king, and the young priest arrived crying that all was well, and blessed the LORD who had delivered up the men who raised their hands against the king. Then David asked the only question he had asked all day: "Is the young man Absalom all right?" Ahimaaz, who knew, chose a fog: when Joab sent him he had seen a great tumult, but he did not know what it was. The king told him to step aside, and the Cushite came, the foreign messenger Joab had sent first with the whole truth, and delivered it in the formula of victories. David asked him the same question, and the Cushite answered with a courtier's terrible precision: "May the enemies of my lord the king and all who rise up against you to harm you be like that young man." The king was shaken. He went up to the chamber over the gate and wept, and as he walked he cried out the words that have never stopped echoing: "O my son Absalom! My son, my son Absalom! If only I had died instead of you, O Absalom, my son, my son!" The kingdom had been saved in a day. The victory was total. And the king's grief filled the gatehouse where the whole army would have to pass under it.

It turned the victory into mourning for all the people. The army that had saved the king's life and throne came back into the city quietly, stealing in like men humiliated after a rout, while the king covered his face and cried the lament at the top of his voice. Then Joab went in to him, the indispensable, unforgivable commander, and performed the last service of his brutal loyalty: he shamed the king back onto his throne. Today, he said, David had disgraced the servants who had saved his life and his family's lives. "You love those who hate you and hate those who love you!" He had made it plain that commanders and soldiers meant nothing to him, and had Absalom lived and all of them died, it would have pleased him. Then the warning, sworn by the LORD: "if you do not go out, not a man will remain with you tonight", and that would be worse than all the adversity of his life from his youth until now. Every word of the rebuke was insubordinate and every word was true, and the king heard it. He rose, washed the grief off his public face, and sat in the gate, and all the people came before him. A king could mourn his son. He could not invoice his army for the mourning.

The restoration that followed was a second coronation conducted in arguments. Israel, Absalom dead, remembered David's rescues and began asking why no one spoke of restoring the king. David moved first on Judah, his own tribe, asking through the priests why his brothers should be last to bring him home, and he sweetened the summons with a political appointment that would cost more blood: Amasa, Absalom's commander, his own nephew, was to be commander of the army from now on in place of Joab. Judah came down to the Jordan as one man, and at the crossing the whole tangled past queued up to be settled. Shimei came first, a thousand Benjamites behind him, fell before the king, confessed his sin and begged that it not be held against him, and Abishai asked why this man who had cursed the LORD's anointed should not die, and David answered in the idiom of a king secure enough for mercy: should any man be put to death in Israel today? "Am I not indeed aware that today I am king over Israel?" And to Shimei he swore an oath: "You shall not die." Then Mephibosheth came down, and his appearance testified before he spoke: he had not cared for his feet or trimmed his mustache or washed his clothes from the day the king left until the day he returned. His account contradicted Ziba's in every particular. Being lame, he had ordered his donkey saddled to ride with the king; his servant had deceived him, and then slandered him to the king in his absence. The king, the text observes, was like the angel of God to do what was good in his eyes. David cut the hearing short, why say any more, and split the estate between them, a king's compromise that decided everything except the truth. Mephibosheth's answer closes his story with the grace that had first brought him to the table: "let Ziba take it all", since his lord the king had come safely to his own house. Last came Barzillai, eighty years old, who had provisioned the court at Mahanaim, invited now to live out his days fed at the king's side in Jerusalem. His refusal is the gentlest speech in the book: how many years had he left that he should go up with the king? At eighty, could he discern what was good and what was not, could he taste what he ate or drank, could he still hear the voices of singing men and women? Why should your servant be an added burden to my lord the king? He crossed a little way over the Jordan, commended his son Chimham to the king's generosity, received David's kiss and blessing, and went home to die near the tomb of his father and mother.

Even restored, the kingdom cracked along its oldest seam in the homeward crowd itself, Israel charging Judah with stealing the king, Judah answering with kinship and insult, until at the river's far end a Benjamite named Sheba son of Bichri blew the ram's horn and shouted the slogan that would outlive David's house: "We have no share in David, no inheritance in Jesse's son. Every man to his tent, O Israel!" Israel deserted to Sheba, and Judah escorted its king home to a palace where the ten concubines were taken and placed in a guarded house, provided for and never visited again, confined until the day of their death, living as widows, the human residue of a sentence pronounced in a prophet's quiet room. Amasa, given three days to muster Judah, missed his deadline, and David sent the pursuit under Abishai, fearing Sheba would do more harm than Absalom. At the great stone in Gibeon, Amasa joined the column, and Joab came forward to greet his replacement, dressed for war with a dagger strapped that slipped conveniently from its sheath. "Are you well, my brother?" he asked, and took Amasa by the beard with his right hand to kiss him, and with the other drove the dagger into his stomach, and Amasa wallowed in his blood in the middle of the road while the army halted around the body until a man dragged it aside and covered it, because no column could march past it. It was Abner again, and Asahel's blood again, and David nowhere in the scene, the king's appointment undone by the king's uncommandable commander. Joab pursued Sheba to Abel-beth-maacah at the kingdom's northern edge and battered the wall, and there the revolt was ended not by Joab but by a wise woman calling down from the city, the second wise woman of the book, who named her city a mother in Israel and asked why Joab would swallow up the LORD's inheritance. Joab disclaimed any wish to destroy; deliver Sheba alone, and he would depart. "his head will be thrown to you over the wall", she promised, and she went to her people with her wise counsel, and it was. The trumpet blew, the army dispersed home, and Joab returned to Jerusalem and to the post the chapter's final list confirms as if nothing had happened: Joab was over the whole army of Israel. David was king again over a united kingdom, and the sons of Zeruiah remained, as he had confessed a lifetime earlier, too fierce for him.
