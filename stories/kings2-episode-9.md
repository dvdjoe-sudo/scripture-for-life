2 Kings Episode 9: The Temple Repaired, and Elisha's Last Arrow
2 Kings 12-13, Berean Standard Bible

The hidden child grew up to be a temple builder, with a qualifier the book attaches to his whole reign: “Joash did what was right in the eyes of the LORD all the days he was instructed by Jehoiada the priest.” All the days of the instruction, not longer. Even so, “the high places were not removed; the people continued sacrificing and burning incense there.” Joash's great work was the repair of the house his great-grandfather's generation had let decay and Athaliah's sons had plundered. His first system failed: the priests collected the sacred money for twenty-three years and the damage went unrepaired. The king confronted them, “Why have you not repaired the damage to the temple?” and built a simpler machine, a chest with a hole bored in its lid beside the altar, the money counted by the royal scribe and the high priest together, and paid straight to the workmen, the carpenters, builders, masons, and stonecutters. The book pauses on the honesty of the arrangement with a sentence any treasurer would envy: “No accounting was required from the men who received the money to pay the workmen, because they acted with integrity.” The repaired temple outlasted its repairer. When Hazael of Aram took Gath and turned toward Jerusalem, Joash bought him off with the temple's dedicated gold, his fathers' and his own, and Hazael withdrew. The boy Jehoiada had crowned was assassinated by his own servants at Beth-millo, and his son Amaziah took the throne.

In the north, Jehu's son Jehoahaz inherited a kingdom being ground down by that same Hazael, until his army was a remnant the text counts like small change: “Jehoahaz had no army left, except fifty horsemen, ten chariots, and ten thousand foot soldiers”, because Aram “had destroyed them and made them like the dust at threshing.” Jehoahaz sought the favor of the LORD, “and the LORD listened to him because He saw the oppression that the king of Aram had inflicted on Israel.” “So the LORD gave Israel a deliverer, and they escaped the power of the Arameans.” The deliverer is never named, and the relief did not reform the relieved: the Asherah pole still stood in Samaria. Jehoash, his son, reigned next, an evil king in the book's verdict, and it is his reign that carries the series' anchor to his deathbed.

Elisha's last scene mirrors his first. When the prophet lay sick with the illness from which he would die, King Jehoash came down to him and wept over him with the exact words Elisha had cried at Elijah's departure: “My father, my father, the chariots and horsemen of Israel!” Elisha's dying act was a piece of prophetic theater. He had the king take a bow, put his own hands over the king's hands on it, and shoot out the east window toward Aram, declaring: “This is the LORD’s arrow of victory, the arrow of victory over Aram, for you shall strike the Arameans in Aphek until you have put an end to them.” Then he told the king to take the arrows and strike the ground. The king struck three times and stopped, politely, moderately, the way kings do. The dying prophet's anger is the last emotion of his ministry: “You should have struck the ground five or six times. Then you would have struck down Aram until you had put an end to it. But now you will strike down Aram only three times.” The measure of the victory had been set by the measure of the striking, and Jehoash had rationed his own deliverance. Elisha died and was buried, and even his grave would not stay out of the work: a burial party, surprised by Moabite raiders, threw their dead man into Elisha's tomb in their hurry, and “as soon as his body touched the bones of Elisha, the man was revived and stood up on his feet.”

Jehoash went on to defeat Ben-hadad three times and recover the cities Hazael had taken, three victories, exactly the number of the arrows, no more. The chapter frames those years with a sentence that explains the whole era of the book, why a kingdom this faithless was still standing at all: “the LORD was gracious to Israel and had compassion on them”, “because of His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.” “And to this day, the LORD has been unwilling to destroy them or cast them from His presence.” The anchor of the series so far is buried. From here the narrator carries the kings alone, and the road turns down.
