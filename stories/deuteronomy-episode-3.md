Deuteronomy Episode 3: One Altar, and Laws for Settled Life
Deuteronomy 12 to 16, Berean Standard Bible

The second address had begun at the mountain. Now Moses turned from the mountain to the land itself, to towns his hearers did not yet own, gates they had never walked through, fields they had never planted. Almost everything in these chapters is future tense. He was describing a settled life to a camp, and the first statute he gave them was about where God would be worshiped.

"Destroy completely all the places where the nations you are dispossessing have served their gods". Their worship was everywhere, he said, on the high mountains, on the hills, under every green tree. Israel was to tear down the altars, smash the sacred pillars, burn the Asherah poles, cut down the idols, and wipe out the very names of those gods from every place. "You shall not worship the LORD your God in this way."

Israel's worship would gather instead of scatter. "You must seek the place the LORD your God will choose from among all your tribes to establish as a dwelling for His Name, and there you must go." To that one place they would bring the burnt offerings, the sacrifices, the tithes, the vowed gifts and the freewill gifts, the firstborn of herd and flock. And the gathering was not grim. "There, in the presence of the LORD your God, you and your households shall eat and rejoice in all you do, because the LORD your God has blessed you."

The Levite inside the gates was written into the rejoicing by name, the man with no portion or inheritance of his own. Twice in the chapter Moses stopped to say it again: rejoice, you and your sons and daughters and your servants, "and the Levite within your gates, since he has no portion or inheritance among you." And near the end, a warning shaped like a kindness: "Be careful not to neglect the Levites as long as you live in your land."

None of it could start yet. "You are not to do as we are doing here today, where everyone does what seems right in his own eyes." The camp still worshiped where it stood. "For you have not yet come to the resting place and the inheritance that the LORD your God is giving you." The one altar waited on the far side of the Jordan, on the far side of the wars, in the rest he said God would give them from all the enemies around them.

Daily meat, though, would not need the journey. When a household craved meat, it could slaughter and eat within its own gates, clean and unclean together, the way a man eats a gazelle or a deer. One rule traveled home with the meat. "Only be sure not to eat the blood, because the blood is the life, and you must not eat the life with the meat." The blood was poured out on the ground like water. If the chosen place proved too far for the holy portions, those still went up: "But you are to take your holy things and your vow offerings and go to the place the LORD will choose."

Then Moses closed the chapter with the danger that comes after victory, and it was not an army. When the nations were cut off and Israel lived in their land, the temptation would be curiosity. "Do not inquire about their gods, asking," and here Moses put the question in their mouths before they could ask it: "How do these nations serve their gods? I will do likewise."

"You must not worship the LORD your God in this way". The reason was not that the old worship was weak or out of date. "They practice for their gods every abomination which the LORD hates. They even burn their sons and daughters in the fire as sacrifices to their gods." And over the whole chapter he set the seal he had set over the first address: "See that you do everything I command you; do not add to it or subtract from it."

Chapter 13 kept the same fight and moved it indoors. A prophet might arise, or a dreamer of dreams, and announce a sign or a wonder, and the sign might come true. Accuracy was not the test. If the voice behind the wonder said, "Let us follow other gods" and "let us worship them," Israel was not to listen. "For the LORD your God is testing you to find out whether you love Him with all your heart and with all your soul."

The answer to a working wonder was not a bigger wonder. It was a walk. "You are to follow the LORD your God and fear Him. Keep His commandments and listen to His voice; serve Him and hold fast to Him." The prophet himself was to die, Moses said, because he had advocated rebellion against the God who brought them out of Egypt, and the evil was to be purged from among them.

Then the test came closer than a prophet. It might come from "your very own brother, or your son or daughter, or the wife you embrace, or your closest friend", whispering in secret the same sentence: "Let us go and worship other gods". Moses did not soften what obedience would cost there. No yielding, no listening, no pity, no sparing, no shielding. The hand of the one enticed was to be first against him, and then the hands of all the people. "Then all Israel will hear and be afraid, and will never again do such a wicked thing among you."

The last case in the chapter was a whole city led astray by wicked men from among its own people, and here the law slowed down before it struck. A report was not enough. "Then you must inquire, investigate, and interrogate thoroughly." Only if the thing was established with certainty did the sword fall, on the people and the livestock together, and the city and all its plunder burned as a whole offering, left "a mound of ruins forever, never to be rebuilt." And a small, strange mercy was buried in the ashes: "Nothing devoted to destruction shall cling to your hands". No souvenirs from an apostate city. No profit from its fall.

Chapter 14 opened with a name before it gave a rule. "You are sons of the LORD your God". On the strength of that name, Israel was not to gash itself with the mourning cuts of its neighbors or shave the forehead for the dead, "for you are a people holy to the LORD your God. The LORD has chosen you to be a people for His prized possession out of all the peoples on the face of the earth."

The food list followed, the one Leviticus had already given, and Moses carried it the way it had been carried there, in a run: the ox, the sheep, the goat, the deer and the gazelle; anything with a split hoof that chews the cud; the camel, the rabbit, and the rock badger named as falling short of the rule; the pig, unclean; fish with fins and scales; the clean birds against the long roll of the unclean, eagle and vulture and owl and stork and bat; nothing that had died on its own on the table, though it could be given or sold to the foreigner inside the gates. And a kitchen rule with no reason attached, stated flat and left standing: "You must not cook a young goat in its mother's milk."

Then the chapter turned the tithe into a meal. A tenth of every year's produce was to be set aside and eaten by the family itself, at the chosen place, "so that you may learn to fear the LORD your God always." And Moses had already met the objection forming in a farmer's mind, the one who lived too far away to carry what God had blessed him with. Convert it. "Then exchange it for money, take the money in your hand, and go to the place the LORD your God will choose." There, at the far end of the journey, the money turned back into a feast. "Then you may spend the money on anything you desire: cattle, sheep, wine, strong drink, or anything you wish. You are to feast there in the presence of the LORD your God and rejoice with your household."

Every third year the tithe never left town. It was laid up inside the gates, and the guest list was the land's exposed people: "Then the Levite (because he has no portion or inheritance among you), the foreigner, the fatherless, and the widow within your gates may come and eat and be satisfied." The promise attached to it was not small: "And the LORD your God will bless you in all the work of your hands."

Chapter 15 set the calendar underneath Israel's money. "At the end of every seven years you must cancel debts." Every creditor was to release what he had loaned to his neighbor, because the LORD's time of release had been proclaimed. A foreigner's debt could still be collected. A brother's could not. And Moses said the thing the release was built to make true: "There will be no poor among you". He tied it to the blessing on the land and to one condition, spoken in his own voice: "If only you obey the LORD your God and are careful to follow all these commandments I am giving you today." A people under that law, he said, would lend to many nations and borrow from none.

Then, in the same speech, he legislated for the poor man standing in the gate anyway. "If there is a poor man among your brothers within any of the gates in the land that the LORD your God is giving you, then you are not to harden your heart or shut your hand from your poor brother." The hand had its own instruction: "Instead, you are to open your hand to him and freely loan him whatever he needs."

Moses named the thought that would argue against the loan, and he named it wicked. As the seventh year approached, a lender's heart would do the arithmetic: "The seventh year, the year of release, is near". A debt about to be canceled is a gift wearing a loan's clothes, and the begrudging look that followed it would be answered in heaven: the poor brother would cry out to the LORD, and the guilt would land on the closed hand. So the command ran the other way. Give generously, and do not let your heart be grieved when you do. And then Moses set the two sentences of his own chapter side by side and harmonized neither of them: "For there will never cease to be poor in the land; that is why I am commanding you to open wide your hand to your brother and to the poor and needy in your land."

The seventh year released people as well as debts. A fellow Hebrew, man or woman, who had been sold into service served six years and went free in the seventh, and freedom was not to be an empty-handed push out the door. "You are to furnish him liberally from your flock, your threshing floor, and your winepress. You shall give to him as the LORD your God has blessed you." The reason underneath it was the one reason Deuteronomy never gets tired of: "Remember that you were slaves in the land of Egypt, and the LORD your God redeemed you; that is why I am giving you this command today."

And the law left room for the servant who would not go. If he said he did not want to leave, because he loved the household and was well off in it, his ear was pierced with an awl against the door, and he stayed for life, his by choice. "Do not regard it as a hardship to set your servant free," Moses added to the masters, "because his six years of service were worth twice the wages of a hired hand."

The chapter closed where the chapters of settled life kept closing, at the altar and the table. Every firstborn male of herd and flock belonged to the LORD: not worked, not shorn, eaten year by year with the household at the chosen place. A blemished one, lame or blind, was never to be sacrificed, only eaten at home, clean and unclean together. And the blood, as always, was poured out on the ground like water.

Chapter 16 laid the year out as three journeys. The first came in the month of Abib, "because in the month of Abib the LORD your God brought you out of Egypt by night." Passover was no longer a meal any town could hold on its own. "You are not to sacrifice the Passover animal in any of the towns that the LORD your God is giving you." It was offered only at the chosen place, in the evening as the sun set, at the same hour of day Israel had walked out of Egypt, and in the morning the families returned to their tents. With it, seven days of unleavened bread, and Moses gave the bread its name: "the bread of affliction, because you left the land of Egypt in haste". Its work was memory. Eat it, he said, "so that you may remember for the rest of your life the day you left the land of Egypt."

Seven weeks from the first swing of the sickle came the Feast of Weeks, its offering a freewill gift measured against the blessing each family had actually received. The rejoicing roll was read out again, sons and daughters, servants, the Levite, and the foreigner, the fatherless, and the widows among you, and underneath the feast Moses set the same foundation stone: "Remember that you were slaves in Egypt, and carefully follow these statutes."

Last came Tabernacles, seven days after the threshing floor and the winepress had given up their produce, the harvest home and the year full. Moses promised the mood outright: "The LORD your God will bless you in all your produce and in all the work of your hands, so that your joy will be complete."

Three times a year, then, the men of Israel were to appear before the LORD at the chosen place, at Unleavened Bread, at Weeks, at Tabernacles. "No one should appear before the LORD empty-handed." But the gift was scaled to the giver: "Everyone must appear with a gift as he is able, according to the blessing the LORD your God has given you." The poor man and the rich man climbed the same road. What they carried was measured by what they had been given.

And then, in the last breath of the section, Moses turned from altars and feasts to the men who would have to guard all of it. "You are to appoint judges and officials for your tribes in every town that the LORD your God is giving you. They are to judge the people with righteous judgment." No bending of justice, no partiality, and no bribes, "for a bribe blinds the eyes of the wise and twists the words of the righteous." The sentence he left ringing over the courts was five words deep: "Pursue justice, and justice alone, so that you may live, and you may possess the land that the LORD your God is giving you."

One altar, one table kept clean, one calendar of feasts, one open hand. And beside the altar, a final small warning, easy to walk past: do not plant an Asherah pole next to it, and do not set up a sacred pillar, "which the LORD your God hates." What those judges would face, the cases too hard for a town gate, and the king Israel would one day ask for, were next in Moses' speech.
