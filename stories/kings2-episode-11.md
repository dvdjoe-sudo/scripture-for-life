2 Kings Episode 11: Samaria Falls
2 Kings 17, Berean Standard Bible

Hoshea, who took the throne by murdering Pekah, was Israel's last king, and the book's faint grading follows him to the end: “he did evil in the sight of the LORD, but not like the kings of Israel who preceded him.” He began as Assyria's vassal, paying tribute to Shalmaneser, and then conspired, sending envoys to King So of Egypt and withholding the tribute. The Assyrian king arrested him, chained him in prison, and invaded the whole land, and Samaria held out under siege for three years. In Hoshea's ninth year the city fell, and the deportation made permanent what the raids of the last episode had begun: “the king of Assyria captured Samaria and carried away the Israelites to Assyria, where he settled them in Halah, in Gozan by the Habor River, and in the cities of the Medes.” The northern kingdom, ten tribes, two hundred years of kings from Jeroboam to Hoshea, ends in a sentence of geography: Halah, Gozan, the cities of the Medes, places the book's first readers could not have found on a map.

Then the narrator stops telling the story and renders the verdict, in the longest sustained sermon in the books of Kings, and this episode carries it as the text gives it, unsoftened. It opens with the whole explanation in one sentence: “All this happened because the people of Israel had sinned against the LORD their God, who had brought them out of the land of Egypt from under the hand of Pharaoh king of Egypt.” What follows is the catalogue the story has been compiling since the first calf. They built high places everywhere, “From watchtower to fortified city, they built high places in all their cities”, set up sacred pillars and Asherah poles “on every high hill and under every green tree”, and served idols under the one flat prohibition: “You shall not do this thing.” They did some of it in secret: “The Israelites secretly did things against the LORD their God that were not right.” And the warning system had run for centuries, through every prophet and seer, with one message: “Turn from your wicked ways and keep My commandments and statutes, according to the entire Law that I commanded your fathers and delivered to you through My servants the prophets.” “But they would not listen, and they stiffened their necks like their fathers, who did not believe the LORD their God.” The sermon's most quoted line is its coldest piece of logic: “They pursued worthless idols and became worthless themselves”. Then the full inventory lands at once: “They abandoned all the commandments of the LORD their God and made for themselves two cast idols of calves and an Asherah pole. They bowed down to all the host of heaven and served Baal.” “They sacrificed their sons and daughters in the fire and practiced divination and soothsaying.” “They devoted themselves to doing evil in the sight of the LORD”, provoking Him to anger.

The verdict follows the catalogue the way sentence follows evidence: “So the LORD was very angry with Israel, and He removed them from His presence. Only the tribe of Judah remained”, and the sermon refuses to let the surviving kingdom stand outside the dock: “even Judah did not keep the commandments of the LORD their God, but lived according to the customs Israel had introduced.” The thread that began at Shechem is gathered up by name: Jeroboam “led Israel away from following the LORD and caused them to commit a great sin”, and Israel persisted in it to the end. “Finally, the LORD removed Israel from His presence, as He had declared through all His servants the prophets. So Israel was exiled from their homeland into Assyria, where they are to this day.” The phrase matters: to this day. The book is written from the far side of the exile it is describing, and its verdict was still standing when it was written.

The chapter ends in the emptied land, with its new tenants. The king of Assyria settled peoples from Babylon, Cuthah, Avva, Hamath, and Sepharvaim in Samaria's towns, and when lions began killing the settlers, the diagnosis sent to Assyria was theological: “The peoples that you have removed and placed in the cities of Samaria do not know the requirements of the God of the land.” So one of the exiled Israelite priests was sent back to live at Bethel, of all places, Jeroboam's own shrine, to teach the newcomers how to worship the LORD. What he taught landed in soil already planted. Each nation made its own gods and set them in the shrines on the high places, Babylon's Succoth-benoth, Cuth's Nergal, Hamath's Ashima, the Avvites' Nibhaz and Tartak, and the Sepharvites burning their children to Adrammelech and Anammelech. The book's refrain for the mixture is repeated until it becomes the region's permanent description: “They worshiped the LORD, but they also served their own gods”. “To this day they are still practicing their former customs.” And the last word on the northern land belongs to the children: “So these nations worshiped the LORD but also served their idols, and to this day their children and grandchildren continue to do as their fathers did.” The kingdom of Israel is gone from the story. Judah remains, alone, with Assyria camped permanently in the world's future and a sermon now on record that has already told the reader exactly how a kingdom like this ends.
