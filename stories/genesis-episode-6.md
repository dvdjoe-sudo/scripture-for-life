Genesis Episode 6: Sodom, Isaac, and the Mountain
Genesis 18 to 22, Berean Standard Bible

The LORD appeared to Abraham near the oaks of Mamre while Abraham sat at the entrance to his tent in the heat of the day.

Abraham looked up and saw three men standing nearby. He ran to meet them, bowed low, and pressed them to stay. Water was brought to wash their feet. Abraham hurried into the tent and told Sarah to make bread from fine flour. He ran to the herd, chose a tender calf, and gave it to a servant to prepare. Then he set curds, milk, and the calf before the men under the tree, and he stood near them while they ate.

One of them said Sarah would have a son.

Sarah was listening at the tent entrance behind him. Abraham and Sarah were old, well along in years, and Sarah had passed the age of childbearing. She laughed to herself. The LORD asked Abraham why Sarah laughed and said, "Is anything too difficult for the LORD? At the appointed time I will return to you, in about a year, and Sarah will have a son."

Sarah denied laughing because she was afraid. The answer stayed firm. She had laughed.

Then the men looked toward Sodom, and Abraham walked with them to send them on their way. What followed turned Abraham from host to intercessor. The outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah was great, and their sin was very grave. The LORD would go down and see.

Abraham remained standing before the LORD. He drew near and asked whether God would sweep away the righteous with the wicked. If fifty righteous people were in the city, would God spare the whole place for their sake? Abraham said, "Far be it from You to do such a thing, to kill the righteous with the wicked, so that the righteous and the wicked are treated alike. Far be it from You! Will not the Judge of all the earth do what is right?"

The number moved downward as Abraham kept asking. Fifty. Forty-five. Forty. Thirty. Twenty. Ten. Each time, God answered that the city would be spared for the sake of the righteous found there. Abraham knew he was dust and ashes, and he kept speaking anyway.

By evening, two angels came to Sodom. Lot sat at the gate. He bowed, urged them to spend the night in his house, washed their feet, and prepared a meal. Before they had gone to bed, the men of Sodom surrounded the house, from young to old, and demanded that Lot bring out his guests so they could have relations with them.

Lot went outside and shut the door behind him. He begged the men not to do this wicked thing. Then he made an offer that exposes how broken the city had become. He offered his two virgin daughters to the crowd instead. The men rejected him, accused him of judging them though he had come to Sodom as a foreigner, and pressed hard against the door.

The angels pulled Lot back inside and struck the men outside with blindness, so they could not find the doorway. Then they told Lot to gather everyone who belonged to him and leave. The city was about to be destroyed.

Lot went to his sons-in-law and urged them to get up and leave, but they thought he was joking. At dawn the angels urged Lot to hurry. He hesitated. The angels seized his hand, his wife's hand, and the hands of his two daughters, because the LORD was merciful to him, and brought them outside the city. They told the family to flee for their lives, not to look back, and not to stop anywhere in the plain. Lot feared the mountains and asked to flee to a small town nearby. The town was called Zoar.

By the time Lot reached Zoar, the sun had risen. Then the LORD rained down brimstone and fire on Sodom and Gomorrah, out of the heavens. He overthrew those cities, the whole plain, all who lived in the cities, and what grew on the ground. Lot's wife looked back, and she became a pillar of salt.

Early the next morning Abraham went to the place where he had stood before the LORD. He looked down toward Sodom and Gomorrah and saw dense smoke rising from the land, like smoke from a furnace. God had remembered Abraham and sent Lot out of the destruction.

Lot left Zoar and lived in the mountains with his two daughters because he was afraid to stay in Zoar. In a cave, the older daughter said their father was old and there was no man in the land to give them children. On two nights they made Lot drink wine until he did not know what was happening, and each daughter slept with him. The older daughter bore Moab. The younger bore Ben-Ammi. Genesis gives their names as fathers of the Moabites and the Ammonites.

Later Abraham moved between Kadesh and Shur and lived in Gerar. There he repeated the old fear. He said Sarah was his sister. Abimelech, king of Gerar, sent for Sarah and took her. God came to Abimelech in a dream and warned him that he was as good as dead because Sarah was a married woman. Abimelech pleaded that he had acted with a clear conscience and clean hands because Abraham and Sarah both had said they were brother and sister.

God kept Abimelech from sinning against Him with Sarah and told him to return her to Abraham, who was a prophet and would pray for him. Early in the morning Abimelech told his officials the dream. They were afraid. He confronted Abraham for deceiving him and risking guilt on the kingdom. Abraham answered that he had feared there was no fear of God in that place and that he would be killed because of his wife. Sarah truly was his half-sister, and Abraham had asked her to show him kindness by calling him her brother wherever they went.

Abimelech restored Sarah, gave Abraham sheep, cattle, and servants, and gave Sarah a thousand pieces of silver. He told Abraham his land lay before him. Abraham prayed to God, and God healed Abimelech, his wife, and his female servants so they could bear children again, for the LORD had closed every womb in Abimelech's household because of Sarah.

Then the appointed time reached Sarah. The LORD was gracious to her as He had said, and Sarah conceived and bore Abraham a son in his old age, at the very time God had promised. Abraham named him Isaac. When Isaac was eight days old, Abraham circumcised him, as God had commanded. Abraham was one hundred years old when Isaac was born.

Sarah said God had brought her laughter, and everyone who heard would laugh with her. The child grew and was weaned. Abraham held a great feast on the day Isaac was weaned.

Sarah saw Ishmael, the son Hagar the Egyptian had borne to Abraham, mocking. She told Abraham to drive out the slave woman and her son, because Ishmael would not share Isaac's inheritance. The matter distressed Abraham greatly because it concerned his son. God told Abraham not to be distressed. He was to listen to Sarah, because through Isaac his offspring would be reckoned. God would also make Ishmael into a nation because he was Abraham's offspring.

Early the next morning Abraham gave Hagar bread and a skin of water, put them on her shoulder, and sent her away with the boy. She wandered in the wilderness of Beersheba. When the water was gone, she put the boy under a bush and sat down at a distance. She could not watch him die. As she wept, God heard the boy crying. The angel of God called to Hagar from heaven and told her not to be afraid. God opened her eyes, and she saw a well of water. She filled the skin and gave the boy a drink.

God was with the boy as he grew up. Ishmael lived in the wilderness and became an archer. His mother got a wife for him from Egypt.

At Beersheba, Abimelech and Phicol, the commander of his forces, made a treaty with Abraham. They recognized that God was with Abraham in everything he did. Abraham raised a complaint about a well his servants had seized. Abimelech said he had not known. Abraham gave sheep and cattle, and he set apart seven ewe lambs as a witness that he had dug the well. The place was called Beersheba, because there the two men swore an oath. Abraham planted a tamarisk tree there and called on the name of the LORD, the Eternal God.

Some time later God tested Abraham.

He told Abraham to take Isaac, his only son, whom he loved, and go to the land of Moriah. There Abraham was to offer Isaac as a burnt offering on one of the mountains God would name. Early the next morning Abraham saddled his donkey, split wood for the offering, took two servants and Isaac, and set out.

On the third day Abraham lifted his eyes and saw the place in the distance. He told the servants to stay with the donkey. He and Isaac would worship and then return. Abraham took the wood, laid it on Isaac, and carried the fire and the knife himself. As they walked together, Isaac asked about the lamb for the burnt offering. Abraham answered, "God Himself will provide the lamb for the burnt offering, my son." And the two walked on together.

When they reached the place God had told him about, Abraham built an altar, arranged the wood, bound Isaac, and laid him on the altar on top of the wood. Then Abraham reached out his hand and took the knife to slaughter his son.

The angel of the LORD called from heaven and told him to stop. "Do not lay a hand on the boy or do anything to him," said the angel, "for now I know that you fear God, since you have not withheld your only son from me."

Abraham looked up and saw a ram caught by its horns in a thicket. He took the ram and offered it as a burnt offering instead of his son. Abraham called that place The LORD Will Provide.

The angel called a second time and confirmed the promise because Abraham had obeyed. His descendants would be as many as the stars in the sky and the sand on the seashore. They would take possession of the gates of their enemies, and through Abraham's offspring all nations on earth would be blessed.

Abraham returned to his servants, and they went together to Beersheba. After this, Abraham received news from his brother Nahor's family in the north. Among the names was Bethuel, and Bethuel had become the father of Rebekah.
