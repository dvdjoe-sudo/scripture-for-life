Amos Episode 1: The Shepherd of Tekoa
Amos 1-2, Berean Standard Bible

Amos was not a court prophet and not a guild prophet. He was a sheepherder from Tekoa, a town in the hill country of Judah, south of Jerusalem, and the words that open his book are careful to place him exactly: what he saw came to him “in the days when Uzziah was king of Judah and Jeroboam son of Jehoash was king of Israel”, and the text adds a marker everyone in his generation could date without a calendar, “two years before the earthquake”. It is the same northern court the story has just come from. Jeroboam II's Israel was at its widest borders and its richest ease, the reign Jonah's prophecy had promised, and God sent this southern shepherd north into the middle of it with a message that began as a sound more than a sentence: “The LORD roars from Zion and raises His voice from Jerusalem; the pastures of the shepherds mourn, and the summit of Carmel withers.” A lion's roar, heard from Judah's capital, aimed at the north, and the first thing it withers is the shepherds' own pastures.

What Amos carried into Israel was a set of eight oracles built on a single frame, and the frame is the sermon. Each one opens with the same formula from the LORD: “For three transgressions of Damascus, even four, I will not revoke My judgment, because they threshed Gilead with sledges of iron.” The count is a Hebrew way of saying the measure is full and overfull, three sins and a fourth on top, too many to let pass. The charges against the nations are war crimes, and they are specific. Damascus had threshed Gilead, Israel's own land east of the Jordan, as a farmer threshes grain, except with sledges of iron on people. Fire is promised on the house of Hazael, exile for Aram. Then Gaza of the Philistines: “they exiled a whole population, delivering them up to Edom”, a whole people sold away in a single transaction, and fire on Gaza's walls, the remnant of the Philistines to perish. Then Tyre, the great trading partner to the north, charged with the same slave dealing, sharpened by a broken agreement: “they delivered up a whole congregation of exiles to Edom and broke a covenant of brotherhood”. Oracle by oracle the ring closes around Israel, and any Israelite listening would have heard these first ones gladly. They are judgments on Israel's enemies, pronounced by Israel's God, in Israel's hearing.

The ring keeps narrowing, and the charges keep getting closer to home. Edom is not a stranger nation but a brother, Esau's people, and the charge is a family's charge: “he pursued his brother with the sword and stifled all compassion”, an anger that never burned out, carried across generations. The Ammonites are charged with the worst single act in the list, stated in the text's own plain words: “they ripped open the pregnant women of Gilead in order to enlarge their territory”, murder as a land policy. Moab's crime is stranger and colder, a king's bones dug up and desecrated: “he burned to lime the bones of Edom’s king”. Then the formula turns toward God's own people, and Judah comes first, the shepherd's own south: “they reject the Law of the LORD and fail to keep His statutes”, and the promised fire falls on Jerusalem itself. By the time the seventh oracle ends, the pattern has done its work. The audience has nodded along through six judgments on foreigners and one on the sister kingdom. The eighth opening is identical, and it is the point of the whole book: “For three transgressions of Israel, even four, I will not revoke My judgment”.

Israel's charges are not war crimes. They are courtroom and marketplace crimes, the sins of a prosperous peace, and they are quoted here at the length the text gives them because no summary carries their weight: “they sell the righteous for silver and the needy for a pair of sandals”. “They trample on the heads of the poor as on the dust of the earth; they push the needy out of their way. A man and his father have relations with the same girl and so profane My holy name.” “They lie down beside every altar on garments taken in pledge. And in the house of their God, they drink wine obtained through fines.” Every item is a perversion of something given: the courts, where the righteous are sold a verdict at a time; the pledge laws, which existed to protect a poor man's cloak; worship itself, carried on in clothes seized from debtors, with wine bought by fines squeezed out of the weak. The nations were judged for what they did to Israel and to each other in war. Israel is judged for what it does to its own poor in peacetime, beside its own altars.

Against the charges God sets his own record, and the oracle turns into something like a prosecution's closing argument, gift answered by insult: “it was I who destroyed the Amorite before them”, the giants of the land cleared out like cedars felled and roots burned. “I brought you up from the land of Egypt and led you forty years in the wilderness”, that Israel might take possession of the Amorite's land. “I raised up prophets from your sons and Nazirites from your young men. Is this not true, O children of Israel?” And then the answer Israel gave, the whole indictment in one verse: “But you made the Nazirites drink wine and commanded the prophets not to prophesy.” They broke the Nazirites' vows with wine and gagged the prophets God had raised from their own sons. So the sentence falls, and its shape is futility: “Escape will fail the swift, the strong will not prevail by his strength, and the mighty will not save his life.” Runner, strongman, archer, horseman, all of them covered, until the last line: “Even the bravest of mighty men will flee naked on that day”. The eightfold ring was never mainly about the neighbors. It was a shepherd from Tekoa walking the formula around the map until it closed on the people applauding it, and the book has only just begun: ahead, the God who roared from Zion addresses Israel directly, and the next word is an invitation to listen, “Hear this word”.
