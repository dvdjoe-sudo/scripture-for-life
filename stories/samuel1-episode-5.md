1 Samuel Episode 5: Jonathan's Climb, and the Sentence on Saul
1 Samuel 14 to 15, Berean Standard Bible

While Saul sat under the pomegranate tree at Migron with his six hundred men and his priest, his son walked out of the camp and did not tell him. Jonathan crossed toward the Philistine outpost at the pass of Michmash with one armor-bearer, two men total, into a defile with cliffs on both sides, and his reasoning for it is the theological high point of the reign: "Come, let us cross over to the outpost of these uncircumcised men. Perhaps the LORD will work on our behalf. Nothing can hinder the LORD from saving, whether by many or by few." Perhaps. Nothing can hinder. Jonathan built no certainty into it, no claim on God, just an open door and a willingness to walk through it, and his armor-bearer answered with the loyalty the whole chapter runs on: "Do all that is in your heart. Go ahead; I am with you heart and soul." They agreed on a sign that put the outcome in the enemy's mouth. If the Philistines told them to wait, they would stay. If the Philistines called them up, that was the signal. The garrison, seeing two Hebrews crawl out of hiding, took the bait with a sneer: "Look, the Hebrews are coming out of the holes in which they were hiding!" And then: "Come on up, and we will teach you a lesson!" Jonathan climbed the cliff on his hands and feet with his armor-bearer behind him, and the Philistines fell before them, about twenty men in the first assault on a scrap of ground, and then the panic did what two swords could not. It struck the whole Philistine host, camp and field and raiding parties, and the earth itself quaked, and the text names its source without blinking: the panic was from God.

Saul's watchmen saw the enemy army melting away. The roll was called and the king learned that the victory he was watching belonged to his absent son. He began a priestly consultation and then, as the noise of the rout grew, cut it off mid-rite: "Withdraw your hand." The army charged into a Philistine force already cutting itself apart, the Hebrew deserters in the Philistine camps switched sides, the men who had hidden in the hills came down and joined the pursuit, and the chapter delivers its verdict in words that leave no room for Saul's later version: "So the LORD saved Israel that day". Not Saul. Not even Jonathan, finally. The LORD, through two men who had said perhaps.

Now watch the king metabolize a victory he did not make. Before the day was out, Saul had bound the army under an oath with a curse in it: no food until evening, he said, "before I have taken vengeance on my enemies". My enemies. The vengeance had become personal property. The army, driven faint through a pursuit on empty stomachs, came into a forest where honey lay on the ground, and no one dared touch it except the one man who had not heard the oath. Jonathan dipped the end of his staff in the honeycomb, ate, "and his eyes brightened", and when a soldier explained the curse, the prince gave his father the review the courtiers would not: "My father has brought trouble to the land," Jonathan replied. "Just look at how my eyes have brightened because I tasted a little of this honey. How much better it would have been if the troops had eaten freely today from the plunder they took from their enemies! Would not the slaughter of the Philistines have been much greater?" By nightfall the starving troops fell on the spoil and ate meat with the blood still in it, a sin Saul had to stop with an improvised altar and slaughtering stones, and when he pushed for a night pursuit and finally consulted God properly, heaven answered with silence. No word at all.

Saul's response to the silence was an investigation, and the investigation was run by lot, and the lot worked its way down, past the whole army, past the king's house, to Jonathan. The confession was clean and it was brave: "I only tasted a little honey with the end of the staff that was in my hand. And now I must die?" Saul, who had sworn the oath, now swore to enforce it on his own son: "May God punish me, and ever so severely, if you, Jonathan, do not surely die!" And here the book stages one of its great reversals of rank. The army, the same fainting, oath-bound army, stood between the king and the prince and overruled the king to his face, on theological grounds better than his: Jonathan had accomplished the great deliverance of the day, and "not a hair of his head will fall to the ground, for with God’s help he has accomplished this today". The people rescued Jonathan, and he did not die, and Saul accepted the overruling and went home. The victory chapter ends with the king's authority spent: his son alive by the army's verdict, his oath void, his pursuit abandoned.

The summary that follows says Saul fought successfully on every side for years, Moabites, Ammonites, Edomites, Zobah, Philistines, even Amalekites. Which is why the next mission matters so much: it came with instructions so explicit that partial obedience would have nowhere to hide. Samuel delivered them as a formal commission. Amalek was the ancient enemy, the nation that had fallen on Israel's stragglers on the way out of Egypt, and the sentence now pronounced reached back across the centuries: "Now go and attack the Amalekites and devote to destruction all that belongs to them. Do not spare them". The command went on to name the scope without exception, men and women, children and infants, oxen and sheep, camels and donkeys, the whole herem, the devotion to destruction that removed a thing from human use entirely. Saul mustered two hundred ten thousand men. He did one thing honorably at the outset: he warned the Kenites, who had shown kindness to Israel in the Exodus, to separate themselves from Amalek, and they did. Then he struck the Amalekites from Havilah to Shur, and the book records the failure with an accountant's eye: "Saul and his troops spared Agag, along with the best of the sheep and cattle, the fat calves and lambs, and the best of everything else. They were unwilling to devote them to destruction, but they devoted to destruction all that was despised and worthless." The king of Amalek lived, a trophy. The best livestock lived, plunder. What died under the ban was everything nobody wanted anyway.

That night the word of the LORD came to Samuel with a sentence this series lets stand in its own words: "I regret that I have made Saul king, for he has turned away from following Me and has not carried out My instructions." Samuel cried out to God all night, and went at dawn to confront a king who, he was told on the road, had stopped at Carmel to set up a monument to himself. Saul greeted the prophet with a blessing and a falsehood in the same breath: "May the LORD bless you. I have carried out the LORD’s instructions." And Samuel asked the question that has echoed down every century since, because the evidence was audible: "Then what is this bleating of sheep and lowing of cattle that I hear?" Saul blamed the troops. The troops had spared the best animals, he said, for sacrifice, to the LORD your God. Listen to the pronouns Across both of his explanations, it is never our God or my God. It is your God, said twice to the prophet, as though the covenant were Samuel's private affair. Samuel cut him off and recited the king's own biography back to him: "Although you were once small in your own eyes, have you not become the head of the tribes of Israel?" The LORD anointed you, sent you, told you exactly what to do. Why did you not obey? Why did you rush on the plunder? Saul insisted once more that he had obeyed, that the troops had taken the animals for sacrifice at Gilgal, and Samuel answered with the oracle that stands at the center of this book and near the center of the whole Bible's teaching on worship: "Does the LORD delight in burnt offerings and sacrifices as much as in obedience to His voice? Behold, obedience is better than sacrifice, and attentiveness is better than the fat of rams. For rebellion is like the sin of divination, and arrogance is like the wickedness of idolatry. Because you have rejected the word of the LORD, He has rejected you as king."

Then, at last, Saul confessed, and even the confession carried its diagnosis inside it: "I have sinned; I have transgressed the LORD’s commandment and your instructions, because I feared the people and obeyed their voice." Obedience was never his problem. He obeyed voices faithfully. The question was always which voice, the crowd's or God's, and on the two great tests of his reign he had chosen the crowd and called the choosing necessity. He begged Samuel to come back with him and worship, and Samuel refused, and turned to leave, and Saul grabbed at his robe and the hem tore, and Samuel read the tear as heaven's own handwriting: "The LORD has torn the kingdom of Israel from you today and has given it to your neighbor who is better than you." In the same conversation Samuel also said this, and the book leaves the two sayings standing side by side without a bridge: "Moreover, the Glory of Israel does not lie or change His mind, for He is not a man, that He should change His mind." God has regretted making Saul king, the chapter has already said twice, and God does not change His mind like a man. Samuel's point, in context, is a door closing: this sentence will not be negotiated, lobbied, or waited out. Saul's next words prove he heard only the public part of it. "I have sinned," Saul replied. "Please honor me now before the elders of my people and before Israel. Come back with me, so that I may worship the LORD your God." Honor me before the elders. Samuel, for the people's sake and the day's, went back with him, and Saul worshiped. Then Samuel finished the mission himself, there at Gilgal, calling for Agag and pronouncing the measure-for-measure sentence over the king whose sword had emptied Israelite homes: "As your sword has made women childless, so your mother will be childless among women." And Samuel went home to Ramah, and Saul went up to Gibeah, and they never met again in life. "And to the day of his death, Samuel never again visited Saul. Samuel mourned for Saul, and the LORD regretted that He had made Saul king over Israel."
