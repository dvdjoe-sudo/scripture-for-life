Amos Episode 4: The Plumb Line and the Priest
Amos 7-8, Berean Standard Bible

The last movement of the book changes its instrument. Up to now Amos has carried sermons; now the LORD shows him pictures, and the pictures are verdicts he is meant to watch being drawn. The first vision is locusts, prepared just after the king's harvest, as the late crop was coming up, the crop the people themselves depended on, and when the swarm had eaten every green plant in the land in the vision, the prophet does the one thing no earlier chapter showed him doing. He argues. “Lord GOD, please forgive! How will Jacob survive, since he is so small?” And the book states the result as simply as the vision: “So the LORD relented from this plan.” “It will not happen,” He said. A second vision follows, judgment by fire, a fire that consumed the great deep and devoured the land, and Amos argues again with the same words, urgency sharpened: “Lord GOD, please stop! How will Jacob survive, since he is so small?” “So the LORD relented from this plan.” “It will not happen either,” said the Lord GOD. The man sent to pronounce the end of Israel twice talked God out of the end of Israel, on the grounds of the nation's smallness. It is the only intercession of its kind in the book, and it works twice. Then comes the third picture, and no argument follows it.

The third vision is a tool from Amos's own world, a builder's instrument: the Lord standing by a wall true to plumb, a plumb line in His hand, the weighted cord a mason hangs to test whether a wall stands straight or leans toward falling. “Amos, what do you see?” asked the LORD. Amos answered that he saw a plumb line. “Behold,” said the Lord, “I am setting a plumb line among My people Israel; I will no longer spare them”. The wall had been measured, and a wall out of plumb is not repaired in Amos's message; it is condemned. Intercession has a limit in this book, and the limit is not Amos's willingness, which is never withdrawn. It is the measurement. The sentence that follows names the whole structure: the high places deserted, the sanctuaries laid waste, and against the reigning house, “I will rise up against the house of Jeroboam with My sword”. It is at this point, and not before, that the institution answers back.

Amaziah, priest of Bethel, the king's sanctuary, reports the sermon to Jeroboam as treason: “Amos has conspired against you in the midst of the house of Israel. The land cannot bear all his words”. Then the priest turns on the prophet and delivers the establishment's whole case in three sentences: “Go away, you seer! Flee to the land of Judah; earn your bread there and do your prophesying there.” “But never prophesy at Bethel again, because it is the sanctuary of the king and the temple of the kingdom.” Amos is treated as a professional who has taken his trade to the wrong market: prophesy for bread if you must, but not here, not at the king's chapel. The answer Amos gives is the only autobiography in the book, and the series has waited four episodes for it: “I was not a prophet,” Amos replied, “nor was I the son of a prophet; rather, I was a herdsman and a tender of sycamore-fig trees.” No guild, no training, no father's office, no bread earned by prophesying. “But the LORD took me from following the flock and said to me”, and the command he quotes is the book's whole origin in one line: “Go, prophesy to My people Israel.” Amaziah had said do not preach against the house of Isaac; the word of the LORD answers the priest personally, and the answer is the harshest oracle in the book aimed at one man: “Your wife will become a prostitute in the city, and your sons and daughters will fall by the sword.” His land measured out and divided, himself dead on pagan soil, and the refrain under it all unchanged: “And Israel will surely go into exile, away from their homeland.” The confrontation at Bethel is the book's one prose scene, and it is a hearing: the king's priest cross-examines the shepherd, and the shepherd's only credential is that he was taken.

The fourth vision is the quietest and the worst: a basket of summer fruit, the harvest's end in a container. “Amos, what do you see?” He asked. The answer is the fruit. “The end has come for My people Israel; I will no longer spare them.” In the Hebrew the word for summer fruit and the word for end sound nearly the same, a pun no harvest could outrun: the fruit is ripe, and ripeness is the verdict. “the songs of the temple will turn to wailing. Many will be the corpses, strewn in silence everywhere!” The chapter then turns from the vision to the men the whole book has been watching, and lets them indict themselves out of their own mouths at their own counters: “When will the New Moon be over, that we may sell grain? When will the Sabbath end, that we may market wheat?” Religion was to them an interruption of business, and the business was rigged: “Let us reduce the ephah and increase the shekel; let us cheat with dishonest scales.” “Let us buy the poor with silver and the needy for a pair of sandals, selling even the chaff with the wheat!” Short measures, inflated prices, crooked scales, sweepings sold as grain, and human beings bought for the price of footwear, the same charge from the book's second chapter, now heard in the merchants' own voices. The oath that answers it is the chapter's coldest line: “The LORD has sworn by the Pride of Jacob: “I will never forget any of their deeds.”

The episode closes with the strangest sentence in the book, a judgment described as a famine that is not one. The sun forced down at noon, feasts turned to mourning, every head shaved, “like a time of mourning for an only son”, and then: “Behold, the days are coming, declares the Lord GOD, when I will send a famine on the land”, and the definition splits the sentence open: “not a famine of bread or a thirst for water, but a famine of hearing the words of the LORD”. “People will stagger from sea to sea and roam from north to east, seeking the word of the LORD, but they will not find it.” The nation that commanded its prophets not to prophesy will one day hunt the whole land for a word from God and find silence. It is the Bethel scene inverted and enlarged: they told the shepherd to take his words back to Judah, and the last famine of the book is a country searching for the voice it sent away. The young women and young men faint from a thirst no water answers, and those who swore by the guilt of Samaria and the god of Dan fall, in the chapter's final phrase, “never to rise again”, the same words the funeral lament used of Virgin Israel, now spoken of the worshipers who made her what she was.
