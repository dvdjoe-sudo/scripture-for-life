Numbers Episode 4: The Spies and the Sentence
Numbers 13 to 15, Berean Standard Bible

From Paran, the wilderness camp could nearly see the end of the story. The land God had promised Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob lay just north of them. Before the army moved, God told Moses to send men in to look at it.

"Send out for yourself men to spy out the land of Canaan, which I am giving to the Israelites. From each of their fathers' tribes send one man who is a leader among them."

Twelve leaders, one from each tribe. The text names all twelve, and two of the names matter for the rest of the Bible. From the tribe of Judah, Caleb son of Jephunneh. From the tribe of Ephraim, Hoshea son of Nun, and the chapter pauses on him for one detail: Moses gave Hoshea the name Joshua. The attendant who had spoken out at Eldad and Medad has a new name now, given before the mission, and the mission would make it famous.

Moses' instructions were a surveyor's questions and a soldier's. See what the land is like, and whether the people living there are strong or weak, few or many. Whether the land is good or bad, whether the cities are camps or fortresses, whether the soil is fertile or unproductive, whether there are trees. And then, almost as an afterthought that turned out to be the point: be courageous and bring back some of the fruit of the land. It was the season for the first ripe grapes.

They went up through the Negev and into the hill country, as far north as Rehob toward Lebo Hamath, and down to Hebron, where the descendants of Anak lived, Ahiman, Sheshai, and Talmai. Hebron, the text notes in passing, had been built seven years before Zoan in Egypt. They came to the valley of Eshcol, and there they cut down a branch with a single cluster of grapes so large that they carried it on a pole between two of them. They took pomegranates and figs as well. The valley got its name from that cluster: Eshcol means cluster.

At the end of forty days, the twelve came back to Moses, Aaron, and the whole congregation in the wilderness of Paran at Kadesh, carrying the fruit and a report that begins like a promise kept.

"We went into the land to which you sent us, and indeed, it is flowing with milk and honey. Here is some of its fruit!"

Every word of that was true, and it was all the encouragement the report contained.

"Nevertheless, the people living in the land are strong, and the cities are large and fortified. We even saw the descendants of Anak there."

They listed the nations like a roll call of reasons: Amalekites in the Negev, Hittites, Jebusites, and Amorites in the hill country, Canaanites by the sea and along the Jordan. Caleb quieted the people before Moses and made the counter-case in one breath.

"We must go up and take possession of the land, for we can certainly conquer it!"

But the men who had gone up with him answered the opposite, and then the report changed character. It stopped being a survey and became a warning about the land itself.

"We cannot go up against the people, for they are stronger than we are!"

They gave the Israelites a bad report about the land they had spied out. The land devours its inhabitants, they said, and all the people we saw there are of great size.

"We even saw the Nephilim there," they said, giants they called "the descendants of Anak that come from the Nephilim!" And then the sentence the report is remembered by: "We seemed like grasshoppers in our own sight, and we must have seemed the same to them!"

Grasshoppers in our own sight. The sentence admits what the whole report was built on: the ten had measured the land by how small it made them feel, and brought the feeling back as if it were a fact about Canaan. The cluster of grapes on the pole said the land was good. The grasshopper sentence said the spies had already surrendered in it.

That night, the whole congregation lifted up their voices and cried out, and the people wept. All the Israelites grumbled against Moses and Aaron.

"If only we had died in the land of Egypt, or if only we had died in this wilderness! Why is the LORD bringing us into this land to fall by the sword? Our wives and children will become plunder. Would it not be better for us to go back to Egypt?"

And then the grumbling found its program, the most practical sentence of rebellion in the book.

"Let us appoint a leader and return to Egypt."

Moses and Aaron fell facedown before the whole assembly. Joshua son of Nun and Caleb son of Jephunneh, who were among those who had spied out the land, tore their clothes and made the last appeal anyone would make to this generation.

"The land we passed through and explored is an exceedingly good land. If the LORD delights in us, He will bring us into this land, a land flowing with milk and honey, and He will give it to us."

"Only do not rebel against the LORD, and do not be afraid of the people of the land, for they will be like bread for us. Their protection has been removed, and the LORD is with us. Do not be afraid of them!"

The congregation's answer was to talk of stoning Joshua and Caleb. At that moment the glory of the LORD appeared to all the Israelites at the Tent of Meeting, and God spoke of ending the story altogether. How long will this people treat Me with contempt? How long will they refuse to believe in Me, despite all the signs I have performed among them? I will strike them with a plague and destroy them, and make you into a nation greater and mightier than they.

It was the golden calf offer again, nearly word for word: let Me start over with you, Moses. And Moses gave the same answer, expanded. The Egyptians will hear of it, and tell the Canaanites, who have already heard that God is among this people, seen face to face, His cloud standing over them, going before them in a pillar by day and fire by night. If You kill this people as one man, the nations will say the LORD was not able to bring them into the land He swore to give them, so He slaughtered them in the wilderness. Then Moses quoted God's own words from the mountain back to Him, the name God had proclaimed when Moses' face was still hidden in the rock.

"The LORD is slow to anger and abounding in loving devotion, forgiving iniquity and transgression. Yet He will by no means leave the guilty unpunished; He will visit the iniquity of the fathers upon their children to the third and fourth generation."

"Pardon, I pray, the iniquity of this people, in keeping with the greatness of Your loving devotion, just as You have forgiven them ever since they left Egypt."

"I have pardoned them as you requested," the LORD replied.

And in the next sentence the pardon and the sentence arrive together, which is the shape of this whole book. As surely as I live, and as surely as the whole earth is filled with the glory of the LORD, none of the men who have seen My glory and the signs I performed in Egypt and in the wilderness, and who have tested Me these ten times and not obeyed Me, will ever see the land.

"Your bodies will fall in this wilderness"

All who were numbered in the census, everyone twenty years of age or older, would fall there, because they had grumbled against Him.

"Surely none of you will enter the land in which I swore to settle you, except Caleb son of Jephunneh and Joshua son of Nun."

Their children, the little ones the congregation had said would become plunder, those children God would bring in, and they would know the land their fathers rejected. As for the fathers: in keeping with the forty days they had spied out the land, they would bear their guilt forty years, a year for each day, wandering until the last of the counted generation had fallen. The ten spies who brought the bad report were struck down by a plague before the LORD, there in front of the camp they had panicked. Of the twelve who carried the grapes, only Joshua and Caleb remained alive.

Moses told the people these words, and they mourned bitterly. And early the next morning they got up and went up toward the ridge of the hill country, confessing as they climbed.

"We have indeed sinned," they said, "but we will go to the place the LORD has promised."

It was obedience, one day late, aimed at a door God had already closed. Moses warned them not to go, because the LORD was not among them, and they would be struck down. They went anyway, daring to climb, while neither Moses nor the ark of the covenant moved from the camp. The Amalekites and Canaanites who lived in that hill country came down, attacked them, and routed them all the way to Hormah. The generation that would not enter the land when God was with them tried to enter it when He was not, and got the war the ten spies had feared, without the God who would have won it.

Chapter 15 reads quietly after that, and its quiet is the point. God gave laws for offerings in the land the people would enter, grain and drink offerings measured against the animal, the same proportions for the burnt offering, the peace offering, and the sin offering for an unintentional sin, with one law for the native born and for the foreigner who lived among them. He gave the law of the first of the dough, offered as a contribution. He gave the warning that the person who sins defiantly, native or foreigner, blasphemes the LORD and is to be cut off, because he has despised the word of the LORD. These are instructions for a future the speakers would not live in, given to a camp under a sentence of death by wandering. God went on legislating for the land anyway. The promise had been delayed for the fathers. It had not been withdrawn from the children.

The chapter's one story is small and terrible. While the Israelites were in the wilderness, a man was found gathering wood on the Sabbath day. The men who found him brought him to Moses and Aaron and the whole congregation, and they placed him in custody, because it had not been made clear what should be done to him. The word came back.

"The man must surely be put to death. The whole congregation is to stone him outside the camp."

So the whole congregation took him outside the camp and stoned him to death, as the LORD had commanded Moses. In a book about a generation dying slowly, one man died quickly, for kindling, on the day of rest, before the wandering had properly begun.

And the chapter closes with a piece of clothing. God told Moses to have the Israelites make tassels for the corners of their garments throughout their generations, with a blue cord on each tassel.

"These will serve as tassels for you to look at, so that you may remember all the commandments of the LORD, that you may obey them and not prostitute yourselves by following your own heart and your own eyes."

"I am the LORD your God who brought you out of the land of Egypt to be your God. I am the LORD your God."

The verdict of the spies had come from following their own eyes: grasshoppers, in their own sight. The tassels were the counterweight, tied where a man could see them every day of the forty years, a reminder sewn onto the sentence itself.

The camp turned away from the border. It had thirty-eight years of wilderness in front of it, and almost none of what happened in them would be written down.
