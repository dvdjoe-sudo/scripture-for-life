2 Samuel Episode 1: The Lament for Saul and Jonathan
2 Samuel 1, Berean Standard Bible

David came home to a burned town. He had wept until he had no strength left to weep, then inquired of God, then ridden south and taken everything back from the Amalekites who raided Ziklag, down to the last wife and the last spoon. Now he was back in the ashes, rebuilding, and he had been there two days when the news he had been outrunning for years finally caught up with him. On the third day a man arrived from Saul's camp with his clothes torn and dust on his head, the uniform of a bearer of disaster, and he fell to the ground before David and did homage. David asked where he had come from, and the man said he had escaped from the Israelite camp. David asked the only question that mattered: what was the outcome? The answer came in three strokes, each one worse. The troops had fled from the battle. Many of them had fallen and died. And Saul and his son Jonathan were also dead.

David did not take a stranger's word for the death of a king. He asked the young man how he knew, and the young man told a story built to please him. He said he had happened to be on Mount Gilboa, and there was Saul, leaning on his spear, with the chariots and the cavalry closing in on him. Saul had turned and seen him and called out, and asked who he was, and he had answered that he was an Amalekite. Then Saul had begged him: "Stand over me and kill me, for agony has seized me, but my life still lingers." So he had stood over the king and killed him, he said, because he knew that after he had fallen he could not survive. And he had taken the crown from Saul's head and the band from his arm, and here they were, brought to his lord. Any reader coming straight from the last book holds a second version of that death in the same hands: there, Saul fell on his own sword after his armor-bearer refused him, and no Amalekite stood over him. This book does not stop to reconcile the two accounts. It lets the man's story stand as the story he told David, and everything that follows in the chapter turns on what he said, and on the crown in his hands.

What the messenger expected was a reward. What he got was grief, total and immediate. David took hold of his own clothes and tore them, and every man with him did the same, and they mourned and wept and fasted until evening. Note the list of the dead they grieved: for Saul, and for his son Jonathan, and for the people of the LORD and the house of Israel, because they had fallen by the sword. The king who had hunted David for years, who had thrown spears at him and chased him through two countries, was mourned by David's whole camp until dark, without qualification. Only then, after the fasting, did David turn back to the young man and ask where he was from. The answer sealed his fate: he was the son of a foreigner, an Amalekite. David asked him one question: "Why were you not afraid to lift your hand to destroy the LORD's anointed?" The man's own story was the whole case against him. He had come expecting that David hated Saul, and he had built his tale on that arithmetic, perhaps shaping it as he went. But David's arithmetic about the anointed king had never changed, not in the cave at En-gedi, not in the camp at Hachilah, and it did not change now that the king was dead and the crown was in the room. David summoned one of his young men and ordered the execution, and the verdict he pronounced was drawn from the man's own mouth: "Your blood be on your own head because your own mouth has testified against you". He had condemned himself with his own boast: "I killed the LORD's anointed."

Then David did the thing that has carried his name down the centuries as surely as the giant did. He took up a lament for Saul and Jonathan, and he ordered that the sons of Judah be taught it, the Song of the Bow, written, the text says, in the Book of Jashar, the same lost book that once preserved Joshua's long day. It is a song with no gloating in it and no mention of God settling any scores for David. It opens in ruin: "Your glory, O Israel, lies slain on your heights. How the mighty have fallen!" Its first instinct is protection of the dead king's honor, that the news be kept from Israel's enemies: "Tell it not in Gath; proclaim it not in the streets of Ashkelon, lest the daughters of the Philistines rejoice". It curses the ground where the king died, the mountains of Gilboa, calling down a sky with no dew or rain on them, because there the shield of the mighty was defiled. And then it does something only David in that generation would have done: it praises Saul at full strength, alongside Jonathan, as a pair bound in life and in death. "Saul and Jonathan, beloved and delightful in life, were not divided in death. They were swifter than eagles; they were stronger than lions." It thanks Saul for Israel's daughters' scarlet and gold, the prosperity of his reign remembered by the very man that reign spent its last years hunting.

The lament narrows at the end to one name. "How the mighty have fallen in the thick of battle! Jonathan lies slain on your heights." Then the most famous sentence David ever spoke about another person: "I grieve for you, Jonathan, my brother. You were delightful to me; your love to me was extraordinary, surpassing the love of women." The prince who had handed David his robe and his weapons, who had sworn him a covenant and accepted second place in a kingdom he would never see, was dead on the same hill as his father, and David's song gives the last word neither to kingship nor to victory but to the refrain that has run under the whole chapter like a drumbeat, spoken a third and final time: "How the mighty have fallen, and the weapons of war have perished!" The crown was in David's hands now. The throne stood empty. And the first public act of the man who would take it was a song for the two men whose deaths had emptied it, taught to all Judah so that no one would be allowed to remember that day cheaply.
