Numbers Episode 3: Complaints, Quail, and Miriam
Numbers 11 and 12, Berean Standard Bible

Three days into the march, the complaints started, and they started at the edges.

The people began to complain about their hardship in the hearing of the LORD. When He heard them, His anger was kindled, and fire from the LORD blazed among them and consumed some of the outskirts of the camp. The people cried out to Moses. Moses prayed to the LORD, and the fire died down. The place was named Taberah, because the fire of the LORD had burned among them.

It was the first round of a cycle this book will run again and again: a complaint, a judgment, Moses praying for the very people who provoked it, and mercy. Taberah was only the warm-up. What came next had a menu attached.

The rabble among the people had a strong craving for other food, and the craving spread until the Israelites themselves were weeping, family after family at the entrances to their tents. Their complaint is quoted, and it is worth hearing in their own words, because it is a complaint about Egypt made by people standing three days out from the mountain of God.

"Who will feed us meat? We remember the fish we ate freely in Egypt, along with the cucumbers, melons, leeks, onions, and garlic. But now our appetite is gone; there is nothing to see but this manna!"

Fish, freely, in Egypt. The slavery that Genesis and Exodus spent two books escaping had been recut in memory as a market with good produce. The manna they despised is described right after, almost in their defense and almost not: it was like coriander seed, the color of a pale resin. The people went out and gathered it, ground it in a hand mill or crushed it in a mortar, boiled it in a pot and made it into cakes, and it tasted like pastry made with oil. When the dew settled on the camp at night, the manna came down with it. It had fed them every day since the wilderness of Sin, without a field, a seed, or a market anywhere in reach. And a whole camp was standing at their tent doors crying over it, because it was not meat, and it was not Egypt.

The anger of the LORD was kindled greatly. And this time Moses broke too.

The burden speech Moses makes in chapter 11 is the most unguarded thing he says in the Bible so far, and it is aimed at God. Why have You brought this trouble on Your servant? Why have I not found favor in Your sight, that You have laid the burden of all these people on me?

"Did I conceive all these people? Did I give them birth, so that You should tell me, 'Carry them in your bosom, as a nurse carries an infant,' to the land that You swore to give their fathers?"

Where can I get meat for all these people, when they keep crying to me, give us meat to eat? I cannot carry all these people by myself. It is too burdensome for me.

"If this is how You are going to treat me, please kill me right now"

If he had found favor in God's eyes, he said, let God do that rather than let him see his own wretchedness.

The man who had argued God down from destroying Israel at the golden calf now asked to die rather than lead them another mile. God's answer was not rebuke. It was help, and meat, in that order.

Seventy elders of Israel, men Moses knew as leaders and officers of the people, were to be brought to the Tent of Meeting. God would come down and speak there, and take some of the Spirit that was on Moses and put that Spirit on them, and they would help him bear the burden of the people, so he did not have to bear it by himself. God's solution to Moses carrying too much was not a lighter people. It was more shoulders, with Moses' own Spirit spread across them.

And the people were to consecrate themselves for tomorrow, because they would eat meat, not for a day or two or five or ten or twenty days, but for a whole month, until it came out of their nostrils and they loathed it, because they had rejected the LORD who was among them and had wept before Him asking why they ever left Egypt. Moses, standing in front of that promise, did the arithmetic out loud. Here I am among 600,000 men on foot, and You say they will eat meat for a month? If all the flocks and herds were slaughtered, would it be enough? If all the fish of the sea were caught, would it be enough?

The LORD answered Moses: "Is the LORD's arm too short? Now you will see whether or not My word will come to pass."

Moses went out and spoke the LORD's words to the people, and he gathered the seventy elders and had them stand around the tent. The LORD came down in the cloud and spoke with him there, and He took some of the Spirit that was on Moses and placed that Spirit on the seventy elders. As the Spirit rested on them, they prophesied, though they never did so again. Two men on the list, Eldad and Medad, had remained in the camp instead of going out to the tent, and the Spirit rested on them there, and they prophesied in the camp. A young man ran and told Moses. Joshua son of Nun, Moses' attendant since his youth, spoke up.

"Moses, my lord, stop them!"

But Moses replied: "Are you jealous on my account? I wish that all the LORD's people were prophets and that the LORD would place His Spirit on them!"

It is the first time Joshua speaks in this book, and he speaks to defend Moses' honor against Moses' own wishes. The man who will one day lead the whole nation starts here, protective of a leader who does not want protecting. Moses, who had just asked God to kill him, answered his young attendant with the most generous sentence in the chapter.

Then the wind came. A wind sent by the LORD drove in quail from the sea and brought them down around the camp, about two cubits above the ground, for a day's journey in every direction. All that day, and all that night, and all the next day, the people stayed up gathering them. No one gathered less than ten homers. They spread them out all around the camp, meat as far as the eye could see, Egypt delivered in feathers.

"But while the meat was still between their teeth, before it was chewed, the anger of the LORD burned against the people, and the LORD struck them with a severe plague."

The place was called Kibroth-hattaavah, because there they buried the people who had craved other food. The meat had been promised for a month. The graves were dug on the first day of it.

Notice what God had and had not done in the whole affair. He had not refused the meat. He had promised it for a month, until it came out of their nostrils and they loathed it, and the quail came on the wind exactly as He said, farther than a man could walk in a day, in every direction. The craving was answered in full, and the answering was the judgment. He had not taken the burden off Moses either, by removing the people or by removing Moses, though Moses had asked for the second one outright. He had spread Moses' own Spirit across seventy men and told Moses his arm was not too short. Both halves of the camp, the leader who wanted to die and the people who wanted Egypt's food, got what they asked for, and both halves buried something at Kibroth-hattaavah.

From Kibroth-hattaavah the people traveled to Hazeroth, and there the complaint moved inside Moses' own family.

Miriam and Aaron criticized Moses because of the Cushite woman he had married. But the charge they spoke out loud was not really about the marriage, and the text quotes it plainly.

"Does the LORD speak only through Moses?" they said. "Does He not also speak through us?" And the LORD heard this.

Miriam was the prophetess who had led the women with tambourines at the sea. Aaron was the high priest whose robes had been sprinkled with blood and oil in front of the whole nation. Both of them had truly spoken for God, which is what made the claim dangerous. It was not false. It was aimed. And between their charge and God's answer, the narrator sets one sentence down about the man they were attacking, and it is the sentence this chapter is remembered by.

"Now Moses was a very humble man, more so than any man on the face of the earth."

Suddenly the LORD said to Moses, Aaron, and Miriam: come out, the three of you, to the Tent of Meeting. They came out. The LORD descended in a pillar of cloud and stood at the entrance to the tent and called Aaron and Miriam forward, and He drew the line they had blurred, in words that explain every dream and vision in the Bible around them.

"Hear now My words: If there is a prophet among you, I, the LORD, will reveal Myself to him in a vision; I will speak to him in a dream. But this is not so with My servant Moses; he is faithful in all My house."

"I speak with him face to face, clearly and not in riddles; he sees the form of the LORD. Why then were you unafraid to speak against My servant Moses?"

The anger of the LORD burned against them, and He departed. As the cloud lifted from above the tent, Miriam stood there covered in a skin disease, white as snow. Aaron turned toward her and saw it, and the high priest who a moment before had claimed a prophet's standing now pleaded like a brother. Do not hold this sin against us, he begged Moses, the sin we have committed in our foolishness. Do not let her be like a stillborn infant, its flesh half eaten away when it comes out of its mother's womb.

So Moses cried out to the LORD: "O God, please heal her!"

Five words in the English, and no hesitation in them. The man attacked an hour before prayed for his attacker on the spot, the way he had prayed at Taberah for strangers. God's answer was mercy with a sentence inside it. If her father had spit in her face, would she not be in disgrace for seven days? Let her be confined outside the camp for seven days. After that she may be brought back in.

So Miriam was shut outside the camp for seven days, in the place where the unclean waited, under the laws her own brother had received. And the people did not move on until she was brought in again. Six hundred thousand men, an army under four standards with an ark waiting to travel, sat in their tents for a week because the march would not leave Miriam behind.

After that, the people set out from Hazeroth and camped in the wilderness of Paran, at the edge of the land they had left Egypt to enter. The spies were about to go in.
