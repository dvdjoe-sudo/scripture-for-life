1 Kings Episode 5: The Queen of Sheba and the Turn
1 Kings 9-11, Berean Standard Bible

After the dedication, God appeared to Solomon a second time, as He had at Gibeon, and the appearance was an answer and a warning in one speech. The prayer had been heard. The temple was consecrated, God's Name placed there forever: “My eyes and My heart will be there for all time.” The throne could run forever, on the old condition: “then I will establish your royal throne over Israel forever”. And then the other branch of the sentence, spoken while the temple was new and the feast barely over: “then I will cut off Israel from the land that I have given them, and I will banish from My presence this temple I have sanctified for My Name”. God Himself described the temple as a future ruin, and strangers passing the rubble asking why the LORD had done such a thing to this land, and the answer being that Israel had forsaken the God who brought them out of Egypt. None of it was fate. It was a fork, laid out in advance, and the king who had prayed the great prayer heard both roads named before he had taken a step down either. The chapter then drops back to business, and the business has an edge. At the end of the twenty years of building, Solomon gave Hiram twenty towns in Galilee as payment for the timber and the gold, and Hiram came down from Tyre to inspect them and was not pleased. “What are these towns you have given me, my brother?” He named the whole district Cabul, worthless, and the name stuck to this day, the text says, a small sour monument inside the great alliance. Hiram had sent a hundred and twenty talents of gold.

The building went on regardless: the terraces, the wall of Jerusalem, Hazor and Megiddo and Gezer, which Pharaoh had burned and given as a dowry with his daughter, and Solomon rebuilt. The forced labor that powered it all is accounted for in this chapter with a distinction the text is careful to draw. The peoples Israel had never conquered, Amorites, Hittites, Perizzites, Hivites, Jebusites, their descendants remained in the land, and “Solomon conscripted these people to be forced laborers, as they are to this day”. Israelites were not made slaves, the text says; they were the soldiers and officers and commanders. A fleet was built at Ezion-geber on the Red Sea, crewed with Hiram's sailors who knew the sea, and it came back from Ophir with four hundred and twenty talents of gold. Then, from farther away than Tyre or Ophir, came the most famous visitor of the reign. The queen of Sheba had heard of Solomon's fame concerning the name of the LORD, and she “came to test him with difficult questions”, arriving with a very large caravan, camels loaded with spices and gold and precious stones. She spoke to him all that was on her mind, and the test ended in a rout: “nothing was too difficult for the king to explain”. But it was not the answers that finished her. It was the sight of the whole establishment, the palace, the food at his table, the seating of his servants, the attendants in their attire, the cupbearers, the burnt offerings going up at the house of the LORD. The text says “it took her breath away”. Her verdict was that the reports had undersold it: “Indeed, not even half was told to me. Your wisdom and prosperity have far exceeded the report I heard.” And a pagan queen blessed Israel's God for Israel's king in words any Israelite would have been proud to say: “Blessed be the LORD your God, who has delighted in you to set you on the throne of Israel.” She left him a hundred and twenty talents of gold and a flood of spices. “Never again were spices in such abundance brought in as those the queen of Sheba gave to King Solomon.” Solomon answered gift for gift out of his royal bounty, and she went home.

Now the book starts counting, and the counting is the commentary. The gold that came to Solomon in one year weighed six hundred and sixty-six talents, besides the merchants' revenue and the tribute of kings. He made two hundred large shields of hammered gold and three hundred small ones and hung them in the House of the Forest of Lebanon. He made a throne of ivory overlaid with pure gold, six steps up to it, a lion standing beside each armrest and twelve lions on the steps, and the text says nothing like it had ever been made for any kingdom. Every drinking cup in the palace was gold; silver was beneath notice: “There was no silver, because it was accounted as nothing in the days of Solomon.” Ships of Tarshish came in once every three years bearing gold and silver, ivory, apes, and peacocks. “The king made silver as common in Jerusalem as stones, and cedar as abundant as sycamore in the foothills.” And Solomon accumulated fourteen hundred chariots and twelve thousand horses, stationing them in chariot cities, and the horses were imported from Egypt and Kue, bought by royal merchants and resold to the Hittite and Aramean kings. It is all reported in the same flat ledger voice as the temple furnishings. But a reader of the whole story carries one older sentence alongside the horse trade, from the law of the king in Deuteronomy, which had said that a king of Israel must not multiply horses for himself, nor send the people back to Egypt to get more. The book does not quote it. It just writes down the horses.

Then comes the chapter the whole book has been bending toward, and it opens with the word however. “King Solomon, however, loved many foreign women along with the daughter of Pharaoh”, women of Moab and Ammon and Edom and Sidon and the Hittites, the nations God had named when He gave the warning the book now quotes outright: “You must not intermarry with them, for surely they will turn your hearts after their gods.” The warning's fulfillment follows in the same breath: “Yet Solomon clung to these women in love.” Seven hundred wives of royal birth and three hundred concubines, and “his wives turned his heart away”. When Solomon was old, they turned his heart after other gods, “and he was not wholeheartedly devoted to the LORD his God, as his father David had been”. He followed Ashtoreth of the Sidonians and Milcom of the Ammonites, and on a hill east of Jerusalem, in sight of the temple mount, Solomon built a high place for Chemosh of Moab and for Molech of the Ammonites, and did the same for all his foreign wives, who burned incense and sacrificed to their gods there. The except that the narrator planted in chapter 3, when the young king loved the LORD and still sacrificed on the high places, had grown into this. God had appeared to Solomon twice, and had warned him explicitly, and the text says the LORD grew angry, and the sentence came in the terms God had laid down in chapter 9: “I will tear the kingdom away from you and give it to your servant.” For David's sake it would not happen in Solomon's lifetime: “I will tear it out of the hand of your son.” And it would not be total: “I will give one tribe to your son for the sake of My servant David and for the sake of Jerusalem, which I have chosen.”

The tearing began at the borders before it reached the throne. God raised up adversaries, the text's own word, and each one was a debt from David's wars coming due. Hadad the Edomite had been a small boy when Joab spent six months in Edom killing every male in the country, and the boy had escaped to Egypt and been raised in Pharaoh's palace and married into the royal house. When David and Joab were dead he asked Pharaoh to let him go home, and Pharaoh, astonished, asked what he had lacked in Egypt. Nothing, Hadad said, but let me go. Rezon, who had fled David's destruction of Zobah and made himself a raider captain, seized Damascus and ruled Aram with hostility toward Israel all Solomon's days. And the third adversary was not foreign at all. Jeroboam son of Nebat was an Ephraimite, a mighty man of valor, industrious, and Solomon himself had spotted the young man's ability and put him in charge of the whole labor force of the house of Joseph. One day outside Jerusalem the prophet Ahijah the Shilonite met him on the road. Ahijah had wrapped himself in a new cloak, and the two of them were alone in the open field, and the prophet took hold of his own new cloak and tore it into twelve pieces. “Take ten pieces for yourself”, he told Jeroboam, and gave him God's charter with the gift: “Behold, I will tear the kingdom out of the hand of Solomon, and I will give you ten tribes.” One tribe would stay with David's house, so that David would always have a lamp before God in Jerusalem. And to Jeroboam came the same offer David's line had lived on, the same condition attached: “If you listen to all that I command you, walk in My ways, and do what is right in My sight in order to keep My statutes and commandments as My servant David did, then I will be with you. I will build you a lasting dynasty just as I built for David, and I will give Israel to you.” Solomon heard of it somehow, and sought to kill Jeroboam, and Jeroboam fled where Israel's strays had always fled, to Egypt, to a new Pharaoh named Shishak, and stayed there until Solomon died. The reign that had begun with a shivering old king and a contested throne ended at forty years, and Solomon rested with his fathers in the City of David, and Rehoboam his son reigned in his place, walking north to Shechem to be made king by a nation already holding a grievance and a prophet's torn cloak in its memory.
